# C18 — Apoha

## #0

Ask what lets one word, *cow*, gather every cow, when the animal in front of us differs in size, colour and countless other respects from the one we met yesterday and the universal the word seems to name is no separately existing thing shared among its instances. Dignāga, in the sixth century, answered by examining what the act of conceptual identification rules out. To apprehend something as a cow is to exclude what is other than a cow, so *cow* is articulated as *not non-cow*. *Non-cow* opens the field from which the term distinguishes its application, and excluding that otherness lets differing particulars be understood together, with the differences that were set aside doing the gathering.

This is **apoha**, exclusion, and it enters the essay as constitutive exclusion: a determination becomes this through its relation to what the mark does not apply to. The achieved term carries a field of non-application, and forgetting that field makes the positive mark appear self-sufficient and conceals how it became determinate.

[Constitutive exclusion](../A08-Apoha-Constitutive-Exclusion.md) **defines** how a term's negative constitution stays active within its positive reach. The Buddhist semantic account explains how a word applies, and Taylor's further sourceward movement asks how the achieved determination can remain answerable to that constituting relation. Absence, repression and rejection have their own objects and operations, and semantic non-application cannot replace them.
