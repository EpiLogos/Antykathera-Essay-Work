# Russell — Portraits from Memory and Other Essays (1956)

## Bibliographic identity

Bertrand Russell, *Portraits from Memory and Other Essays* (New York: Simon and Schuster, 1956); the British edition is London: George Allen and Unwin, 1956. The consulted object is the Internet Archive lending scan of the Simon and Schuster printing (item `portraitsfrommem011249mbp`; title page “PORTRAITS FROM MEMORY and Other Essays by Bertrand Russell, Simon and Schuster, New York 1956”). Its pagination differs from the Allen and Unwin edition (the order of the essays differs, and the essay begins on p. 54 here). The Routledge Classics reprint (2021) has not been read; the earlier working note's “pp. 45–49” for it is unconfirmed.

## Chicago 18 forms

**Shortened note:** Russell, “Reflections,” 54.

**Bibliography:** Russell, Bertrand. *Portraits from Memory and Other Essays*. New York: Simon and Schuster, 1956.

## Essay use

Russell's own later account of why he turned to the foundations of mathematics: the wish for certainty “in the kind of way in which people want religious faith,” the discovery of fallacies in the demonstrations his teachers expected him to accept, the elephant and tortoise, and the conclusion after some twenty years that nothing more could be done to make mathematical knowledge indubitable.

### russell-1956-portraits-from-memory-q002 — some twenty years and the conclusion (pp. 54–55)

> But the tortoise was no more secure than the elephant, and after some twenty years of very arduous toil, I came to the conclusion that there was nothing more that I could do in the way of making mathematical knowledge indubitable.

- **Locator:** p. 55, continuing from the elephant and tortoise sentence on p. 54. The next sentence runs “Then came the First World War, and my thoughts became concentrated on human misery and folly.”
- **Status:** locator-verified; the essay paraphrases (“After some twenty years of strenuous work he concluded that he could do no more to make mathematical knowledge indubitable”), which is faithful. “Strenuous” renders Russell's “very arduous.”
