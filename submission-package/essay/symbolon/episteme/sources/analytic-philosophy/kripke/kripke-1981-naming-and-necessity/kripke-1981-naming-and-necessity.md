## Bibliographic identity

The work is the transcript of three lectures Saul A. Kripke delivered at Princeton University in January 1970. The transcript first appeared in 1972 as "Naming and Necessity" in *Semantics of Natural Language*, edited by Donald Davidson and Gilbert Harman (Dordrecht: D. Reidel), pp. 253–355 with addenda at 763–69. The first stand-alone book edition appeared in 1980, published simultaneously by Basil Blackwell (Oxford) and Harvard University Press (Cambridge, MA), with a lightly edited lecture text and a substantial new preface that exists only in the book form.

The selected object is the Harvard University Press book edition, paperback ISBN 978-0-674-59846-1 (hardcover 978-0-674-59845-4). Publisher and bookseller records date the Harvard paperback to 26 July 1980. Some library records and later printings carry 1981, and this house's `source_id` preserves the 1981 label given at intake; the citation year for the Harvard edition itself is 1980. Confirming the printing year on the copyright page of Frank's copy remains an open step, and a printing difference would leave the Chicago forms below unchanged.

Frank's encounter notes cite preface pagination — pp. 8–15, preface n. 12 on the rigidity of demonstratives (crediting David Kaplan), and the two-dice possible-worlds passage — all of which belong to the 1980 preface. This corroborates that the consulted object is the book edition rather than the 1972 Reidel article.

## Source scholarship

Kripke's argument runs against the Frege–Russell descriptivist account of proper names. A proper name is a rigid designator: it refers to the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists, while most descriptions pick out different individuals across worlds. Reference is fixed and transmitted through a historical chain of use rather than through a cluster of descriptions the speaker believes; identity statements between coreferring names ("Hesperus is Phosphorus") are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori, severing the Kantian equation of necessity with apriority. The 1980 preface defends rigidity through intuitions about the truth conditions of simple sentences in counterfactual situations, extends rigidity to demonstratives and indexicals ("this," "I," "you"; preface n. 12, crediting David Kaplan), and warns against reifying possible worlds: the dice passage treats them as stipulated counterfactual states of the world, ways things might have been, and denies them the standing of distant planets viewed through a telescope.

Frank's encounter-intent, recovered from `NOTES.md` and named here as his and only his: he reads rigidity as disclosing a layer of identification beneath the name — a counting register that individuates the entity prior to naming, the copula carrying both "is" and "equals," a long "conspiracy between name and number." He hears in Kripke's per-reading counterfactual test — asking, for each reading of a sentence taken separately, whether what it expresses would hold of a counterfactual situation exactly when the fixed individual bears the relevant property — the operational shape of apoha and dependent-arising definition-by-exclusion, with rigidity supplying the positive case he finds under-secured in Buddhist accounting. He takes the preface's appeal to intuition as matter for the psychoid register, where QL is proposed as the architecture of intuition; he places Kripke among the post-Russell divergences beside Whitehead and Spencer-Brown; and he reads the dice passage as a 36-fold state space congenial to the P/P′ 6+6′ position relation.

Claim boundary: Kripke states none of these developments. He formulates neither apoha, nor a psychoid register, nor a numeric substrate of naming; the evidential relation for each mapping is Argued from or Resonant with, to be fixed passage by passage at verification. The rigidity doctrine deepens and tests the project's naming-and-counting thread; it does not derive it.

One factual clarification for the notes' open question: the David Kaplan credited in the preface is the UCLA philosopher of demonstratives ("Dthat," "Demonstratives"). He is a different author from Robert Kaplan, whose *The Nothing That Is* the project uses elsewhere; no relation between the two is documented.
