# Arendt — The Origins of Totalitarianism (new ed., 1973)

## Bibliographic identity

Selected edition: Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism*, new ed. with added prefaces (New York: Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, 1973). First published 1951 (New York: Harcourt, Brace); the 1958 enlarged edition added the chapter “Ideology and Terror”, which stands as chapter 13, “Ideology and Terror: A Novel Form of Government,” in the 1973 edition. ## Essay use

Primary source for Arendt's distinction between isolation and loneliness and between loneliness and solitude (chapter 13, last section), and for her description of organised denunciation and the destruction of private ties under total domination (chapter 12). The essay's use in §0/1 is of these two accounts; its extension to any particular contemporary condition is its own.

**Claim boundary:** Arendt says that totalitarian government bases itself on loneliness and that ideological logic offers a last support to the lonely; she does not say, in these words, that loneliness makes a “promise of total belonging” powerful (see q001). Her account of informing is of a population organised into denunciation and of camp inmates forced into complicity; she does not describe relatives, in general, policing and consigning to death their loved ones to appease an authority (see q002, q003).

## Passage card — `arendt-1973-origins-of-totalitarianism-q001` — isolation, loneliness and solitude
^arendt-1973-origins-of-totalitarianism-q001

*(Paraphrase.)* Chapter 13 closes with a section (pp. 474–479) asking what basic experience permeates a government whose essence is terror and whose principle of action is ideological logicality. Terror can rule absolutely only over men isolated from one another; isolation is pretotalitarian, its hallmark is impotence, because power comes from men acting together (p. 474). Isolation belongs to the political sphere and loneliness to social intercourse as a whole: one can be isolated without being lonely and lonely without being isolated, and totalitarian domination, unlike tyranny, is not content with isolation but destroys private life as well, basing itself on loneliness, the experience of not belonging to the world at all (pp. 474–475). Solitude requires being alone, whereas loneliness shows itself most sharply in company; in solitude I am with myself, “two-in-one,” and thinking is done in solitude, while in loneliness I am one, deserted by others (p. 476). Solitude can become loneliness when I am deserted by my own self; loneliness loses the self and the world together, capacity for thought and experience (p. 477). What prepares people for totalitarian domination is that loneliness has become an everyday experience of the masses; the ice-cold reasoning of ideology seems a last support in a world where nobody is reliable, and it confirms identity outside relations with others; organised loneliness destroys even the chance that loneliness might become solitude (p. 478).

- **Locator:** ch. 13, “Ideology and Terror: A Novel Form of Government,” final section, pp. 474–479; the solitude/loneliness distinction at pp. 476–477; the account of what prepares people for total domination at p. 478.
- **Status:** locator-verified; no excerpt.
- **Verification:** Agent G5b, 2026-10-04; text layer of the Harcourt Brace Harvest “New Edition with Added Prefaces” scan (archive.org, `hannah-arendt-the-origins-of-totalitarianism`), page numbers from its running heads (474 to 479 end of the chapter); unclear-rights scan, locator use only; not collated against the 1973 hardcover.
- **Source relation:** paraphrased.
- **Evidential action:** supports.
- **Argument function:** isolation diminishes the capacity to act together; solitude is the condition of thinking with oneself; loneliness removes dependable relation to others and to one's own experience.
- **Consumers:** essay §0/1, M04, marker `s01-arendt`.
- **Use boundary:** supports the essay's sentence up to “loneliness makes the promise of total belonging especially powerful,” whose last clause is the author's gloss: Arendt's own statement is that totalitarianism bases itself on loneliness and that its logic offers a last support (p. 478).

## Passage card — `arendt-1973-origins-of-totalitarianism-q002` — a population organised into denouncing
^arendt-1973-origins-of-totalitarianism-q002

*(Paraphrase.)* In the chapter “Totalitarianism in Power,” section “The Secret Police,” Arendt observes that collaboration of the population in denouncing political opponents, and volunteer service as informers, are not unprecedented but in totalitarian countries are so well organised that the work of specialists is almost superfluous; in a system of ubiquitous spying, where everybody may be a police agent and each person feels under constant surveillance, every word becomes equivocal (p. 431).

- **Locator:** ch. 12, “Totalitarianism in Power,” section “The Secret Police” (begins p. 419), p. 431.
- **Status:** locator-verified; no excerpt.
- **Verification:** as q001; the passage follows the running head 431 on the same page.
- **Source relation:** paraphrased.
- **Evidential action:** supports (informing, organised across a population) and qualifies (the text speaks of a population and of everybody as a possible agent; it does not single out family members).
- **Argument function:** the population itself as the instrument of surveillance.
- **Consumers:** essay §0/1, M04, the sentence beginning “Hannah Arendt saw this going as far as friends and family policing” (no marker of its own).
- **Use boundary:** licenses organised denunciation by the population; it does not license “family members informing on one another.”

## Passage card — `arendt-1973-origins-of-totalitarianism-q003` — grief forbidden; the dilemma of betrayal
^arendt-1973-origins-of-totalitarianism-q003

*(Paraphrase.)* In the section “Total Domination,” Arendt writes that organised oblivion extends to the families and friends of the victim, grief and remembrance being forbidden; in the Soviet Union a woman will sue for divorce immediately after her husband's arrest to save her children's lives, and if he returns she turns him out (p. 452). Totalitarian terror cut the moral person off from the individualist escape by confronting a man with the choice of betraying and so murdering his friends or sending his wife and children, for whom he is responsible, to their death, so that the alternative becomes murder or murder; she cites a Greek mother whom the Nazis allowed to choose which of her three children should be killed (p. 452). The SS made concentration-camp inmates complicit by giving them administration, so that they faced the dilemma of sending friends to death or helping murder strangers (pp. 452–453).

- **Locator:** ch. 12, section “Total Domination,” pp. 452–453.
- **Status:** locator-verified; no excerpt.
- **Verification:** as q001; the passage follows the running head 452.
- **Source relation:** paraphrased.
- **Evidential action:** supports (friends and family drawn into the machinery of death) and qualifies (the dilemmas are imposed on victims and camp inmates; Arendt does not describe loved ones policing one another in the name of appeasing an authority).
- **Argument function:** terror destroys private loyalty and makes conscience equivocal.
- **Consumers:** essay §0/1, M04, the sentence beginning “Hannah Arendt saw this going as far as friends and family policing.”
- **Use boundary:** licenses the cited examples only.
