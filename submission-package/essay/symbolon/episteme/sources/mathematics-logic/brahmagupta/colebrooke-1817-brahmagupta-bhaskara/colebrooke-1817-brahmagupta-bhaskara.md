# Colebrooke — Brahmagupta and Bhāskara (1817)

## Bibliographic identity

This public-domain London edition translates mathematical works attributed to Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II. The Internet Archive scan was checked against its title page and against the rendered leaves containing Bhāskara's zero rules and Brahmagupta's algorithm. Colebrooke's nineteenth-century English is itself a historical translation layer: its “cipher,” “affirmative,” and “negative” are quotation-ready as this edition's words, not as a critical settlement of the Sanskrit.

The zero–subject historical braid uses this record for the operational transition and for the zero-denominator pressure that later exposes a formal container. It does not treat the primary mathematical text as evidence for a general consciousness-history claim.

## Essay use

The record supplies a lawful primary-text carrier for [[14-s1-p1-sunya-operational]]. Brahmagupta's rules place zero among positive and negative quantities in addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, powers, and roots. Bhāskara's rules preserve a zero-denominator expression and then compare its invariance under finite addition or subtraction with divine immutability. The mathematical rule and the analogy must be read together without making either one prove the other.

**Claim boundary:** this translation establishes what Colebrooke's 1817 English edition says at the cited verses. Philological claims about *śūnya*, *khahara*, textual dating, or the precise Sanskrit manuscripts require a critical edition and specialist scholarship. These rules also do not show that ordinary modern field division by zero is valid.

## Passage card — `colebrooke-1817-brahmagupta-bhaskara-q006` — the commentators on *khahara* and the zero-multiplier

> C'ha-hara, a fraction with cipher for its denominator. According to the remark of Gańésa, an indefinite, unlimited, or infinite quantity: since it cannot be determined how great it is. ## Passage card — `colebrooke-1817-brahmagupta-bhaskara-q008` — "termed an infinite quantity" and Kṛṣṇa's argument

> This fraction, of which the denominator is cipher, is termed an infinite quantity.

> As much as the divisor is diminished, so much is the quotient raised. If the divisor be reduced to the utmost, the quotient is to the utmost increased. But, if it can be specified, that the amount of the quotient is so much, it has not been raised to the utmost: for a quantity greater than that can be assigned. The quotient therefore is indefinitely great, and is rightly termed infinite.

- **Locator:** *Bījagaṇita* I.15, statement for "three divided by cipher", printed p. 137; and Colebrooke's note 2 to I.14, attributed "Crishn.", printed p. 137 n. 2; IIIF leaf `$233`. Note 5 gives *ananta-rāśi*, "infinite quantity", and *c'ha-hara*, "fraction having cipher for its denominator".

## Passage card — `colebrooke-1817-brahmagupta-bhaskara-q009` — the qualifying note: varied numerator, alike infinite; the gnomon's shadow

> The numerator of the infinite fraction may indeed be varied by the addition or subtraction of a finite quantity, and so it may by that of another infinite fraction: but whether the finite numerator of a fraction, whose denominator is cipher, be more or less, the quotient of its division by cipher is alike infinite.

## Passage card — `colebrooke-1817-brahmagupta-bhaskara-q013` — Kṛṣṇa on negation as contrariety; Patna, Benares, Prayāga

> here negation is of three sorts, according to place, time, and things. It is, in short, contrariety.

> So in respect of chattels, that, to which a man bears the relation of owner, is considered as positive in regard to him: and the converse [or negative quantity] is that to which another person has the relation of owner. Hence so much as belongs to Yajnyadatta in the wealth possessed by Dévadatta, is negative in respect of Dévadatta. The commentator gives as an example the situation of Pattana (Patna) and Prayága (Allahábád) relatively to Ananda-vana (Benares). Pattana on the Ganges bears east of Váránasí, distant fifteen yójanas; and Prayága on the confluence of the Gangá and Yamuná, bears west of the same, distant eight yójanas. The interval or difference is twenty-three yójanas; and is not obtained but by addition of the numbers. Therefore, if the difference between two contrary quantities be required, their sum must be taken.

- **Locator:** Colebrooke's note 3 to *Bījagaṇita* I.5 (subtraction), reporting Kṛṣṇa Bhaṭṭa's commentary, printed p. 132; IIIF leaf `$228`.
- **Status:** quotation-ready.
- **Verification:** rendered page image, agent, 2026-10-05.
- **Source relation:** extracted.
- **Evidential action:** supports (the sign of a quantity is a relation to a reference point: an owner, a city, a time).
- **Consumers:** §1 · #1 (reworked draft, 2026-10-05).
- **Pairing:** paired with q010 (Brahmagupta's subtraction rule): the rule and a commentator's case.
