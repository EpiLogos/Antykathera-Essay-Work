## Source scholarship

The dialogue is Plutarch's retrospective account, addressed to Serapion, of a discussion at Delphi when he was young. Rival explanations of the E dedicated in Apollo's temple are offered in turn. In chapters 7–8 Eustrophus proposes that E stands for five, the pempad. The narrator, the young Plutarch, then develops the number five in his own speech (chs. 8–16). In chapter 9 he digresses to the theologians' doctrine of the god's two modes: transformation into fire and solitary unity (Apollo, Phoebus), and turning into winds, water, earth, stars, plants and animals as a tearing apart and dismemberment (Dionysus, Zagreus, Nyctelius, Isodaetes). Delphi's cult calendar is read as keeping the ratio of these two periods: paean for most of the year, dithyramb at the beginning of winter. The speaker then marks the passage as a digression (ch. 10).

## Passage card — `plutarch-1936-e-at-delphi-babbitt-q002` — The theologians: the god as fire and unity, and as dismemberment and dispersion
^plutarch-1936-e-at-delphi-babbitt-q002

> Now we hear the theologians affirming and reciting, sometimes in verse and sometimes in prose, that the god is deathless and eternal in his nature, but, owing forsooth to some predestined design and reason, he undergoes transformations of his person, and at one time enkindles his nature into fire and makes it altogether like all else, and at another time he undergoes all sorts of changes in his form, his emotions and his powers, even as the universe does to-day; but he is called by the best known of his names. The more enlightened, however, concealing from the masses the transformation into fire, call him Apollo because of his solitary state, and Phoebus because of his purity and stainlessness. And as for his turning into winds and water, earth and stars, and into the generations of plants and animals, and his adoption of such guises, they speak in a deceptive way of what he undergoes in his transformation as a tearing apart, as it were, and a dismemberment. They give him the names of Dionysus, Zagreus, Nyctelius, and Isodaetes; they construct destructions and disappearances, followed by returns to life and regenerations—riddles and fabulous tales quite in keeping with the aforesaid transformations. To this god they also sing the dithyrambic strains laden with emotion and with a transformation that includes a certain wandering and dispersion. […] But to Apollo they sing the paean, music regulated and chaste.

- **Locator:** ch. 9, 388F–389B; English pp. 221–23 (Greek pp. 220–22). The ellipsis omits the Aeschylus quotation (fr. 355 Nauck). "Solitary state" renders τῇ μονώσει (389A context, Greek p. 222); "dismemberment" renders διαμελισμόν.
- **Status:** quotation-ready.
- **Verification:** agent, 2026-10-05; page images English p. 221 (n236) and p. 223 (n238), Greek p. 222 (n237) collated. Footnote markers omitted from the transcription.
- **Source relation:** extracted.
- **Evidential action:** supports, as context for q001 and q003.
- **Consumers:** §4 · #4 (reworked draft, 2026-10-05).
- **Use boundary:** the doctrine is ascribed to "the theologians," reported by the speaker, and dressed in Stoic cosmology (conflagration/arrangement; Babbitt cites Stobaeus and Clement). It is not Plutarch's settled theology. "Unity/dispersal" is a fair paraphrase (μόνωσις; πλάνη καὶ διαφόρησις), but the English words are "solitary state" and "wandering and dispersion."

## Passage card — `plutarch-1936-e-at-delphi-babbitt-q003` — In winter the dithyramb replaces the paean
^plutarch-1936-e-at-delphi-babbitt-q003

> "But since the time of the cycles in these transformations is not equal, but that of the one which they call 'Satiety,' is longer, and that of 'Dearth' shorter, they observe the ratio, and use the paean at their sacrifices for a large part of the year; but at the beginning of winter they awake the dithyramb and, laying to rest the paean, they use the dithyramb instead of it in their invocations of the god; for they believe that, as three is to one, so is the relation of the creation to the conflagration."

- **Locator:** ch. 9, 389C; English p. 225, Greek p. 224.
- **Greek printed by Babbitt (p. 224):** ἀρχομένου δὲ χειμῶνος ἐπεγείραντες τὸν διθύραμβον τὸν δὲ παιᾶνα καταπαύσαντες, τρεῖς μῆνας ἀντ᾽ ἐκείνου τοῦτον κατακαλοῦνται τὸν θεόν, ὅπερ τρία πρὸς ἐννέα τοῦτο τὴν διακόσμησιν οἰόμενοι χρόνῳ πρὸς τὴν ἐκπύρωσιν εἶναι (app. crit. n. 2: ἐννέα Bases and Strijd: ἓν or ἓν οὖσα).
- **Status:** quotation-ready (English as printed).
- **Verification:** agent, 2026-10-05; page images English p. 225 (n240) and Greek p. 224 (n239) collated; marginal C stands at Ἐπεὶ δ᾽.
- **Source relation:** extracted.
- **Evidential action:** supports.
- **Consumers:** §4 · #4 (reworked draft, 2026-10-05).
- **Use boundary (important):** Babbitt's English does **not** say "three months" or "the three winter months." The phrase τρεῖς μῆνας ("for three months they invoke this god instead of that one") is in his Greek but is not rendered in his translation. Babbitt's English also gives the ratio as "three is to one," while his printed Greek reads τρία πρὸς ἐννέα ("three to nine") after Bases and Strijd's emendation. The prose may (a) quote Babbitt's English as above, or (b) state that the Greek specifies three months, citing 389C and the Greek τρεῖς μῆνας, marked as the essay's own rendering.
