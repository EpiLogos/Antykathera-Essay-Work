# The Prisoner — Number Six, the changing Two, and the question of One

This record tells the series whole, through four selected episodes, and then lays three layers beside it: the mythemic (a participant counted by an office he cannot question), the mathemic (the signed polarity, its two collapses and the count `2 + 2² = 6`) and the epistemic (the programme's sources and the argument records). Each layer amplifies the others and keeps its own office. The television work is a television work, the notation is the essay's, and the comparison between them is the essay's too. Its status is Argued.

## #0 — The resignation that becomes an address

<a id="prisoner-resignation-arrival"></a>

An intelligence agent resigns. He leaves an office which has a claim on his knowledge, returns home and prepares to depart. Before he can carry that decision into another life, he is abducted. He wakes in surroundings that resemble his own room, but the room now belongs to the Village. The familiar interior opens onto a picturesque coastal settlement that he has no freedom to leave.

The Village wants the reason for his resignation. Its administration already knows much about him, and what it seeks is his cooperation in completing the account. Its visible chief is Number Two. He asks where he is, who rules, and whose side the institution serves. His own institutional designation is Number Six. His number arrives with accommodation, supervision and the means of preventing departure.

“Arrival” gives this arrangement material actors. Technicians watch the settlement; Rover, the mobile balloon-like guardian, intercepts escape. In the hospital Six encounters his former colleague Cobb, then learns that Cobb has supposedly died. A woman, Number Nine, offers an escape arrangement associated with him. Her pass allows Six to reach a helicopter, but its controls are taken over remotely and it brings him back. Cobb's death was staged. Meanwhile another man has already become Number Two. That change of administrator belongs to the first encounter with the Village, before a season of replacements makes the office familiar. This sequence follows the recovered [written “Arrival” telling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrival_%28The_Prisoner%29); the [canonical source house](../../../../episteme/sources/media-technology-philosophy/mcgoohan-markstein/mcgoohan-markstein-1967-the-prisoner/mcgoohan-markstein-1967-the-prisoner.md) **sources** the original programme and the established series-level account.

A recurring title exchange compresses this situation:

> **Six:** Who is Number One?  
> **Two:** You are Number Six.  
> **Six:** I am not a number; I am a free man!

Two's laughter follows. Its source house's [q001](../../../../episteme/sources/media-technology-philosophy/mcgoohan-markstein/mcgoohan-markstein-1967-the-prisoner/mcgoohan-markstein-1967-the-prisoner.md#mcgoohan-markstein-1967-the-prisoner-q001) **sources** this exact wording. There is no comma after “are” that would turn the reply into a concealed answer identifying Six as One. What the opening does is assign the questioner's number. Its standard spoken sequence occurs in fourteen episodes, with “Arrival,” “Living in Harmony” and “Fall Out” outside that repeated form. [Opening transcript and reproduced production-script evidence](https://www.theunmutual.co.uk/COMMA.HTM).

This whole is the original seventeen-episode British television series conceived by Patrick McGoohan with George Markstein, produced by Everyman Films and ITC and broadcast on ITV in 1967–68. Four episodes are enlarged: “Arrival,” “Free for All,” “Once Upon a Time” and “Fall Out.” They carry entry, administered participation, the final interrogation and the return; the remaining episodes retain their places in the programme's field below. Outside this telling lie the 2009 remake and the later novels, comics and audio continuations.

## #1 — A whole world for obtaining one answer

<a id="prisoner-administered-world"></a>

The Village is capable of staging the circumstances in which Six seeks to act. An apparent ally can be part of an interrogation; an escape route can return its user to confinement; an invitation to participate can bring resistance inside an administered procedure. Its repeated demand concerns a past decision, but its means reach into the prisoner's present world. Surveillance, drugs, impersonation, public ceremonies and arranged social encounters pursue what direct questioning fails to obtain. Six answers with concealment, refusal, testing and counteraction. Number Two changes; the demand and the resources behind it continue.

“Free for All” gives participation an electoral form. Six accepts a contest for Number Two's position after the prospect of meeting Number One is held out to him. Number Fifty-Eight becomes his assistant. His campaign is already prepared to display him: publicity bearing his face is ready, and a newspaper reporter supplies acceptable answers when Six declines to give them. His refusal can be printed as a campaign statement. [Clayton Barr's episode study](https://prisoner.popapostle.com/html/episodes/P67/Free-for-All.htm) **sources** these reported details of the publicity machinery.

Six promises the inhabitants freedom, undergoes drugging and coercion, and wins the vote. At the Green Dome, his attempt to use the office to free the Village meets violence. Fifty-Eight, previously speaking a language he cannot understand, now addresses him in English and takes authority as the incoming Number Two. The outgoing administrator leaves, and the election has carried Six into the office without giving his command the power to end captivity. This ending follows the [written episode synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_for_All_%28The_Prisoner%29), alongside the official [ITVX episode entry](https://www.itv.com/watch/the-prisoner/ENT1307/ENT1307a0004).

## #2 — The last interrogation and the road beyond the Village

<a id="prisoner-degree-absolute-return"></a>

In “Once Upon a Time,” the Number Two previously encountered in “The Chimes of Big Ben” returns. He commits to Degree Absolute: a week of isolation in the embryo room with Six and the Butler. His interrogation works through regression, nursery language, counting and enacted stages of life. Two tries to occupy the positions from which Six will answer. The struggle reverses direction; Six gains the upper hand and Two dies as the allotted period ends. When the Supervisor arrives, Six demands to see Number One. This sequence follows the recovered [episode study](https://prisoner.popapostle.com/html/episodes/P67/Once-Upon-a-Time.htm). Its reported actions are used here; the study's speculative explanations of the equipment and Butler's allegiance are not adopted.

“Fall Out” takes Six before a masked assembly and its President. His resistance is acclaimed as an achievement qualifying him for freedom and possibly leadership. Two is resuscitated. Two and the younger Number Forty-Eight appear as forms of revolt to be judged; Six is ceremonially distinguished from them. When invited to speak, his attempt at a first-person address is drowned out by the assembly. He is then brought to One. Beneath the outer mask is an ape mask, and beneath that a face identical to his own. Six starts a rocket countdown, frees Two and Forty-Eight, and escapes with them and the Butler through armed resistance. This sequence follows the [written finale synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_Out_%28The_Prisoner%29).

The departure continues beyond the unmasking, as the escapees travel toward London. Forty-Eight goes his own way; Two enters Parliament; the Butler accompanies Six to his home. Its door opens automatically, recalling the Village's doors. Six returns to his car, and the closing driving image recalls the series' beginning. These reported aftermath details are retained from [Barr's finale study](https://prisoner.popapostle.com/html/episodes/P67/Fall-Out.htm), with its speculative explanations kept separate. Its source house and [BFI's account](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/prisoner-patrick-mcgoohan-50) **qualify** any claim that London supplies an unequivocal final exterior to the prison.

The finale changes the first question without replacing the entire programme with its last revelation. The Village has abducted, confined, watched and coerced people, and Six's own face under One's masks enters after those acts and within a continuing escape. It leaves a relation between the institution's sovereign position and the participant capable of taking that position within himself. The material apparatus stays part of the same image.

## #3 — Two names Six while One remains unanswerable

<a id="prisoner-asymmetric-count"></a>

A number does different work at the two ends of the Village's administration. "Number Two" names an office that persists as its bearers change. Access, command and institutional memory pass to the next administrator, and the office gains continuity through replacement. "Number Six" names a person the office means to hold in an assigned position, and the captive is denied any authority over how his designation will count. One is the withheld source of command, Two exercises its delegated authority, and Six receives an answer that classifies him, so that his inquiry into who may order the field is redirected into an order within the field. The seams ledger's [whole-series interpretation](../../../../../quilt/final-argument-quilt-2026-08-23/MYTHEME-AND-DEEP-SOURCE-SEAMS.md) **sources** this distinction among three offices: delegated command, assigned identity, and the source to which the prisoner asks to answer.

A number can identify a person accurately in a situated record, and [identification through difference](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A02-Copula-Self-Identity-through-Difference.md) **defines** the line between identifying a participant and making a predicate exhaustive of him. Political appropriation begins when the office treats its assignment as authority over the subject who answers. [Faithful definition](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A01-Subject-God-and-Faithful-Definition.md) **grounds** the requirement that an account stay answerable to what it names, and [the immutable gap](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A03-Immutable-Gap-Formal-Limit.md) **qualifies** the demand to close that relation in one final determination. Six's refusal figures this limit in an institutional encounter. No formal theorem is proved by the series, and the institution's concealed information is not itself the Immutable Gap.

Laid beside the signed arithmetic, the Village's count is appropriation in both chiralities. An office reckons the whole span between itself and the individual from its own pole: what he is, what he owes, what he must say. That reckoning is `(+1)−(−1)=+2` for the institution, and the same span charged to Six is `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, a finished index he is handed and cannot revise. Six's refusal, "I am not a number; I am a free man!", claims the held polarity `(−1)/(+1)`, in which administrator and administered each have their sense through the other and the zero between them is not owned by the office. The Village performs cancellation as well. When "Free for All" prints his refusal as a campaign statement, the dissent is absorbed into the institution's own material, and the polar term sums with the office's publicity to nothing, `(−1)+(+1)=0`. This amplification lays three layers together: the mythemic action of the series, the mathemic operations of cut and gathering, and the epistemic records of the count. Its status is Argued, and the programme supplies no notation. [A13 grounds the exact political consequence of the cut](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A13-Two-Logics-of-Two-Dia-Syn.md): administrator and administered stay distinct, and the power that produces and maintains their unequal relation disappears behind their designations. Six's answer tests whether that reckoning can return to an encounter able to change the region's terms.
