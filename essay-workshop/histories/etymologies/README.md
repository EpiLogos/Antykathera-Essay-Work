# Etymologies — Meaning Fields, Word-Histories, and Re-entries

An etymology begins where a word remembers the paths by which it arrived; it becomes useful to *The Return of Zero* when that memory lets an operation become more exact in the present. The field therefore keeps both matters in view. A word's documented descent can correct the thought that receives it, while a pun, a shared sound, or a cross-cultural recurrence can return a live question to language in a way no pedigree could prove. Neither office should counterfeit the other.

These are repeatable anchors for the essay's flights: a writer can return to *nomos* when a number becomes a norm, to *symbolon* when a mark bears a separated relation, or to *homologia* when several traditions seem to answer in one ratio. The entry supplies the word-history, identifies the operation the essay may take up, and states the relation's evidential register before it enters an argument.

**Initial intake provenance:** the user-provided etymological notes of 16 July 2026. Their strongest historical leads have been retained as leads or source-shelf tasks until a lexical or primary source is attached; their authorial and poetic pressure is retained in the operational and phonic registers.

## The four registers

| Register | What it records | What it may do in the essay |
|---|---|---|
| **Philological descent** | A documented cognate, derivation, morphology, or language-specific semantic history. | Support a historical or lexical claim once its cited lexical or primary source is stable. |
| **Attested semantic field** | Several historically recorded senses of one word, without asserting a single philosophical essence behind them. | Make a word's available range explicit and preserve tensions that a modern gloss suppresses. |
| **Operational homology** | A precisely named operation recurring across otherwise distinct words, practices, or traditions. | Offer an authorial comparison under the project’s *pros hen* discipline; it never proves common descent or identity of doctrine. |
| **Poetic / phonic re-entry** | Pun, sound relation, folk etymology, or unexpected verbal echo that genuinely discloses a live operation. | Work as marked authorial insight, question, or image; never serve as historical attribution or source evidence. |

The last two are not embarrassed versions of the first two. They have their own truth-conditions: the operation has to be named, its local consequences have to be shown, and the involved traditions must retain their own terms, history, and warrant. A rhyme is not a root; a root does not dictate a theorem.

## How an entry is built

Each cluster keeps five things together:

1. **Word record** — forms, languages, and the exact relation being claimed.
2. **Meaning-field** — the operation that makes the cluster available to this essay.
3. **Relation ledger** — whether a connection is descent, attested semantic range, operational homology, or poetic re-entry.
4. **Source shelf** — dictionaries, editions, grammars, or passages needed to move a lead toward quotation-ready evidence. These remain internal research references unless a work begins to bear a first-class burden elsewhere in the essay.
5. **Argument routes** — exact sections, concepts, arguments, and histories where the field does work. The linked canonical node remains the authority for the claim.

The field is deliberately porous: a single word can belong to more than one cluster, and a cluster can enter more than one history. The relation is named each time rather than inferred from adjacency.
