### jung-1976-psychological-types-cw6-p003 — Superior and inferior functions in Schiller's diagnosis

**Locator:** ch. II, ¶¶105–113; PDF pp. 76–82. Index-inferred printed pp. c. 68–76 ("differentiation of, and culture, 70–73"; "suppression of inferior functions, 63, 69–70, 72, 74").

**Located operation:** Jung follows Schiller in tracing the separation of functions to culture, the differentiation of one function causing the hypertrophy of one and the atrophy of others (¶105); the Christian world moved the ancient subjugation of the many into the individual, so that a privileged function dominates an enslaved inferior majority, the "ever-bleeding wound" of modern man (¶108); man is represented by his one differentiated function (¶109); one-sided development must provoke a reaction, since the suppressed functions cannot be excluded indefinitely (¶112); the neglected functions may hide higher individual values (¶113).

### jung-1976-psychological-types-cw6-p004 — Acknowledgement of the inferior function unites the opposites

**Locator:** ch. II, ¶115 (with ¶114); PDF p. 83 (¶114 PDF p. 82). Index-inferred printed pp. c. 75–77 ("inferior functions … need to develop/accept, 74–77"; "redemption, 76").

**Located operation:** After setting Schiller's turn to antiquity against the Christian doctrine of redemption, which detached the one valuable function (¶114), Jung concludes that on the path of nature it is not a detachment or redemption of the inferior function but its acknowledgement, a coming to terms with it, that unites the opposites; he adds Schiller's fear that accepting it unleashes a conflict of blind instincts, an internal civil war that threatens freedom (¶115).

**Status:** locator-verified; paraphrase-only. ¶115 is the paragraph for the essay's claim; ¶114 is the contrast.

### jung-1976-psychological-types-cw6-p005 — The reconciliation as "beautiful illusion"

**Locator:** ch. III, ¶228 (with ¶¶226, 230); PDF p. 137 (¶226 PDF p. 136). Index-inferred printed pp. c. 139–41 ("Apollinian impulse: reconciliation with Dionysian, 140–41"; "Apollo, 138–39").

**Located operation:** Nietzsche takes the reconciliation of the Delphic Apollo with Dionysus as a symbol of reconciled opposites in the civilized Greek; Jung answers that by Nietzsche's own compensatory formula this reconciliation would be a "beautiful illusion," a desideratum of the civilized Greek struggling with his barbarian side (¶228). The phrase picks up Nietzsche's description of Apollo as ruling the beautiful illusion of the inner world of fantasy (¶226). Jung adds that Schiller and Nietzsche both credit art with a redeeming role, leaving the problem stuck in aesthetics (¶230).

**Status:** locator-verified; paraphrase-only. The words "beautiful illusion" are attested in the carrier at ¶228 but not admitted as quotation (carrier provenance unregistered).

### jung-1976-psychological-types-cw6-p006 — Dionysian extraversion, Apollinian introversion

**Locator:** ch. III, ¶¶235–236, 238; PDF p. 141. Index-inferred printed p. c. 144 ("introversion: Apollinian, 144"; "intoxication and Dionysian impulse, … 144").

**Located operation:** In the Dionysian state sensation participates to the highest degree: it is an extraversion of the feelings bound up with sensation, which Jung calls feeling-sensation (¶235). The Apollinian is a perception of inner images of beauty and measure, a state of introspection and hence of introversion (¶236). Qualifier: Nietzsche's pair lacks the differentiation Jung's attitude-types require; the Dionysian is archaic affective sensation and the Apollinian an inner perception or intuition of the world of ideas (¶238).

**Status:** locator-verified; paraphrase-only.

**Verification:** as p003.

**Source relation:** Paraphrased; ¶238 is the qualifying pair.

### jung-1976-psychological-types-cw6-p007 — The pair points to the "aesthetic" types

**Locator:** ch. III, ¶¶239–240 (with ¶¶241–242); PDF p. 142. Index-inferred printed pp. c. 145–46 ("aesthetic standpoint, 145"; "intuitive type … 145–46"; "sensation type … 145–46").

**Located operation:** Beside the rational elaborations there is an aesthetic standpoint which in introversion develops intuition and in extraversion sensation (¶239); Nietzsche's concepts therefore lead to a third and fourth type, "aesthetic" as opposed to rational: the intuitive and the sensation types (¶240); the sensation type is the converse of the intuitive (¶241); Nietzsche himself is read as an intuitive with leanings to introversion (¶242).
