## Source scholarship

Book 21 narrates the contest of the bow in Odysseus’s hall. Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, strings the great bow that none of the suitors could string, and the simile compares him to a man skilled in the lyre stretching a string about a new peg. Tom Mackenzie argues that Heraclitus B51’s bow and lyre allude to this simile (see `mackenzie-2021-heraclitean-allusion-odyssey`); the allusion is Mackenzie’s argument, not a statement of the poem.

## Passage card — `homer-1919-odyssey-murray-vol2-q001` — the bow strung like a lyre
^homer-1919-odyssey-murray-vol2-q001

> “even as when a man well-skilled in the lyre and in song easily stretches the string about a new peg, making fast at either end the twisted sheep-gut—so without effort did Odysseus string the great bow. And he held it in his right hand, and tried the string, which sang sweetly beneath his touch, like to a swallow in tone.”
