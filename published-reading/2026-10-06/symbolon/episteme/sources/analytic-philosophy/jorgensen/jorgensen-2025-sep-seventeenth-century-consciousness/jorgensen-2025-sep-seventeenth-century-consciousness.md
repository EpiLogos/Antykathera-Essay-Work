## Source scholarship

A peer-reviewed survey of how *consciousness* acquired its modern psychological sense in the seventeenth century across Descartes, Spinoza, Hobbes, the Cambridge Platonists, Locke and Leibniz, with attention to the shared Latin and French word for conscience and consciousness.

<a id="jorgensen-2025-sep-seventeenth-century-consciousness-q001"></a>
## Passage card — `jorgensen-2025-sep-seventeenth-century-consciousness-q001` — The linguistic transition: conscientia and conscience take a psychological sense

**Paraphrase:** Latin *conscientia* and French *conscience* each served for both moral conscience and consciousness; before the seventeenth century they primarily signified moral conscience, and in that century they took on a purely psychological meaning, philosophers being among the chief influences on the shift. English *consciousness* became widely used in the seventeenth century for the new concept; German *Bewusstsein* was coined in the early eighteenth. The semantic shift marked a philosophical division between the psychological or phenomenal aspects of thought and a moral sensibility.

- **Locator:** §1 “The Linguistic Transition,” and the entry’s opening summary.
- **Status:** locator-verified; paraphrase-only
- **Verification:** Read 2026-10-04; no quotation promoted.
- **Source relation:** Paraphrased
- **Evidential action:** Supports the claim that the division between consciousness and conscience took shape chiefly in the seventeenth century.

## Passage card — `jorgensen-2025-sep-seventeenth-century-consciousness-q002` — Cudworth: “con-sense” or “consciousness” (True Intellectual System, p. 159)

**Paraphrase:** A largely independent English line ran through the Cambridge Platonists, especially Ralph Cudworth, who reflecting on Plotinus’s *synaisthesis* (“sensed with”) rendered it in English as “con-sense” or “consciousness,” citing *The True Intellectual System of the Universe* (London: Richard Royston, 1678), p. 159; the entry notes that Thiel (1991) argues this line is independent of the Cartesian one.

- **Locator:** §4.2 “The Cambridge Platonists.”
- **Status:** locator-verified; paraphrase-only
- **Verification:** Read 2026-10-04; the 1678 page reference is the entry’s, not independently checked against a copy of Cudworth.
- **Source relation:** Paraphrased
- **Evidential action:** Names Cudworth’s work and the page at which he uses the word *consciousness*; SEP’s Cudworth entry (Hutton) likewise lists “consciousness” among his coinages.

## Passage card — `jorgensen-2025-sep-seventeenth-century-consciousness-q003` — Locke: consciousness without moral sensibility

**Paraphrase:** Locke uses consciousness in his theory of personal identity, which is a purely psychological account with implications for moral responsibility; consciousness itself is not equated with the moral sensibility. The entry quotes his definition of *person* from *Essay* 2.27.9.

- **Locator:** §4.3 “Locke.”
