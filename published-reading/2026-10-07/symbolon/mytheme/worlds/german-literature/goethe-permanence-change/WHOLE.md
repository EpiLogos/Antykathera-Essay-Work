# Goethe — Permanence in Change

This record serves the essay's account of the One and the All as Taylor reads them, and it works in the mytheme register: Goethe's *Dauer im Wechsel* is told whole, in the order of its five stanzas, and the relation is read from what the speaker undergoes. The One-in-change reading belongs to Taylor, and the poem supplies a lived pressure on it and no proof of it.

## #0

<a id="goethe-blossom-and-shade"></a>

The speaker begins by asking that this early blessing might be held for just one hour. Even as he asks, the mild west wind shakes down a full shower of blossom: the force that carries the beauty away is gentle, and the loss starts inside the weather that belongs to its flourishing.

Green foliage offers another occasion for pleasure, and the speaker has only just thanked it for its shade. He sees autumn already inside that gift: the leaves will turn pale and a storm will scatter them. Within eight lines the poem has passed through a wish, a loss already accomplished, a question about enjoyment and a loss still to come, and spring's passing is felt from within the pleasure it gives. The question the speaker asks is how to receive something whose passing has already entered the reception.

All forty German lines of *Dauer im Wechsel* appear in the [Klett 2010 witness](https://www2.klett.de/sixcms/media.php/229/350470_0239_Goethe_Dauer.pdf), which identifies the Frankfurt edition of Goethe's works, I.2, *Gedichte 1800–1832* (1988), pp. 78–79. That witness is distinct from [John Frederick Nims's selected 1983 translation](../../../../episteme/sources/literature/goethe/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims.md), whose page 169 is not yet collated. "Permanence and Change" is this project's alias for the same poem, and the account here follows the German whole without supplying or impersonating Nims's translation.

## #1

<a id="goethe-fruit-valley-river"></a>

In the second stanza the wish to hold meets an instruction to take one's share. Whoever wants the fruit has to reach for it promptly, because ripening and germination overlap: while one growth approaches fulfilment another has begun. What passes therefore includes the loss of a present gift together with the arrival of another, and the imperative is to take part in this ripening, since waiting for a moment secured against change would miss what is there to be taken.

Rain widens the movement from plant to landscape: with every downpour the beloved valley changes, recognisable enough to be addressed as one's own and given no exemption by affection. Then the stanza steps into the river: one does not swim in the same river a second time. The swimmer now takes part bodily in what had been observed from the shade and the bank, carried forward through a medium that cannot be kept for a repeat encounter.

Several temporal movements are held together in these eight lines: fruit has its moment of readiness, germination begins beside it, rain changes the valley through repeated events, and swimming cannot recover the same encounter. Such different ways of arriving, receiving and passing prepare the turn in which the one who notices change becomes its subject. [German whole, lines 9–16; source and witness](../../../../episteme/sources/literature/goethe/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims.md#passages).

## #2

<a id="goethe-changing-eyes-and-limbs"></a>

Now the poem turns directly on the addressee. Walls and palaces seem rock-solid, and yet we always see them with different eyes, so that recognition has a history on the observer's side. The apparently enduring object cannot secure an identical experience of itself when the person looking has changed.

Next it leaves the spectator's eyes for bodily powers remembered through their acts. The lip that once found healing in a kiss has vanished, and so has the foot that measured itself against the cliff with a chamois's boldness. Intimacy and daring make the body's alteration particular, and what is lost in each case is a way of meeting the world: receiving a kiss, trusting one's footing, answering a height with movement.

Walls and cliff do different work: the walls occasion the recognition that seeing changes, and the cliff recalls a bodily capacity that once met its challenge, and neither provides an immovable observer outside the changes being described, and the poem has carried the person who wanted to retain the blossom into the very loss that his desire first set before him. [German whole, lines 17–24](../../../../episteme/sources/literature/goethe/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims.md#passages).

## #3

<a id="goethe-hand-name-and-wave"></a>

The body continues across the stanza break into the fourth stanza. The hand that moved willingly and gently to do good, and the whole articulated form it belonged to, are declared changed, so that lip, foot and hand are gathered into one bodily form and all of it is different now. Kindness belongs to the history of this body together with pleasure and daring, and physical change reaches the means by which those acts became possible.

After that the name stays where its bearer has altered. What now calls itself by that name has arrived like a wave and hurries on toward the element. Water enters the description of the named person here, and a recognisable designation continues across a succession that it cannot arrest.

A wave is distinct enough for a moment to be met and named, while its arrival and its departure belong to the element, and the poem narrates no return of the old body unchanged, and a continued name restores neither lip nor foot nor hand. By the end of line 32 the question of duration includes the bodily bearer of the question, and the last stanza has to answer from within that condition. [German whole, lines 25–32](../../../../episteme/sources/literature/goethe/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims.md#passages).

## #4

<a id="goethe-beginning-end-muses"></a>

Another imperative closes the poem: beginning and end are to draw together into one, faster than the objects themselves flee past the addressee. The opening asked that an early blessing stay for an hour, and the ending asks for an act of gathering across its passage, so that what time has separated is brought into a relation holding beginning and end together.

Here the Muses enter, and their favour promises something imperishable, and the addressee is asked to give thanks. The closing lines separate what is carried in the breast from its form in the mind:

> Den Gehalt in deinem Busen  
> Und die Form in deinem Geist.

Content borne inwardly and the form through which it is apprehended have different places in the closing address, and the Muses give the gathering an artistic office. The poem has itself brought blossom, fruit, valley, swimmer, changing eyes, bodily acts and the named wave into one formed sequence, and its ending can receive the beginning because the intervening losses have been articulated.

What the Muses offer remains a promise, and the lyric supplies no sequel in which the aging person recovers the earlier body or the valley stops changing, and its continuation lies in what the closing distinction makes available, an inwardly borne content and a formed expression that can carry the relation onward. The opening wish for duration returns changed by the poem that has answered it. [German whole, lines 33–40; verified German closing words](../../../../episteme/sources/literature/goethe/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims/goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims.md#goethe-1983-selected-poems-nims-q002).

<a id="goethe-two-ones-return"></a>

### One in change

Taylor's One-in-change reading relates Goethe's complete lyric to his Two Ones. The blossom passes, the perceiver changes, the named wave hastens toward its element, and the final gathering receives those movements through content and form, so that a final-stanza fragment taken alone cannot carry what the preceding embodied losses have made consequential.

The [Two Ones](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A11-The-Two-Ones-0-One-1-All.md) **define** `0 = One = Self-Identity` and `1 = All = Self-Difference`. The All includes each changing determination and the manifest field in which it appears, and the One has the source-office through which those determinations belong within a relation. Their offices are reciprocal: manifestation depends on its source, and source becomes articulated and knowable through manifestation. The poem returns a lived pressure to this native relation, since change reaches the perceiver, the body and the named identity, and the concluding unity has to receive those differences if it is to receive the poem at all.

A wave arrives, takes a distinguishable form and passes toward its element, and in Taylor's reading the particular stays real while it belongs to a whole that it neither originates nor possesses. [Mono/Poly](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A12-Mono-Poly-One-All-Whole-Many.md) **grounds** that reading by keeping the changed bearer of the name, the remembered acts and the element together through different offices. Turning the element into an unchanged personal substance would discard the bodily changes through which the poem reaches it, and equating poetic water with QL's `0` would introduce a symbol-key that nothing in the poem supports.

Through form the final gathering deepens the same relation. Beginning and end are joined after the body has been followed through its passing, and content in the breast and form in the mind give received life and its articulation different offices within the achieved whole. Taylor's reading meets this deepening without equating the German pair word for word with the two native Ones, so that content and form, and source and manifest, stay two stated operations.

An [integral return](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A36-Advent-of-Integral-Zero.md) **extends** the poem's ending: it carries change without pretending to restore what has passed. Reading the ending returns the reader to the first desire for an hour of arrested beauty, and the poem in between has made that desire more exact, since it now includes the changing person who desires, the bodily acts at stake and the means by which an experience receives form. The opening blossom remains lost within the relation the lyric now carries. In a comparative reading the interpreting agent is the relative functional knower, the poem and the proposed relations are known, and text, retained context and comparison rules are means. This apparatus is in turn a means within the containing person's inquiry, and the poem's lived bodily losses and inward reception keep their own personed office. A discrepant reading can correct a claim, a task, an interpretation or a relevant governing condition, or warrant retaining a fitting condition with reasons, and the consequence belongs in what the reader subsequently says, judges or does.

<a id="goethe-witness-and-reception"></a>

### The poem's world and its later witnesses

The German-literature home names the work's cultural and linguistic world, and the valley, river, buildings and cliff belong to its narrated field, and no particular geographical location is established for them here. The passing seasons and the aging body constitute the poem's time, which is distinct from the dates through which its editions and translations have reached this project.

The Klett sheet, a Stuttgart publication of 2010, cites the Frankfurt critical edition of 1988, and those dates identify witnesses and later educational reception. The source house separately identifies Nims's 1970 magazine publication, the selected Princeton collection of 1983 and the 1994 reprint. Exact wording in the selected translation remains uncollated, and its final-stanza fragment prompted the project's admission, and that fragment is no verified quotation from page 169 and no recovered independent Taylor utterance. No full modern English translation is reproduced here.

Our German closing words are verified at the extracted-text level of the Klett witness, and its page image and the cited critical-edition pages remain uncollated. These limits concern quotation provenance, whereas all forty German lines sustain the embodied losses and the artistic gathering, and the One-in-change relation is Taylor's interpretation. Other Goethe works keep their own identities, and neither *Selige Sehnsucht*, *Eins und Alles* nor *Faust* substitutes for this lyric.

The [Two Ones](../../../../../section-rooms/arguments/A11-The-Two-Ones-0-One-1-All.md) **embody** their relation in the lyric through the changed observer, the passing named wave and the gathering of beginning and end. The particular life stays real within a wider whole, and its changed experience receives a formed continuation. These operations depend on the full forty-line movement, in which the remembered acts, the vanished bodily powers and the remaining name belong to the unity the final stanza can receive.
