### jung-2009-red-book-liber-novus-p001 — Prometheus is forethinking (Liber Primus, "Mysterium. Encounter," p. 247, with n. 168)

**Provenance:** as recorded for this source: edition detail: First edition (2009) of the large-format folio volume (hardcover, ISBN 978-0-393-06567-1, Philemon Series) that sets the facsimile folios beside the English translation; the translation section, with Shamdasani's editorial footnotes at the foot of each page, carries the page numbers cited here. These are not the page numbers of the separate 2012 Reader's Edition, whose pagination differs.; local copy recorded in this house; accessed 2026-10-04.

**Locator:** Liber Primus, chap. IX, "Mysterium. Encounter," p. 247 (text), nn. 167–168 on the same page; the discussion of Elijah and Salome as forethinking and pleasure runs pp. 246–248.

**Located operation:** In the "powers of my depths" passage Jung's text pairs *predetermination* (also rendered *forethinking*) with *pleasure* and names the first Prometheus, the one who, without determined thoughts, brings the chaotic to form and definition and holds the object before pleasure. Elijah and Salome are the two principles in person: "the dark space is the space of forethinking" (p. 247), and p. 248 has Jung say that, being a thinker, he met the principle of pleasure from forethinking as Salome. Shamdasani's n. 167 records that the Corrected Draft replaces "predetermination or forethought" with "The Idea"; n. 168 glosses Prometheus as the maker of mankind from clay who could foretell the future and whose name signifies forethought, and refers to Jung's 1921 analysis of Carl Spitteler's *Prometheus and Epimetheus* with Goethe's *Prometheus* fragment in *Psychological Types*, CW 6, chap. 5. Shamdasani's note does not itself call Prometheus Logos; the equation with Logos is Jung's in the sense set out in p002 below, and it is joined across pp. 247 and 365, not stated in one sentence.

**Status:** locator-verified. No quotation admitted by this card (see header).

**Source relation:** Paraphrased; Argued from (the identification of the forethinking principle with Logos goes through p002).

**Consumer:** M05 note `s01-jung-prometheus`.

### jung-2009-red-book-liber-novus-p002 — Elijah's principle named Logos (Appendix B, p. 365)

**Provenance:** as recorded for this source: edition detail: First edition (2009) of the large-format folio volume (hardcover, ISBN 978-0-393-06567-1, Philemon Series) that sets the facsimile folios beside the English translation; the translation section, with Shamdasani's editorial footnotes at the foot of each page, carries the page numbers cited here. These are not the page numbers of the separate 2012 Reader's Edition, whose pagination differs.; local copy recorded in this house; accessed 2026-10-04.

**Locator:** Appendix B, "Excerpts from Liber Novus, layer 2: Commentaries," p. 365 (Jung's commentary on the Mystery-play images, the editor's cross-reference there to Liber Novus pp. 245–248), continuing p. 366.

**Located operation:** Jung's own commentary on the Elijah and Salome images states that the old man represents a spiritual principle that could be called Logos and the maiden an unspiritual principle of feeling that could be called Eros, and describes Logos as an independent principle of form meaning understanding, insight, foresight, legislation and wisdom; the old prophet is its fitting allegory. The same commentary reads the serpent as what adheres to Logos as well as Eros, and Salome as Elijah's daughter, an order of succession that marks Eros's subordination to Logos.

**Status:** locator-verified. No quotation admitted by this card.

**Source relation:** Extracted (the Logos/Eros naming); Argued from (its joining to p001).

**Consumer:** M05 note `s01-jung-prometheus`.

### jung-2009-red-book-liber-novus-p005 — The desert: the self, the spirit of this time and of the depths, the Word (Liber Primus, chap. IV, "The Desert," p. 236)

**Locator:** Liber Primus, chap. IV, "The Desert," p. 236 (the chapter opens on p. 235; its running head "THE DESERT" is on p. 235 and the page-236 header is "LIBER PRIMUS"). On p. 236: the paragraph on giving the soul all he could give and finding the place of the soul a hot desert, with the sentence on the self that "enters in there" and on the one who is neither in events, nor in men, nor in his thoughts, and the contrast between "the spirit of this time in me" and "the spirit of the depths"; and, a few paragraphs on, the Word as a creative act ("in the beginning was the Word") followed by the line on the words that oscillate between nonsense and supreme meaning. The sub-chapter "Experiences in the Desert" begins lower on the same page.

**Located operation:** The desert as the place of the soul reached when desire turns away from things and men; the turn leaves him captive to his thoughts; the creative act of the Word; the oldest and truest words as oscillating between nonsense and supreme meaning.

### jung-2009-red-book-liber-novus-p006 — Divine madness and the two delusions (Liber Primus, chap. V, "Descent into Hell in the Future," p. 238)

**Locator:** Liber Primus, chap. V, "Descent into Hell in the Future," p. 238 (the chapter opens on p. 237). The paragraph beginning with the desert coming to bloom and strange plants, through the passage that distinguishes divine madness from sick delusion by the spirit of the depths overpowering the spirit of this time, and sick delusion when the spirit of the depths forces a man to speak in tongues and believe he is that spirit, or when the spirit of this time forces him to see only the surface and take himself for the spirit of the times.

**Located operation:** Madness named as divine life when it is the overpowering of the spirit of this time by the spirit of the depths; two forms of sick delusion, one from each spirit.

### jung-2009-red-book-liber-novus-p007 — Meaning as a moment and a transition (Liber Primus, chap. VII, "Murder of the Hero," p. 242)

**Locator:** Liber Primus, chap. VII, "Murder of the Hero," p. 242 (running head "242 | LIBER PRIMUS fol. iv(v)/v(r)"), left column, in the section marked [2] that follows the dream of the murder of Siegfried; the sentence stands in the closing lines of the italicised verse-like paragraph on day and night, meaning and absurdity, a few lines above the heading of chap. ### jung-2009-red-book-liber-novus-p008 — God set outside, God moved into the self; "my God has everyone" (Liber Primus, chap. VIII, "The Conception of the God," p. 245)

**Locator:** Liber Primus, chap. VIII, "The Conception of the God" (pp. 242-245), p. 245, left column, the closing discourse of the chapter just above the heading of chap. IX, "Mysterium. Encounter" (running head "MYSTERIUM. ENCOUNTER | 245"). Shamdasani's n. 153 attaches after "what is outside us" in the second sentence of the passage; n. 152 sits on the sentence before it.

**Located operation:** Jung sets singleness within and communality outside, says that a God set outside the self is more powerful than we are and tears us loose from the self so that the self falls into privation, and that a God moved into the self takes us from what is outside, so that we arrive at singleness in ourselves; he then states that no one has his God but his God has everyone, himself included, and that the Gods of all individual men have all other men, so that it is always only the one God despite his multiplicity.

**Printed wording against the essay's transcription (reported, essay prose not edited):** the page prints "a God" and "the God" with a capital G throughout, "outside of ourselves", "he snatches us", "We arrive at singleness in ourselves" and "my God has everyone"; the essay's quotation has lower-case "god", "outside ourselves", "he snatched", "We arrived at singleness into ourselves" and "my god". Both debts named in the verification file ("he snatched", "singleness into ourselves") are therefore live against this edition; whether the owner worked from another printing or a draft is not known.
