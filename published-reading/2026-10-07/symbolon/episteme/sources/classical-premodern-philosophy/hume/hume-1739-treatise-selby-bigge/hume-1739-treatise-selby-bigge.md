## Source scholarship

Book I, Part IV, Section VI ("Of personal identity", beginning SB 251) opens against "some philosophers" who claim an intimate, demonstrative consciousness of a simple and identical self. Hume's test is his own copy principle: every real idea must come from some impression, and no impression is constant and invariable through a life, so there is no impression from which an idea of a simple, continuing self could derive. He then reports what introspection actually finds: always some particular perception, never a self apart from one. From this he concludes that the mind is "a bundle or collection of different perceptions" (SB 252), and devotes the remainder of the section to explaining why we nevertheless ascribe identity to it (resemblance, causation, the imagination's easy passage).

The Appendix (1740) qualifies the result. Hume says that on review of this section he finds himself in "a labyrinth" (SB 633). He restates the arguments that deny a simple self, then admits that his account of what binds the perceptions together is "very defective" (SB 635), because two principles he cannot give up cannot be reconciled: that all distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences (SB 636). He leaves the problem open rather than withdrawing the finding.

The essay pairs this house with the Descartes house (`descartes-1641-meditations`), whose orientation already places Hume at the same position as Descartes, fixed on the other side of the crossed zero. This house supplies the textual site of that pairing. The essay's reading of the site is its own argued operation; nothing here attributes QL, the crossed zero, or any doctrine of position to Hume.

## Passage card — `hume-1739-treatise-selby-bigge-q001` — never without a perception
^hume-1739-treatise-selby-bigge-q001

> For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call *myself*, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch *myself* at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception.

## Passage card — `hume-1739-treatise-selby-bigge-q002` — the labyrinth (Appendix)
^hume-1739-treatise-selby-bigge-q002

> But upon a more strict review of the section concerning *personal identity*, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent.

- **Status:** quotation-ready.
- **Verification:** transcribed from the page image of SB 633 in the 1888 Clarendon printing (Internet Archive `atreatisehumann00selbgoog`, image n665); *personal identity* italic in the printing; "involv'd" as printed. Verifier: agent, 2026-10-05.
- **Source relation:** Extracted.
- **Evidential action:** qualifies q001 (Hume himself finds his account of personal identity unsatisfactory).
- **Consumers:** §4 · #3 (reworked draft, 2026-10-05).
- **Use boundary:** Hume does not retract the finding of q001. In the Appendix he restates that he never perceives a self without perceptions (SB 634) and locates the failure in his account of what connects the perceptions. Do not report the Appendix as a recantation of the bundle view.

## Passage card — `hume-1739-treatise-selby-bigge-q003` — two principles he cannot render consistent (Appendix)
^hume-1739-treatise-selby-bigge-q003

> In short there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent; nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz. *that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences*, and *that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences*.

> For my part, I must plead the privilege of a sceptic, and confess, that this difficulty is too hard for my understanding.

- **Locator:** Appendix, SB 636 (the paragraph naming the defect, "I am sensible, that my account is very defective", is on SB 635).
