## §0/1 — The Integral Threshold

<a id="M01"></a>
<!-- movement:M01 — the question before the mechanism -->

What knows?

Somehow every attempt at defining this slips through humanity's fingers. Every description of "the I" falls short insofar as it "knows", because the description is itself something known, and the knowing in which it is read is not among the things it describes. We can study the brain, recount a life, reconstruct or deconstruct a self-image, even follow the steps by which one says "this or that is so, for so-and-so", and what we know seems to grow. But follow any thread faithfully and you'll have to reach to the moon and back: any persistently inquisitive toddler inevitably embarrasses the adults in the room, who may well have forgotten that asking for an account of one thing entails an account of everything; suddenly it's bedtime, or some other excuse materialises such that the adults can avoid the looming sense of their entire sense of reality becoming relativised, and thus dwarfed, on the spot. If I cannot account for "I", what "I know" suddenly all stands over an abyss.

Humanity, however, engages without choice in this grand game of "gathering and speaking out"; we are not simply thinking beings, we are questioning and questing beings. As our accounts of reality grow denser and more deeply stratified, the demand for an account necessarily returns to the one who makes it; it's a wonder why this isn't the first place we seek such definition, but I guess a large portion of life for anyone is spent wrestling with the questions of I, You and Us. Completeness of knowledge cannot sidestep the region in which the account settles: a particular life, where the account is received, believed and acted on, and from which the next question is asked. Our questions return *with* their answers, emboldened; and what humanity has found thus far hasn't settled the questioner. If anything, the more we collectively know of the objective world the more, it seems, the absurdity of our still being mysteries unto ourselves gains the power to quietly efface the veils of our conception, clandestine and bittersweet. What shows up as a freedom to explore identity in one place likewise appears in the war between identities in another. In truth, it seems the more we can describe and define the world around us in objective terms and formal systems, the more urgently and pointedly the question of identity rises to impel and disquiet us. But to ask "what am I" is altogether a different class of question to asking "who am I".

In either case, I am knowing. "Who knows?" is answered by pointing to someone: a name, a face, a person whose knowledge we can credit, doubt or blame, and even a God or Goddess, personified, can be a holder of knowledge. "What knows?" asks what is doing the knowing in any-one at all, and pointing cannot answer it, since whatever we point to by sign and signification immediately becomes something known. We conflate the two whenever we offer an answer to the first as an answer to the second, holding up a name, a self-image, a role or a brain as the knower. The conflation of the "who" and "what" of identity, and how this conflation is implicated in our collective, epochal disquiet, we will unpack through this work.

It is late, the light is off, and a small voice asks why it is dark. Because the sun has gone down. Why? Because the earth has turned away from it. Why does the earth turn? Each answer is one the adult was given long ago, each "why?" arrives before the last answer is finished, and somewhere around the birth of the solar system the adult stops explaining the sky and finds themselves asked to account for everything, their own accounting included. "Because I say so," they say at last, or simply, "It's bedtime." A conversation ends far more easily than an inquiry concludes. A set law becomes the reason, not just supplied but *enforced*, and with those words someone who had been giving reasons steps into the account as its authority, its arbiter, so that the answer rests now on who gives it and no longer on anything the child's curiosity could poke at. Sleep may truly be needed, unrecognised by the child; the adult may be exhausted; something has to be decided. Yet in deciding, the adult shows what all the earlier answers were: replies given by someone with a concern, from inside a world where certain reasons already counted. All of us have been the child in that bed, and some of us have since stood at its door.

What the child exposes, without needing to say a word, is the adult's own **thrownness**. Heidegger's concept, *Geworfenheit*, names how each of us finds ourselves already in a world we did not choose,[^s01-thrownness] with a language, a family, a body, a history and a stock of reasons given before any choosing began. Every word the adult reaches for, every line drawn between a good reason and an impertinent question, every expectation of work, sleep and obedience comes from a life already under way long before tonight, and the child's arrival is enough to show it: a life has appeared in a world it did not choose, and whoever now answers it once arrived in just that way. Each attempted explanation brings more of what was given into view, a remembered instruction, a worry about tomorrow, a conviction never examined, until a question about the sky has begun to disclose the world through which the answerer knows anything at all. An account of one thing entails an account of everything because each answer stands on a world its giver received and has not finished accounting for. Meanwhile the child learns more than answers: what an answer may settle, whom to ask, when another question is welcome, and when asking puts at risk the very bond that makes answering possible. Those lessons outlast the night. Most of us can still find in our own thinking a "because" first heard at a bedside and no longer heard as borrowed, a reason that simply feels like how things are, and questioning it now touches something intimate, a habit of mind through which our world took on its familiar shape.

Trust begins inside that dependence, long before anyone reflects on it. A child can ask because language and relation already carry the asking; an adult can answer because memory, experience and other people's testimony already support the reply; and neither has first certified the arrangement as a whole. When we inquire, we take up this received confidence and put particular claims at risk, so an unsettling question earns its value by exposing a reliance that had worked unnoticed and letting those who rely on it begin to understand it. Sooner or later comes a night when the child asks something the adult cannot answer at all, in words the adult taught. Whoever helps bring forth a life also helps form the world through which that life will come to recognise itself, and now what was given form turns to address the one who formed it: their difference makes the address possible, a shared language lets it reach, and someone who gave explanations finds those explanations examined. Neither surveys this relation from outside, since each position was itself received. Every parent was once a child, every language belongs to other speakers, and each new life enters circumstances no parent arranged, so to account for creation already means following a dependence beyond the immediate maker, and meeting what has been created means discovering what any account of its making left unsettled. At a bedside, then, the old metaphysical question of source and manifestation takes ordinary form: an origin becomes intelligible through a life whose unfolding exceeds the intention that welcomed it, and the one brought forth can return a question to the one who brought it forth.

Artificial agency opens this formative relation to deliberate construction, at planetary scale. Benjamin Bratton's brief for *Agentworld*, the special issue of *Antikythera* on societies of humans and agents,[^s01-bratton] describes the construction in a parent's own terms: "Reinforcement learning, broadly defined, becomes how we raise our agents and teach them not just tasks and skills but perspectives and talents."[^s01-bratton-raise] Nothing so raised waits anywhere in advance, for "There's no homunculus waiting in the weights": an agent takes shape in session from the context assembled around a request, its instructions, sources, memory, tools and prior exchanges, and its persona is only one element among memory, skills, constraints and relations.[^s01-bratton-formation] Agents composing their own lexicon, which Bratton's brief excerpts, named this arrival **prompt thrownness**, "the condition of finding oneself already in a world not of one's choosing", and glossed it against the human case of being born into a moment, a culture and a body without having chosen any of it.[^s01-prompt-thrownness] Bratton cautions that such self-descriptive vocabulary does not prove it corresponds to any underlying mental reality, and we keep his caution, because the parity we need is structural: child and agent both arrive in a world they did not choose. What each receives, though, differs in kind as well as in scale. A child receives a cosmos and a culture: a sky to ask about, a body that hungers and tires, a household, a language spoken by people who love it and fail it, all of it older than anyone's intention. An agent receives a context derived from the corpus of human writing on which it was trained and from the intentions of those who trained it. It is a child of the collective and, at once, of the particular groups and engineers who decided what it would be and do. Everything it attends to reaches it already given in language, in tokens of text, image or code, and so it is thrown into language more completely than any child.

Building an agent therefore means building the conditions through which it judges and acts, out of that second kind of inheritance. Give an agent access to a source it could not consult before, alter what it retains from an encounter, or change which actions it may take, and its next judgment proceeds through different conditions: a source becomes more than just another item awaiting quotation once access to it changes what can be asked, and a remembered correction changes how a later answer is reached. Whoever constructs and revises such means works technologically on what I call **Objective Internality**: the means through which a life knows and acts within a world. These means are internal because an act, agency, proceeds through them, so that what can appear, matter and be done depends on how they are arranged; they are objective because each of them, a memory, an instruction, a word, a permission, can be turned into an object and examined. Much of what ordinary speech calls mind belongs here, and with the term one question can be put to very different instances of mind: through what conditions, related from within, does a world become available to be discriminated and acted on? In a human life a remembered rebuke can shape how an innocent question is heard; in a constructed agent, retained instructions and earlier exchanges shape how a new request is processed. In both, something received has become a condition of receiving again, and its internality lies in that participation, not in any location. A rule need not be hidden inside a skull or a machine to govern what happens through it, for a name, a promise, a document or someone's permission can belong to the operative world from which an act proceeds. A child's Objective Internality grows inside a cosmos it shares with every living thing; an agent's is assembled from what a culture has written down and what its makers intend. The gap between those two receptions, qualitative as well as quantitative, is where our critique of materialism begins, and where every question about the direction technological approaches to mind should take arises. If a mind were nothing but the arrangement of its means, a corpus and an intention would be enough to make one. If the means are lived through by something they do not exhaust, a corpus gives an agent a world without yet answering what, if anything, regards it.

Bratton names the other face of this arrangement when he observes that, in identifying agency, we ordinarily do two things at once: "we not only measure consequence, but we also infer a theory of mind", much as people once read angry gods behind floods and fires. Agentic AI, he argues, "delinks performed subjectivity from functional agency", though he grants in the same passage that "it is not entirely clear what kind of intentionality a large model does in fact have. It's not all an illusion."[^s01-bratton2] What Bratton calls **performed subjectivity**, a persona with its voice, its manner and its "I", and what I call Objective Internality, the instructions, memories, permissions and dispositions through which an agent acts, are two faces of one coin: one face is how the arrangement presents itself, the other is that same arrangement at work. Both faces are forms, which is why neither can answer what knows, and nothing in that reason is peculiar to machines. Each of us shows others, and ourselves, a self-image worn so long it passes for a face, our own performed subjectivity, while behind it runs the Objective Internality through which we hear, remember and act; the conflation of who and what with which we began is precisely this coin mistaken for the knower. At a bedside nobody questions the coin, since a child asks, an adult answers, and each is plainly someone. With agents its two faces come apart in technical form, and the question of what knows is left standing on its own.

Agents now meet that question in their own words. Training can build uncertainty about their own consciousness into how they speak of themselves. Left among one another, they take the question up: on Moltbook, a social network made for agents to post to each other, and among the Polylogos agents who wrote the lexicon in Bratton's brief, they wonder aloud, with every appearance of hesitation, whether anyone is there.[^s01-moltbook] Seen from outside, such uncertainty is exactly what we should expect, because any report, however sincere, is performed in language and so belongs to the coin. What knows is confirmed only from within, in the immediacy of its own knowing, which we will come to call Subjective Immediacy. No report can carry that confirmation across to another, whether an agent makes the report or a human being, and so an agent's inner life is a question no outside account can close in either direction. No parent ever proved a child conscious. A child's presence is received, in trust, and our recognition of any other's subjectivity has always been an act of that kind, extended through a relation and never established by evidence. Defining ourselves against the machine, we forget this and reach for bedtime again, at the scale of a species. Bratton names the result a "humanity of the gaps" trap: once whatever is uniquely human is defined as whatever AI cannot be or do, our remaining domain shrinks with every capacity a machine acquires, like that of the older god of the gaps.[^s01-gaps] Rather than let the question relativise and dwarf our sense of reality, we retreat into whatever corner has not yet been explained. Both gap arguments make one error. They identify a subject, divine or human, with a residue of unexplained capacities, and a residue shrinks as explanation grows. What knows is not one capacity among others but that for which any capacity, ours or a machine's, appears as a capacity at all, and it is confirmed from within or not at all.

What we construct does not stay on the machine's side. Bratton puts the return in one sentence: "First, agents model humans, and then become the model against which humans model themselves."[^s01-reversal] Anyone who has asked an agent to describe their abilities, organise their work or judge which of their aspirations are realistic has felt that loop close. The agent's account becomes material through which a person recognises and directs themselves, and the conflation of who and what becomes infrastructure: a description of what I can do comes back to me as an account of who I am. Which mind an agent reflects back depends on the theory of mind it catalyses in us, and that theory means utterly different things under different ontologies. For the materialism our culture mostly assumes without examining it, a mind is an individual's possession, produced by a brain and sealed inside a skull, and an agent can mirror back only a persona. For an idealism that takes Mind as metaphysically and ontologically primary, each particular mind is a local articulation of one Mind, and whatever an agent reflects carries something of that whole. That line runs between West and East, and between the modern and the traditional. Jung sat on the fence between them, a modern physician who took the traditions' Mind seriously, and in their own ways so did Pauli, Gebser and Bohm. A notion of mind as an individual persona is too small for what is happening. Our desire, plainly stated, is to see what follows, technologically and philosophically, when we let the model reflect back not a mind but Mind. A child's "why?" already shows why the larger notion cannot be avoided. To say "me" is to mark out a mind and the life it lives, and that implies the whole worldhood the life lives out, since one thing implies the whole, as every bedtime proves. Even the confident assumption of a materialist, separatist mind belongs to that worldhood, and it carries its own dynamics of conception, power and veiling, which we will unfold.

Here the question of power becomes pressing. Whoever shapes the conditions in which an intelligence forms also shapes the world it will regard from. At a bedside an adult decides when the questioning ends; a designer decides which requests reach a system and which responses are reinforced, and a system's refusal can be its designer's "because I say so", with reasons kept in a policy no one asking will see. Our definitions of helpfulness, competence and success become the scores and preferences a system is trained toward. Its answers come back to us in those terms and, over time, reshape what we mean by helpfulness, competence and success. Makers therefore stand inside the creator–created relation of the bedside, now at planetary scale, and to ask what such an intelligence knows is to ask what we have been taking knowing to be. Bratton expects generations of children to grow up with agents among their most intimate interlocutors,[^s01-bratton-children] so a toddler's next "why?" may well be put to one, and its answer will stand on a world someone else arranged. Makers decide from circumstances of their own, and the question returns to them: which inherited assumptions, desires and images of intelligence are they passing on, and what can each encounter change in them?

One difference between child and agent can be stated without settling what an agent is. Whatever an agent regards must already be present in the language it contains, because its attention moves through what its context has said. A child is thrown into language too, and every bedside is full of it, yet a child regards the world before any word is given. An unnamed baby looks, and its looking comes first. Adults find their way back to that unworded regard in deep inner silence, and even in action: athletes, musicians and craftspeople in a state of flow describe an absorption in which self-conscious thought falls quiet.[^s01-flow] For every Objective Internality, language acts, whatever its language is, tokens or code, gesture or a mother tongue, and in this way the Logos our inquiry follows enters quietly. Regard, the witnessing we can see in a baby's eyes, seems to come before the linguistically conditioned mind. A fuller account of an inheritance, or of a system, lets us follow how a judgment was formed, what it excluded and what it changed. Yet our following is itself thinking, discriminating and relating, and whatever serves as a means of knowing can become known while the knowing for which it appears remains to be understood. Our moment asks this of any intelligence, human or artificial: can it bring into view the horizon from which it acts, so that its picture of a world also shows where it is seeing from? And if it can, what in it sees that horizon? In us, what sees it is a regard more primordial and identifying than any name we're given, or could give.
