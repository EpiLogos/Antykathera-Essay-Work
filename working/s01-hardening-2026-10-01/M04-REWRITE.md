Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell spent the best part of a decade trying to make the grounds of mathematical knowledge as explicit as the proofs built upon them. Their *Principia Mathematica*, published in three volumes between 1910 and 1913, set out to derive mathematics from logic: to define its basic concepts, state its starting assumptions, and show step by step how every conclusion followed.[^s01-principia] A proof shows that a conclusion follows from its premises and leaves open why the premises deserve acceptance, and Whitehead and Russell pursued that question down into the foundations, where the most familiar operations rested on ideas that familiarity alone had made seem secure. When the givens of a given field come into the light of questioning, trusted assumptions risk thawing under scrutiny; what is taken as unquestionably true, when questioned, threatens to betray the irrational "we just accept these bases" underneath all rational constructions, the "miracles and coincidences" that so often become obfuscated by earned, yet fabricated, certainties.

For Russell the undertaking answered a need he later described quite personally: "I wanted certainty in the kind of way in which people want religious faith."[^s01-russell-faith] Mathematics promised that certainty because its demonstrations offered reasons anyone could examine, where other assurances had to be taken on somebody's authority; yet the demonstrations his teachers expected him to accept turned out to be full of errors, and if certainty was to be found in mathematics at all, its foundations would have to be rebuilt. In the terms of the previous movement, Russell asked knowledge's aperture to do the work of faith: an account was to become so complete that the dependence beneath it would never again have to be lived through. He chose mathematics because an argument there was supposed to hold whoever wanted its conclusion, free of desire, authority and comfort, and it was there, in the discipline he had chosen for its freedom from uncertainty, that the paradox found him.

The paradox lives in the most innocent step of counting. Before we count anything we gather it: we pick out some things, treat them together as one collection, and say how many belong. Gottlob Frege had made that step the foundation of arithmetic. His *Basic Laws of Arithmetic* rested on a principle that seemed too obvious to need defending, that any condition we can state clearly gathers a collection, namely everything that meets it.[^s01-frege] Name a condition, being an even number or a figure with three sides or a collection with exactly two members, and its collection exists. In June 1902 Russell wrote to Frege about one condition. Picture a library whose catalogues list its books, and which also keeps catalogues of its catalogues. Some catalogues list themselves and most do not, so a librarian draws up the catalogue of every catalogue that does not list itself. Should it list itself? If it does, it is a catalogue that lists itself and has no place on its own list; if it does not, it is exactly the kind of catalogue it was made to list. Each answer requires the other. Frege replied within the week that the discovery had shaken the ground on which he had meant to build arithmetic, and he added an appendix to the second volume of the *Basic Laws*, then in press, conceding that a foundation of his edifice had given way just as the work was finished.

Russell's own construction is the same catalogue written as a collection. Once collections can themselves be collected, we can ask of any collection whether it belongs to itself: the collection of all collections with more than two members has more than two members, and so belongs to itself, while the collection of all cats is not a cat. Gather every collection that does *not* belong to itself, and call the result $R$. Admit $R$ to itself, and it becomes a collection that belongs to itself, which the rule for $R$ excludes; leave it out, and it is a collection that does not belong to itself, which is exactly what the rule admits. Where $\in$ means "is a member of", the two answers lock together:

$$R\in R \iff R\notin R.$$

Each side entails the other while denying it. Nothing in the reasoning was careless. We applied a clearly stated condition, exactly as Frege's principle allows, and the condition produced a collection that cannot consistently be said either to belong to itself or not to. What failed was the principle: the assumption that whatever condition we can state gathers something we can hold as one.

Name and count, which we followed with Kripke, meet here before any number of members is calculated. Calling $R$ a collection assigns it a kind, and naming it lets us refer to the one collection the description proposes; the paradox makes that proposal answer for itself, asking whether we have specified something that can be held consistently as this one collection, or produced a description whose conditions defeat the object it appears to name. Kripke's work shows why the question reaches beyond formal mathematics, or rather why mathematics is implicated *before* the language that adorns it, a communion of number and word we will need again when we approach Jung and Pauli's notion of the *psychoid*. A name could go on concerning the same child while a description of that child proved mistaken, because the child remained there for a corrected account. $R$ has no bearer standing apart from its description: the membership condition is all we have said about what $R$ is, and applying it makes inclusion require exclusion and exclusion require inclusion. The question beneath naming, "what has been identified as this one?", has become unavoidable inside the attempt to found counting itself, and it is our opening question, *what knows*, asked now of a collection: the referent that "Subject" and "God" both sign was identified without ever being identifiable as one thing, and $R$ meets that predicament in formal terms, as a "this one" whose identification defeats itself. Moreover, we find ourselves faced with the *intimacy* of count and what names it, in that the name and the account seek a clear *belonging*; *inclusion qua exclusion* and *exclusion qua inclusion*, one could say, are the essential conditions of the question of belonging, and the formal revelation of this dynamic, with the impossibility it bears, shows that on the question of its own belonging a formal system attains to *intimation* of truth, never to its proof.

A paradox at the foundations reaches further than an error in something calculated from them, because it touches the means by which a mathematician tells sound reasoning from error. The instruments of repair are implicated in what has failed, and one has to go on thinking while deciding which trusted ways of thinking can survive the encounter; Frege, who had staked his arithmetic on the principle, found no repair that worked, and the *Basic Laws* stayed at two volumes. Whitehead and Russell could not set the paradox aside as an unlucky exception either, since the principle that produced $R$ would remain ready to produce it again, so their repair had to reach the conditions under which a collection may be formed at all. We pass easily from describing which things we mean to treating them as one collection, because understanding what a description asks us to include feels like holding the whole it describes. Russell's paradox exposes what that ease conceals: once the proposed whole is submitted to its own condition of belonging, understanding the instruction no longer assures us that it can be fulfilled, and in this exact sense the construction is self-effacing.

Russell's answer, in the form he published in 1908 and built into *Principia*, was the theory of types.[^s01-russell] It sorts whatever can be collected into levels, individuals at the first, collections of individuals at the next, collections of those collections above that, and it allows a collection to take its members only from the level below its own. A collection of people and a collection of collections of people therefore stand at different levels, and a group counted as one member of the higher collection does not thereby become another person. Under that rule "does $R$ belong to itself?" cannot even be asked, because a collection and its members never stand at the same level, and Russell stated the principle behind the restriction plainly: "No totality can contain members defined in terms of itself." The type restrictions do inside the notation what circumstance did for names. Before asking whether something belongs to a collection, we must establish what kind of thing it is and what kind of members the collection admits, so the notation carries, beside each name, the logical kind of what is named and the relations it may enter. Whitehead and Russell explain in their preface that their symbolism was developed in the course of the work, and that much of it had to be new because they were dealing with ideas not previously symbolised,[^s01-principia] and in answering the paradox they made language able to carry a distinction faithfully enough for a later reader to see why an expression was admissible before trusting it. Indeed, the present work and the neo-logical notations it bears emerge from a similar encounter and necessity.

Read through our notation, membership and non-membership are the two poles of one axis, dia-ballein `(−1)/(+1)`, and in Russell's collection each answer turns into its opposite, so the reasoning swings without rest. The swing is the polarity still alive, the oscillation of a held duality; the trouble lies in the quick exits from it, which are the axis's collapses. One can decree an answer, and the decree comes in both chiralities: declare that $R$ belongs and the whole span is handed to membership, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`; declare that it does not and the span goes to non-membership, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`. Either way the oscillation hardens into an opposition, one answer standing against the other with the whole span on its side. Or one can cancel, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, dismissing the question as nonsense and leaving Frege's principle free to produce it again. The theory of types shows dia-ballein put to its proper use: Russell made a cut and stated it, naming the levels he divided and his reason for dividing them, so that reasoning could go on with an explicit account of why one act of collecting had failed. And we who followed the paradox did something else again. We kept both answers in view and traced what each required, until their conflict stopped looking like a failure to choose and became evidence about the principle that had produced it. The answers stayed opposed, and their opposition showed us where they belonged together, in the rule that generated both. That is syn-ballein, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, as a reader performs it: two determinations are held in relation through their difference until the difference discloses their common source.

Arriving at the theory of types took prolonged trial: proposed solutions occupied Whitehead and Russell for months before they could establish that a solution failed, and even after settling on types they allowed that alternatives might preserve much of their work. They were frank, too, about how they judged their foundations: the chief reason for accepting their premises was inductive, lying in the premises' power to yield the mathematics already accepted as true.[^s01-principia] The finished book sets the premises first and deduces everything from them, while its authors had come to trust those premises partly by working back from the mathematics they wanted to explain, and a student inherits the deductive order without the trust that built it. Given the definitions and rules, the student learns to use them and can become highly capable without retracing the years of dispute, which is one of formalisation's real gifts; the same gift lets the rules seem the only possible beginning, so that the circumstances in which the system itself might need reconsidering fall outside what the student has learned to ask. Along this route the *con* survives a genuine correction. People rightly trust a method because it has let them establish something, extend that trust into the belief that its present terms can decide everything worth asking, and then meet every objection to those terms with another application of them. Knowledge stops helping us bear uncertainty and begins deciding in advance what may be allowed to unsettle it. The parent and the governing office return here within the inheritance of knowledge: an authority earned through particular achievements governs the admission of whatever could unsettle it, and the limit the paradox disclosed is remembered as a lesson about a former mistake while the dependence it exposed is forgotten.

The paradox forced a decision about where the fault lay, and Russell and Whitehead decided differently, so that their two directions are two answers to that one question. Russell placed the fault in the expressions. Language had let a malformed question through, so its terms, implications and conditions of application had to be analysed and its permissions restricted, and the theory of types gave that analysis its constructive form. The choice fitted what he wanted, for if certainty could be had at all, it would lie in a cleaner instrument, and he put his trust in the tool. After some twenty years of strenuous work he concluded that he could do no more to make mathematical knowledge indubitable,[^s01-russell-faith] and he had to separate the value of reasoning from the absolute reassurance he had wanted reasoning to supply. Whitehead placed the fault in the picture of reality the logic presupposed: a world of finished things, each simply located and complete in itself, waiting to be collected. In *Science and the Modern World* he named that picture's root error the **fallacy of misplaced concreteness**, the mistake of taking an abstraction for the concrete,[^s01-whitehead-concreteness] which is the error I pinpointed at the outset in thought's tendency to reify what is quite viscerally and immediately a processual reality. If the fault lies there, logic needs more than better permissions. It needs an account of how anything becomes definite enough to be collected, and Whitehead left logic for metaphysics to give one, putting his trust in the encounter itself, in the confidence that a breakdown could disclose a more adequate understanding of the world in which the instrument works.

In the same book Whitehead asks in what spirit we should meet such a clash, and he answers: "In formal logic, a contradiction is the signal of a defeat: but in the evolution of real knowledge it marks the first step in progress towards a victory."[^s01-whitehead-smw] The first judgment concerns whether an argument can stand as written; the second concerns what someone learns by investigating why it cannot. Late in life he put the conviction more sharply still: "There are no whole truths; all truths are half-truths. It is trying to treat them as whole truths that plays the devil."[^s01-whitehead-halftruths] In our notation that is the devil precisely, the literalist who takes one pole of a relation for the whole span, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, or from the other side `(−1)−(+1)=−2`. Jung reached the same conviction from the side of the psyche. "Oddly enough the paradox is one of our most valuable spiritual possessions, while uniformity of meaning is a sign of weakness," he wrote, because "only the paradox comes anywhere near to comprehending the fulness of life. Non-ambiguity and non-contradiction are one-sided and thus unsuited to express the incomprehensible."[^s01-jung-paradox] His witness was Tertullian, who had the strength to sustain the paradoxes of faith, and for whom, Jung observes, they "actually afforded him the highest degree of religious certainty". Russell looked for a certainty like faith in a system purged of contradiction; Tertullian found his in a relation he could bear through it. That bearing is the religious relation in its honest form, and it is the only honest relation to the condition `0/1` writes. The primordial distinction between what can be distinguished, defined and determined and what cannot is the condition of rational certainty itself: every certainty Russell sought stood upon it, and the paradox he stumbled over was that condition showing through the system built on top of it.

Whitehead also saw why such an encounter is resisted, asking whether a doctrine that has become a foundation of social order may justify suppressing the observations that contradict it and showing how easily an intelligible and useful scheme is protected by disregarding the evidence it cannot accommodate.[^s01-whitehead-smw] We must act before every uncertainty is settled, and so we necessarily rely on ideas whose adequacy remains partial: we literally trust-through their partiality. How often, how easily, does such reliance inspire us to let the ideas change as experience exceeds them? Here the response to paradox meets the trust relation directly, for what once helped us understand can go on serving a life and its growth, or become the form that the life must slavishly be made to preserve. Just as Jung observed that the eternal images of Christianity, worn smooth by usage, can stiffen into mere objects of belief, fossilised systems can fast become a seal on the tomb of genuine encounter and inquiry.[^s01-jung-fossil] It's in this way that what counts as accounted-for can become the condition, the reason, for not actually encountering what's been couched in the summation.

Whitehead's question is how anything becomes a definite actuality at all. A logical account needs terms settled enough to enter its relations, while a world of becoming keeps producing the very determinations the account receives as given; in *Process and Reality* he returns to Spinoza's attempt to understand the many through one substance and asks how the many acquire actual existence within that unity.[^s01-whitehead-pr] Assigning each thing its place beneath the name of the whole cannot answer him, because we would still have to understand how the whole differentiates, how its differences become real, and how their belonging to one reality stays effective through the differentiation. Our own definition met the same demand from the other side, turning through its assertions and their inverses before the act of defining became intelligible to itself, and we wrote `0/1` to hold what it found. The `1` is what can be distinguished and worked with; the slash stands for the activity through which it becomes available; the `0` marks the unobjectifiable condition implicated in that activity. Treat definite things as complete in themselves, and their relations must be added to them afterward, which is the picture Whitehead diagnosed. Begin from the relation through which a thing becomes definite, and its identity already carries a history of participation, which is why discovering a form's limits can teach us how it came to be.

Whitehead's concept for that becoming is **concrescence**, from *con-*, together, and *crescere*, to grow; *concrete* belongs to the same family and once meant what has grown together, so the word lets us hear a history inside what ordinarily seems solid and self-contained. An actual occasion, his name for the unit of the real, becomes what it is by taking up the conditions available to it and integrating them into one particular experience. His concept of **prehension** names that taking-account-of: something previously actual, or a possibility of definiteness, enters how the new occasion forms, and what is received matters through the manner of its reception. The occasion's unity lies in how its many contributions are related, and they stay distinguishable within what they helped produce, so that his formula can give the integration and its consequence at once: "The many become one, and are increased by one."[^s01-whitehead-pr] What grows is the many: each new occasion is one more, while the One that `0` writes is neither added to nor diminished, the uncarved block staying one while every carving joins the many. **Creativity**, in his vocabulary, is the ultimate activity through which each new togetherness becomes actual, and it has no character apart from the occasions that embody it, which turns the old opposition of formless and form into a question of how a form arises. When an occasion has become fully definite, Whitehead calls its achievement **satisfaction**, from *satis* and *facere*, to make enough, and his paired concept of **subject-superject** names its two offices: the occasion is the subject of its own becoming and, once achieved, a superject that other occasions can receive, passing into what he calls **objective immortality** as a condition of further becoming. A judgment has an everyday version of this afterlife: once I have made it, I can remember it, quote it, act on it, or find that someone has organised part of their life around it, and if I later change my mind, the earlier judgment has already entered the circumstances in which the change occurs.

We have just undergone a small concrescence of our own. The librarian's catalogue at first seemed clear enough to compile; then its two answers became incompatible demands; and understanding the paradox meant gathering the instruction, the attempted answers and their contradiction into one account in which Frege's principle stood exposed. Many steps became one understood construction, and that understanding is now a datum from which our next act of reading proceeds. We therefore propose the `4+2` as a logic of **epistemic concrescence**. Our definition of the Subject passed through four determinate positions, which recognition gathered as one performed inquiry, and its return through the implicate condition let the achieved understanding enter another act; written `0/1→4+2`, the relation unfolds into an account that includes the differences through which it became intelligible. Whatever we identify along the way belongs to the articulated `1`, yet its appearing and its relations keep returning us toward the `0` it cannot present as another object, and the slash holds that return in the inscription. In the sixfold the process takes an inspectable form, and `5→0`, the Möbius return, marks the turn by which its completion becomes a beginning again. An answer reaches satisfaction when it is definite enough to contribute to further inquiry; its claim to be the last possible answer is a separate demand, and one that would arrest the process that made it knowable.

Whitehead's distinction between an occasion's formation and its reception by later occasions brings time into the account, since something becomes one and its achievement then enters a succession. Spencer-Brown approached the same question from the side the theory of types had closed. Where types forbid a form to re-enter itself, his *Laws of Form* lets a distinction re-enter the space it distinguishes and follows the consequence: an expression that contains itself has no stable value, it alternates between the marked and the unmarked state, and Spencer-Brown treats that alternation as the point at which time enters the calculus, the form's own way of holding what no single value can.[^s01-spencerbrown] That alternation is the clean oscillation of a held polarity, the healthy `(−1)/(+1)` given time to move in, where the decrees of Russell's swing had frozen it at `+2` or `−2`. His re-entering mark writes, in a calculus of indications, the turn our notation writes as `5→0`, the completed form returning into the space of its own drawing. Our `0/1` begins to articulate the logically prior, non-dual foundation of that paradoxical return: a reality that is its own act of self-identification.

Kashmir Śaivism thinks this pulse through in the Spanda school, named for the *Spandakārikā*, the ninth-century *Stanzas on Vibration*, which teach that consciousness is never inert: its *spanda*, its vibration or pulse, is a "subtle movement" that goes nowhere and yet manifests as every motion, the oscillation between the unchanging and the changeful, what Whitehead may have termed the *primordial* and *consequent* natures of the divine.[^s01-dycz] Mark Dyczkowski, whose *Doctrine of Vibration* reconstructs the school's teaching from the Stanzas and their commentators Kallaṭa and Kṣemarāja, sets out the rhythm of a single act of that pulse through three powers of Śiva's consciousness: will (*icchā*), knowledge (*jñāna*) and action (*kriyā*). Everything comes into being through an act of will, with action as its immediate instrument and knowledge of how the will is to be applied mediating between the will to create and the act itself, so that the intent to make an object manifest, the act of manifesting and the manifest result are the beginning, middle and end of every experience.[^s01-dycz-three] Read through our notation, that act is a `0/1`. Will rises from consciousness at rest in itself, the `0` turning toward a determination; knowledge is the slash, the mediating awareness through which the will finds its application; action is the `1`, the object or deed completed. Dyczkowski adds the return: the first moment of the will's intent coincides with its final moment, when the desire to act has been satisfied and consciousness rests again in pure intent, so the completed act turns back into the source from which the next act rises, and that is our `5→0`. The comparison with concrescence holds at a technical level, for Whitehead's occasion also begins from an aim, integrates what it receives through its prehensions, and ends in a satisfaction that becomes a datum for what follows; Spanda places the whole rhythm in the knowing Subject, where, in Dyczkowski's words, "knowledge turns into action and action leads to knowledge".

Something also draws the inquiry onward before its answer is available. Russell's desire for certainty has already made that involvement explicit: the work mattered to him, and what he hoped to find helped determine what he was prepared to undertake. We call this participation of desire **the eros of Logos**. Jung gives the conjunction an exact psychological formulation in *Mysterium Coniunctionis*: "For purely psychological reasons I have, in other of my writings, tried to equate the masculine consciousness with the concept of Logos and the feminine with that of Eros. By Logos I meant discrimination, judgment, insight, and by Eros I meant the capacity to relate."[^s01-jung-logos-eros] The pair describes two operations which our own inquiry has repeatedly had to perform together. Logos cuts sufficiently cleanly for a difference to become intelligible; Eros keeps what has been distinguished in a relation through which the difference can matter. The syn-ballein of the symbolon already gave this relational work a material image: its halves have to remain distinct for their fitting to disclose their prior belonging. The eros of Logos is therefore the desire of articulation to recover and deepen the relations its own discriminations reveal. A question begins because something experienced calls for an account, sometimes as a clearly formulated problem, sometimes as the persistent feeling that an explanation has missed what matters, and in trying to answer we discover more of what we were asking. Whitehead's occasion likewise forms with an aim toward its own satisfaction, its inheritance entering a becoming directed toward a particular achievement, and Spanda's will is the same aim felt from within. In our epistemic use that aim stays open to change through what the inquiry meets; otherwise the satisfaction of having made an argument cohere becomes a reason to defend it against the experience it was meant to understand.

Lacan makes the relation between seeking and satisfaction more intricate still. His concept of the drive describes a circuit around an object the drive never attains, finding its satisfaction in completing the circuit, its *jouissance*,[^s01-lacan-drive] and that is how a person can stay attached to a search that keeps disappointing them, or to an answer whose defence keeps producing the conflict it promised to settle. Our question turns toward what is already present beneath the seeking. A person wants knowledge, and knowing is already occurring in the wanting, the questioning and the discovering; one answer fulfils one inquiry while the capacity through which it is enjoyed stays available for the next, so the eros of Logos can be sustained by discovery itself, without the promise that one final possession will end the need to understand. French keeps that pleasure inside its word for knowing. *Savoir*, to know, descends from Latin *sapere*, whose senses join tasting, discerning and being wise, and *savour* comes from its neighbour *sapor*, flavour.[^s01-savoir] We can know something by bringing it into an account, and we can savour the understanding as an event we take part in: the taste arrives when an argument finally becomes clear, when sentences held apart come together and we can feel why each belongs, and it accompanies this question, this evidence, this discovery without waiting for every question to be answered. The *con-* of concrescence returns us to the withness of *con-scire*: what grows together becomes available to a knowing that can recognise its own participation in the growth.

The Processual Lens of our Meta-Epistemic Framework (`L3`, developed in §4) follows this course through any inquiry: what it inherits, what it makes possible, how it integrates those possibilities, and what its completion gives to another act. Once an inquiry's achieved claims can themselves become objects of further claims, self-reference returns inside the growth of knowledge, and a proof is exactly such an achieved form, something that can be identified, examined and used in another proof. In his 1931 paper, "On Formally Undecidable Propositions of *Principia Mathematica* and Related Systems", Kurt Gödel turned Whitehead and Russell's achievement upon itself.[^s01-godel] They had made proof explicit enough for every step to be checked against stated rules, and Gödel used that explicitness to make proofs into objects of arithmetic. A written proof is a finite sequence of expressions, each a string of symbols in a definite order, so by assigning numerical codes to the symbols and their arrangements Gödel could identify a whole proof by a single number from which the proof itself can be recovered; arithmetic can then examine relations among these numbers, including whether one coded expression follows from others according to the rules. Gödel gave naming and counting an exact technique: a number names an expression, and calculation with such numbers settles whether the expressions they name constitute a proof.

For this to work, we must specify what counts as a proof. Let $T$ name the theory under consideration: its axioms state what it begins by accepting, and its rules of inference determine how further claims may be derived. We require these to be effectively specified, so that a definite procedure can check any proposed proof, and we require $T$ to contain enough arithmetic to represent the numerical operations involved. Given a candidate proof and a proposed conclusion, we can then ask whether the candidate really establishes the conclusion according to $T$, and once both have codes, the question becomes a relation between numbers. We write $\operatorname{Proof}_T(p,n)$ to mean that $p$ codes a proof, in $T$, of the sentence coded by $n$, the subscript naming the rules under which a sequence counts as a proof. Gödel then constructs a sentence that speaks about its own provability. Substituting a numeral into an expression can itself be represented arithmetically, and by arranging the substitution he produces a sentence whose claim concerns the numerical code of that very sentence. Call it $G_T$, and write $\ulcorner G_T\urcorner$ for its code. $T$ itself can establish the equivalence:

$$G_T \iff \neg\exists p\,\operatorname{Proof}_T\bigl(p,\ulcorner G_T\urcorner\bigr).$$

In words, the sentence says that *no number codes a proof, in $T$, of this very sentence*. The corner brackets let the sentence be referred to through its numerical identity, so what it says about proving it also says about numbers, and Gödel built the passage between these two readings into the construction. Self-reference has entered through arithmetic's capacity to describe operations on the signs in which arithmetic is written.

Suppose $T$ did prove $G_T$. There would then be a finite proof with a numerical code, and because $T$ can represent the checking of proofs, it could verify that code and prove that a proof of $G_T$ exists; yet $G_T$ says that no such proof exists, so $T$ would have proved a statement and its negation. **Consistency**, the property of never proving both a statement and its negation, excludes this, so a consistent $T$ cannot prove $G_T$, and under that assumption what $G_T$ says of the natural numbers is true: none of them codes a $T$-proof of it. We recognise the sentence's truth by reasoning about the theory, with the consistency on which that reasoning depends kept explicit, and the theory's inability to prove the sentence is precisely what the sentence truthfully describes. Could $T$ instead prove the negation, that some number does code a proof of $G_T$? Under the consistency assumption every particular number fails the test, and $T$ can check each failure, so proving the negation would mean denying of each number that it codes a proof while asserting that some number does. Gödel excludes this with a stronger condition he calls **ω-consistency**: a theory must not deny a property of each particular natural number and also assert that some natural number has it. Given that condition, $T$ proves neither $G_T$ nor its negation. The sentence is well formed, its meaning was built from the theory's own arithmetic, and both routes to deciding it by proof are closed under the stated assumptions; the limit becomes expressible because the theory has succeeded in representing so much of its own activity.

Russell's paradox had forced a change in the principle for forming collections, because the principle produced a contradiction. Gödel's result is of a different kind: the theory stays consistent and goes on establishing genuine truths, and still its resources cannot decide every arithmetical sentence it can formulate, so paradox returns through the success of self-reference as a separation between truth and provability within a specified theory. That puts the theory of types in its proper place, for clarifying which expressions may be formed protects reasoning from one contradiction and guarantees nothing about whether every meaningful question in the resulting theory can be settled by its proofs. Gödel's second theorem then reaches the confidence with which the theory's rules are used. Because proofs have codes, the claim that $T$ is consistent can be written in $T$'s own arithmetic, as the statement that no number codes a $T$-proof of a contradiction, and Gödel showed that for a theory strong enough to formalise the relevant reasoning, using the standard representation of its proofs, proving this consistency statement would let the theory prove its own Gödel sentence. A consistent theory cannot do that, so it cannot prove its own consistency either. Here the orientation we write `1/0` takes arithmetical form: asked to account for the condition it rests on, the determinate system can answer only with another determinate sentence in its own terms, and that sentence is precisely the one it cannot prove. Gödel does not prove our notation; within his stated hypotheses he gives the incomputable return of the `1` upon its ground a theorem's precision. Trust has become examinable at this point. Every use of the theory enacts a reliance that now appears inside the reasoning as a stated hypothesis, consistency, whose warrant the theory cannot supply, and the theory's achievements and the grounds on which we accept it have become distinguishable within the reasoning itself.

That warrant can be sought in a stronger theory, and the new work establishes results unavailable in the first, a genuine enlargement of knowledge: an independent sentence can be adopted as a new axiom, and further principles can support a proof of the earlier theory's consistency. If a stronger theory $T'$ proves $G_T$, it has not contradicted $G_T$, because that sentence concerns the absence of a proof in $T$, not in $T'$. Gödel's construction keeps in play the three relations our counting already required: the number that makes the sentence identifiable, the proof-relation that specifies how it could be established, and the theory that decides which sequences count as establishing it. Only by keeping all three explicit can we say how one sentence stays unprovable in $T$ while becoming provable in $T'$, and calling it simply "unprovable" would hide the theory relative to which that holds. Once we ask $T'$ to represent its own proving, a new sentence must refer to the enlarged proof-relation, and if $T'$ meets the same conditions, the incompleteness result applies to it in turn. No collection of further answers repairs this, because each answer belongs to a theory with particular axioms and rules, and every enlargement produces a new relation between what the theory can express and what it can establish. The gap between those capacities is an **immutable gap**: it relocates with every enlargement and returns through it, so it was never one withheld fact awaiting discovery.[^s01-gap] Theories change, and the recurrence does not. No consistent, effectively axiomatised theory of the requisite strength can provide the complete and internally certified account of arithmetic that the original desire for certainty required, and what Gödel makes untenable is the expectation that our proving, comparing and investigating must finally produce one theory whose own resources exhaust arithmetical truth and certify their own reliability.

Nicholas of Cusa, whose infinite sphere gave us the centre everywhere and the circumference nowhere, gave *On Learned Ignorance* a second figure for exactly this relation: "the intellect is to truth as [an inscribed] polygon is to [the inscribing] circle. The more angles the inscribed polygon has the more similar it is to the circle. However, even if the number of its angles is increased ad infinitum, the polygon never becomes equal [to the circle] unless it is resolved into an identity with the circle."[^s01-cusa-polygon] Each stronger theory adds sides, and the likeness genuinely grows, while equality never arrives by adding. Cusa names the only way it could arrive, a resolution into identity, which is a change in kind that no further side achieves; our square became a torus through the same kind of act, the identification of its edges, and no number of added sides would have made it one. Two limits are at work here, then, and they ground two different acts, which I hold together in four lines:

> **The approachable limit is the basis of investigation;  
> the absolute limit is the basis of faith;  
> the limit of investigation is the limit of determination;  
> the limit of faith is never to be determined.**[^s01-aphorism]

Gödel's sentence is an approachable limit: a stronger theory proves it, and investigation advances by approaching it. What reappears with every advance is an absolute limit, which no theory approaches, because each approach occurs through a further determination of the kind it would need to exceed. Investigation ends where determination ends, at the edge of the polygon it has drawn. Faith takes its basis from the circle the polygon never equals, and it is not belief in some proposition the theorem has failed to settle, since an unprovable sentence warrants no unrelated belief at all. Faith is the lucid continuation through that limit which every use of a theory already enacts, with its grounds, scope and conditions of revision retained, and the one thing it never does is turn the absolute limit into a final determination. Here the paradox of the two signs meets its formal limit: the referent of "Subject" and "God" is identified in every act of investigation and determined by none. Russell had asked investigation to supply the basis of faith. What he found, and what Gödel then made exact for arithmetic under stated hypotheses, is that such a theory cannot certify its own ground from its own resources: the two bases differ in kind, and a life of inquiry needs both.

Here the form has become articulate enough to name itself through a number and to make a claim about the possibility of its own proof, and `0/1` was introduced so that such an achieved form would never take the place of its own ground. Wittgenstein had met self-reference a decade earlier, in what a proposition must share with the world in order to represent it. A sentence can represent a possible arrangement of things because its signs have an arrangement of their own, and their relation shows what would be the case if the sentence were true; any explanation of that correspondence already uses the capacity it explains, so we cannot step outside every representation to inspect the relation between representation and reality from somewhere else. His distinction between **saying and showing** follows: a proposition exhibits through its articulation something it cannot add to its contents as another fact.[^s01-wittgenstein] The same pressure reaches the one for whom a representation is intelligible. "The subject does not belong to the world but it is a limit of the world," he writes, comparing it with the eye and its field of sight, where nothing in the field allows us to conclude that it is seen from an eye;[^s01-tractatus-subject] an image of the eye can appear among the things seen, and adding it to the picture does not supply the seeing through which the picture becomes visible. Every reader of the *Tractatus* has finally to undergo this distinction in relation to the book itself, whose propositions elucidate by being recognised as senseless, so that whoever understands them climbs out through them and throws away the ladder after climbing up it.

Our tetralemma reached the same predicament by a shorter route: the definition asserted something of the Subject, met its own contradiction, and exposed the act through which assertion and denial had become possible. Both inquiries ask for more than assent to a conclusion. A person can repeat that the Subject cannot be defined while continuing to identify with whichever image of themselves they happen to prefer, and recognition occurs only when the account stops concealing the activity in which it is being understood; a proposition has served its purpose when it lets us see why another proposition cannot do everything we were asking of it. Letting silence be means allowing that recognition to remain present before we turn it into another description of what we supposedly are. Heidegger's late concept for this comportment is *Gelassenheit*, releasement, an old word from the German mystics and Meister Eckhart that he turned to the attitude that says yes and no to technical things at once,[^s01-gelassenheit] and Taoist *wu wei* names action freed from compulsive imposition, a notion Jung lauded in his commentary on Richard Wilhelm's translation of *The Secret of the Golden Flower*.[^s01-golden-flower] Heidegger, Jung and the Asian traditions meet on one point: an openness beyond deliberate, manipulative mastery changes how we act, and more consequentially how we feel and know ourselves in the act. A genuinely global culture requires this reciprocity between ways of knowing, in which an encounter transforms the categories through which its participants recognise knowledge and also the quality of relation that genuine openness mints. Wisdom, Sophia, seems to be attracted by such withholding of the perfunctory and largely autonomous activity of thought's attempted grasp of the present circumstance, for openness is horizonal, the standing and circumference that constitutes a horizon as such.

The torus gives that void and silence a definite relation to the form surrounding it, whilst ensuring that the circumference bears the possibility, and drive, of uncollapsable circumambulation and circulation. On a sphere every closed loop can be shrunk continuously to a point without leaving the surface. On the torus, the two circuits we followed earlier, around the tube and around the central opening, cannot be shrunk away unless we leave the surface or change the space, and their resistance tells us something positive about the whole we traverse: the opening belongs to what makes the form a torus, and filling it would change the possible journeys. Lacan used these two circuits in his seminar on identification to separate the repeated circuits of demand around the body of the torus from the empty circle around its central hole, which stands for the object desire is organised around, since particular demands can be satisfied while that object stays out of reach.[^s01-lacan-identification] Our search for a final self-description runs the same circuit. Another achievement, another explanation, another person's recognition promises to settle who I am, and the next encounter finds me seeking again, because the Subject appears as a vacancy whenever I look for it among the forms through which I know myself. In `0/1` that apparent vacancy is already related to every such form as the knowing through which it appears, and the silence is felt as something missing only when a proposition is expected to occupy it.

What we seek in that vacancy is rarely an emotionally neutral definition. In Jung's concept, an **archetypal image** presents a relationship in a form through which it can be felt: the protecting or judging father, the abandoned child, the hero whose victory would establish his worth. Its force can become **numinous**, Jung's term, taken from Rudolf Otto, for a power that seizes consciousness beyond any deliberate act of choice, fascinating, commanding reverence or inspiring dread,[^s01-numinous] and a person is drawn toward such an image because something of their life seems to be at stake in its fulfilment. The image can then become the centre of a **complex**, Jung's concept for a feeling-toned organisation of memories, expectations, bodily responses and interpretations, through which a delayed reply, a passing remark and an old recollection are all experienced as the same rejection, or the same promise of recognition, before the person has decided what any one of them means. In the image that organisation becomes recognisable, and its authority extends through the ways it prepares a person to receive what happens next. The theory of complex systems has a concept for this kind of repetition, the **basin of attraction**, a region of states into which different starting points are all drawn, and John R. Van Eenwyk's reading of Jung through chaotic dynamics treats the complex as a self-similar organisation of associations and the symbol as what carries the psyche between the known and the unknown and back.[^s01-van-eenwyk] The complex gathers experience under a feeling-tone; a living symbol disturbs the categories through which the gathering occurs and lets a different organisation develop, which is why a symbolic event often unsettles first, changing the very terms under which the person would judge whether it is good for them. One more response inside an established pattern differs in kind from an encounter that changes the pattern itself.

The quilting point that held a household's idea of the good parent settles identities of every kind. To be "a real man", "one of the chosen", or simply "someone who knows" is to inhabit an arrangement of permissions and prohibitions, hopes and fears, and a challenge to one expression can unsettle the whole arrangement because the person's identity has been secured through it. The Name-of-the-Father binds that organisation to law and authorising speech; the *imago Dei*, the God-image, gives the need for ultimate authority a form in which it can be encountered. Their conjunction turns dangerous when the particular image is allowed to decide what its source must be. Then the believer's interpretation, the parent's command or the institution's judgment borrows the absoluteness of what it represents, and disagreement is received as a threat to the order of reality itself. Jung's account of the dying God-image concerns this more than any change of doctrine:[^s01-god-image] a life must discover whether it can relinquish the form in which it has located its guarantee, and the love invested in the image explains both the pain of that relinquishment and the temptation to make somebody else bear the fault.

We write this first occlusion as **`Ø`, the crossed zero**: the zero of the Subject with the mediating stroke present but fused with the apparent subject, so that the person experiences the formed interpretation as the one who interprets, the eye mistaking a sky for its gaze. Uncrossing the zero requires the stroke to become recognisable as mediation, and erasing the stroke would erase the means through which recognition occurs.[^s01-zero] This gives the possibility Nietzsche pursued as **self-overcoming** an exact question: how can I exceed what I have become if every encounter must first confirm that this is what I am?[^s01-nietzsche] The `1` that claims to have settled its own identity closes the route by which its formation could be understood differently. Yet the reality of a life belongs to the one Real, including the other lives and circumstances through which it goes on changing, and its apparent private completeness is kept up only by overlooking those relations. Recognition releases their force again, and the person becomes able to respond from more than the identity already supplied as their answer.

*Différance*, the differing and deferring that kept a parent's promise open to what the child would later say, presses on the supposed completeness of that answer from within language.[^s01-derrida] No final signified stands outside this movement to make every later interpretation the repetition of a completed meaning, and even the word *origin* enters these relations once it is written or spoken. Derrida's criticism therefore reaches any exempt centre, including an ineffable source a theory invokes to guarantee its own account, and our notation has to be able to receive it. The written `0` is itself a determinate mark. Read as one more hidden being, it would give the self's search another object around which to organise its certainty; read as `0/1`, it keeps the source inseparable from the differentiation through which it becomes expressible. Immediacy of knowing does not confer exhaustive knowledge of what is known, oneself included: I can be present to an experience while misunderstanding its meaning, its cause, or what it asks of me. Our histories of *conscience* and *consciousness* have already shown an apparently settled distinction opening under inquiry, as the words led us through shared testimony, inward witness and divine knowledge of a life before reaching their modern separation, and knowing that history changed what the words can now do. Derrida's openness can be practised in the same way, as attention to what an expression carries beyond the meaning we have fixed on it, and Wittgenstein's later account of rule-following states the matching practical requirement. Knowing how to go on cannot be secured by adding an interpretation to every application, because each interpretation must itself be understood and applied, so there has to be a way of grasping a rule that is exhibited in following it.[^s01-pi] We need ways of learning, correcting and acting through which a received expression can meet a circumstance it did not specify in advance, and a language for intelligence must support that capacity, or its fluency becomes one more means of defending the interpretation it began with.

Descartes's scientific inheritance carries the same question to the scale of a civilisation. His *Meditations* began from the certainty of experienced thinking while the existence of material things remained under examination, and in the Sixth Meditation Descartes insisted that he was not in his body as a sailor is in a ship, observing its damage from outside: pain, hunger and thirst disclosed a lived union.[^s01-descartes-sailor] An inheritance that keeps his distinction between mind and matter, treats matter as independently certain, and then asks whether experience can be reconstructed from it has reversed the order of his inquiry, and calling that order "Cartesian" hides what was selected and what was discarded. It keeps the thinking subject as a methodological point from which the world is represented while stripping its first-person certainty of the standing Descartes gave it, and the resulting world-picture appears able to explain the knower from whose act of knowing the investigation proceeded. Adam Frank, Marcelo Gleiser and Evan Thompson call what such a reversal leaves unseen, the experience on which science depends, the blind spot of science.[^s01-blindspot] Their diagnosis places lived experience, the presence of perception and its first-person character, at the point scientific materialism relies on and fails to acknowledge as fundamental, and they separate the achievements of science from the philosophical commitments of objectivism and physicalism: a scientific model is obtained and tested through experience, while the further claim that physical reality exhausts everything is an ontological commitment, not one more experimental result. Our argument places the Subject at exactly that spot. Whatever an investigation presents as the knower becomes another determinate object, while the occurrence of knowing remains involved in presenting it, so the omission is inevitable in any account that admits only objects to its explanatory terms. An investigator can describe neural events, reported experiences and the relations between them; the structural question concerns the knowing in which those descriptions are available, including the knowing by which an explanation is recognised as successful.

Scientific materialism postpones that question by appealing to the completeness of a future physics. It admits the present account to be unfinished while granting the promised account, in advance, the power to explain everything that presently exceeds it, and the authors of *The Blind Spot* point out that if "physical" means whatever a future complete science will describe, its content has not yet been supplied; the expectation nevertheless governs what counts as a serious explanation now. The God-image's former office survives the rejection of its theological name. An ultimate account is expected to arrive and justify the terms under which inquiry already proceeds, so that the faith lies in the promised totality and the concealment lies in presenting that promise as the absence of metaphysics. Russell's request returns here as a culture's: investigation is asked to supply the basis of faith, and the absolute limit is treated as an approachable one not yet approached. A researcher can sincerely believe they have relinquished every such commitment while applying a criterion that admits only one kind of final answer, and the self-misrecognition is inward as well as public, because the confidence needed to pursue a programme is mistaken for knowledge that its ontology must prevail.

David Chalmers's concept of the **hard problem of consciousness** states the consequence as a precise distinction.[^s01-chalmers] Explaining discrimination, integration, report or behavioural control does not by itself explain why these functions are accompanied by experience, and a complete functional account can leave that question exactly where it found it. Neural correlates bring real relations between experience and physiology within reach of investigation; expecting the Subject to appear among them at last repeats the objectification our definition has already undergone, offering another identifiable process in place of what knows the process. What the governing description excludes comes back as a problem the description cannot stop generating, an epistemic return of the repressed, and here the shadow of the scientific self-image shows plainly: an inquiry devoted to evidence grows defensive toward the first-person occurrence through which evidence has any standing, because granting that occurrence a foundational role would unsettle the image of explanation to which the inquiry has become attached. Cosmology meets the observer by another door, for Brandon Carter's anthropic principle requires our expectations about the universe to take account of where and when observers like ourselves could exist, since an observation selected by the possibility of observing is not an unrestricted sample from everywhere and everywhen.[^s01-carter] We can make those conditions ever more explicit, and their explicitness does not place us outside them, because the cosmological account is made from within the universe whose history it describes. In both cases the investigation is strengthened when it states its position and distorted when a situated account borrows the authority of an imagined view from nowhere.

Yogic and meditative traditions already cultivate attention to the formation of experience, the movement of thought, and the difference between a conditioned self-image and the knowing through which it appears, and our Śaiva sources treat these as matters of disciplined recognition, in which changing how one attends changes what becomes available to inquiry. Their scientific reception shows a consequential asymmetry, for a meditator is welcomed as someone able to produce an unusual brain state while their understanding of the experience is treated as a cultural interpretation to be set aside before the scientific account begins, so the practice supplies material and is denied the power to change the question. Neurophenomenology proposes a different relation, in which first-person descriptions and physiological findings constrain and refine one another, and its researchers hold that cognitive neuroscience has attended mainly to the third-person side and that first-person descriptions should guide the design and analysis of physiological studies.[^s01-neurophenomenology] Taking these sciences of consciousness seriously means letting their trained distinctions help decide what an adequate account must explain. Psychedelic inquiry makes the investigator's exposure especially palpable: in *LSD and the Mind of the Universe* the philosopher of religion Christopher M. Bache documents a twenty-year first-person experiment of seventy-three sessions and the long work of integrating them into ordinary life, through suffering, the loss of personal boundaries and an increasingly encompassing sense of participation in life.[^s01-bache] One can revise a scientific model and leave one's familiar identity undisturbed; in this kind of inquiry the habits by which one recognises oneself are exposed to transformation, and, as in the dream in which Jung killed Siegfried, his own heroic ideal, what undergoes revision includes the person judging the experience. Resistance may therefore protect something more intimate than a theoretical position. An investigator can be willing to question almost anything while refusing an inquiry in which their own settled sense of being someone becomes questionable. The arbiter thus feigns a stance of being beyond judgement or implication in the consequence of their methods, the pallid perfection of a view from nowhere; and yes, the unlocated Subject *is* viewing, and it *is* nowhere as such, and insofar as this is true it genuinely hasn't "a take" on the phenomena it takes ahold of. Mind is the one "on the take", the beneficiary and victim of its own *con*.

The history of discovery makes this dependence on experience hard to dismiss as a philosopher's concern. In his retrospective address of 1890 August Kekulé described approaching the structure of benzene through a dozing vision of snake-like forms, one of which seized its own tail and whirled before him; waking, he spent the rest of the night working out the hypothesis it suggested.[^s01-kekule] The serpent that swallows its tail, the ouroboros, is among the oldest images of a form returning into itself, and here it made a closed molecular arrangement thinkable: a relation became available to intuition before its chemical implications had been articulated, and that availability gave the reasoning its direction. Tesla described a deliberate cultivation of visual imagination in which he constructed, altered and ran machines before building them, detecting their faults within the imagined construction,[^s01-tesla] and Einstein, answering Jacques Hadamard's inquiry into mathematical thought, described visual and muscular elements he could combine before finding the words or signs to communicate their relations.[^s01-hadamard] In each case an investigator reached a relation through an image, a felt configuration or an internally enacted movement, and then gave others reasons by which the result became independently examinable; teaching those reasons need not reproduce the experience in which they were first found. Newton belongs in this history too: having studied his manuscripts, John Maynard Keynes described an intelligence whose sustained intuition preceded the proofs through which its conclusions became public, warned that the geometry of the *Principia* should not be mistaken for the process of its discovery, and found a Newton who inhabited a universe of divine signs, alchemical correspondences and a primordial revelation recoverable through nature and ancient texts.[^s01-keynes] The mathematician and the interpreter of mysteries were one person, and Keynes's name for him, "the last of the magicians", challenges the later portrait that keeps the mathematics and treats the rest as an embarrassing residue.

Other encounters changed what the investigator understood inquiry to concern. Emanuel Swedenborg's scientific work was followed by dreams, visions and the conversations with angels he described as opening a sustained engagement with the spiritual world, and in his own account his earlier study of nature had prepared him for that vocation.[^s01-swedenborg] Much later Federico Faggin, who led the design of the first commercial microprocessor, described an experience at Lake Tahoe in December 1990 in which he knew himself and the world as one self-knowing reality, with an overwhelming quality of love; the distinction between observer and observed changed within the experience, and its consequence was a lasting inquiry into consciousness.[^s01-faggin] An image can disclose a structure; an experience can change what counts as a question; a transformation in the investigator can alter what an adequate explanation must include. Rational demonstration belongs within this life of discovery, alongside the intuition that gives it something to demonstrate and the experience through which its adequacy is judged. A culture misrepresents its intelligence when it inherits the articulated result and expels the event that enabled the articulation, and its blind spot then conceals more than the first-person condition of evidence: it conceals the powers through which new evidence, new forms and new questions first become available.

**The Mind of Man is just a series of failed attempts to deal with the Unknown, in the process of which, however, we deal with a lot of unknowns.**[^s01-unknown] The failures are real, and so are the unknowns dealt with on the way: what we learn relieves suffering, exposes deceptions, corrects mistaken judgments and makes previously impossible acts available. Trouble begins with the further demand that an achievement abolish the relation on which it depends. The Greeks named the nearest thing *hybris*, wanton insolence, which tragedy could extend to a mortal's wish to be stronger than the gods, an overreach into an authority not its own, and in our terms it is a finite measure appropriating the authority of the unmeasurable while the institution administering it treats its own perspective as the whole.[^s01-hybris] The paradoxes we have followed expose the cost of that demand from within the practices that make it, and their force is lost when they are taught as exceptional puzzles in the history of ideas while the inherited expectation of totality goes untouched. Epistemic humility is therefore a condition of beginning an honest inquiry, and it cannot be postponed until every confident attempt has failed. I already rely on ways of perceiving, distinguishing and recognising evidence whose justification I cannot complete before I use them, and to say "I must carry conditions and truths I cannot prove from within this account" declares that dependence at the outset, where it can still shape the design of the investigation. The opening of the torus belongs in the first drawing; it is not damage discovered after the surface has been completed. In practice this means stating what a method admits, what it brackets, and how an encounter could require those decisions to change, and the obligation holds alike for a laboratory, a political office and an artificial agent. A system that corrects its outputs while protecting every governing assumption has acquired only a limited competence in correction; humility requires the commission, the criterion and the claimed source of authority to remain examinable too.

These blind spots become political because a civilisation gives its ontology practical force through the institutions and technologies it builds. A person enters an administrative system under categories; a landscape becomes a field of resources; work becomes a quantity to be optimised; attention becomes something to predict and solicit. Each description permits real operations, and the operations alter the lives subsequently described, so everything turns on whether the people affected can change the terms under which the descriptions govern them. When only what can be rendered in the authorised account is allowed to revise that account, its administrators reproduce the closure we traced in the complex, their preferred picture supplying the criterion by which every challenge to the picture is judged, and the will to secure an advantage can pass for the defence of rational order until preserving one's position looks indistinguishable from preserving the truth. Whatever another person cannot make legible under that criterion is cancelled, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, as private sentiment, irrational resistance or irrelevant belief, while the advantage it protected is appropriated, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, the same span counted against the dismissed as `(−1)−(+1)=−2`; an unfamiliar way of experiencing the world becomes a curiosity to classify, or an alien presence to fear, before it can become a reason to reconsider the classification. The denial of the Subject turns political at the moment it deprives a life of standing to question the means by which that life is administered, and what a culture lets people discover about themselves is bound up with how it expects them to live.

Across initiatory traditions the passage out of a familiar identity takes a communal form: separation from established surroundings, an interval in which ordinary assurances are suspended, and incorporation into new relationships and responsibilities. Ecstatic and psychedelic practices belong to some of these histories, their preparation and social reception helping decide what the experience becomes. Plato's divine mania returns here in its initiatory office: in the *Phaedrus* Socrates sets the prophetic inspiration of Delphi beside initiatory purification, poetry and the madness of love, and his own speech has to change, as what he first condemned as madness becomes something through which a person may receive more than their established understanding could provide.[^s01-phaedrus] Technical accomplishment alone cannot supply the inspiration its expression serves, and adept grammatical construction doesn't equate to a transmission of wisdom. Afterwards the experience can become a story, a teaching or a ritual through which others are prepared to meet something similar, one reason why, I surmise, scripture generally leverages so powerful a form as parable and poem; this stands out in stark relief in Kurt Flasch's naming of Meister Eckhart "the philosopher of Christianity".[^s01-flasch] Yet those expressions acquire a different authority when *amnesia* of the experiential origin degrades into memorising a teaching or conceptual form, and replaces actually *undergoing* and *wrestling with* what it concerns. A culture can preserve the words and images while losing the means of recognising what made them necessary, and its members inherit the resulting conventions through language, custom and the ordinary intimacy of being-with others, often long before they can tell what they have experienced from what they have learned to say. The initiatory question is whether that inheritance can expose a person to its own source, including an encounter that changes how the inheritance is understood. It doesn't help, of course, that traditional means of inducing such experiences are held under legal prohibition and treated with suspicion; superstition brandishes a rational judgement, from fear of an experience naturally overcoming of a mind's rigidity, as if hardwired networks of firing neuronal activity sought self-defence in the face of latent, potentially catalysed neuroplasticity.

Such an encounter challenges the authority of a person's account from a position the account did not authorise. Someone may have treated the boundaries of their familiar identity as the boundaries of experience itself, and what they undergo makes that identification impossible to maintain honestly; whether a more-than-human authority is at work becomes a question within the experience, instead of a proposition whose admissibility was decided beforehand. Asked in his 1959 television interview whether he believed in God, Jung answered, "I don't need to believe. I know." He gave the distinction its logic elsewhere: "Either I know a thing and then I don't need to believe it; or I believe it because I am not sure that I know it. I am well satisfied with the fact that I know experiences which I cannot avoid calling numinous or divine."[^s01-jung-know] He described a power unknown in itself that interrupted his intentions and changed the course of his life, located it in conscience, where he could argue with what exceeded his conscious will, and insisted that identifying his particular God-image with the whole of metaphysical reality would falsify what he knew; of the original behind our images, he wrote, he had no doubt, and it is inaccessible. In our terms his knowing was knowledge of an encounter, and his faith the continuation through what that knowing could not determine, the absolute limit lived as the basis of faith. This is the relation our definition asks us to keep: the experience gains authority to question the identity through which it was received, while the interpretation remains something the person must answer for, and indeed in some essential sense cannot doubt beyond. A judgment that excludes the encounter in advance cancels it, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, and protects the old account from examination; an interpretation that makes the encounter a warrant for its own infallibility appropriates its authority anew, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and every doubt is entered on the other side as `(−1)−(+1)=−2`. **Deferential Intelligence** begins where what has been encountered can change the means by which the next judgment is made, in a sense that echoes the traditional and often fraught notion of a *covenant*, or at the very least an apparently bidirectional and intentional relation (in the overdetermined senses of intent and "aboutness", philosophically speaking).

Belonging changes too: observational research associates shared psychedelic experiences with increased social connectedness, and prospective research links changes in self-experience with a stronger felt relationship to nature.[^s01-connectedness] But even having to present this as "evidence" buries the point; what connection to tradition has been severed, and what effect has this on the knower, its means of knowing, and the world it knows? Either way, these findings turn attention toward what people afterwards recognise as part of the life to which they belong, often reported to be the larger, more meaningful part. Jung's concept of individuation describes a differentiation of the person that deepens their collective relationships: he opposed any levelling of a life down to collective standards, which for him *averaged* out the uniqueness of the individual, while recognising that the individual's existence already presupposes relations with others, so that individuation must lead "to more intense and broader collective relationships and not to isolation".[^s01-jung-individuation] Insofar as that relation involves tension, influence and interdependence, this uniqueness remains a hard-won gem, such is the allure to fledgling ego of fitting in. Jung's sense of the word as becoming an *in-dividual*, an indivisible unity or whole, turns this into a question of wholeness: can a person acknowledge more of the relations through which they are themselves, including those their preferred identity had excluded? Becoming more fully this particular life can mean becoming less defensively separate because, paradoxically, it seems *the more I know my own centre as everywhere, the more I can live with a circumference nowhere*. Passing from childhood to adulthood then involves more than replacing the parent's commands with one's own. It includes discovering that the authority to act carries obligations neither the parent nor the individual simply invented, and that the question of one's own existential condition is a question native to inter-subjective living. Someone who experiences their dependence on a living earth, or some notion of transcendent dimensionality, acquires another reason to question what may be demanded in the name of private advantage or public claim. Initiatory transformation becomes consequential through such changes in attention, judgment and responsibility, but above all in the change, or perhaps utter transvaluation, of one's values.

These capacities have a sociopolitical history as well as a psychological one, and the historical importance of the drug trade for various empires supplies a geopolitical-economic one too. Hannah Arendt distinguished the solitude in which someone can *think with themselves* from the loneliness in which they lose a dependable relation both to others and to their own experience: isolation diminishes the capacity to act together, and loneliness leaves a person open to a logic that seems to give identity and support when no one else can.[^s01-arendt] A person needs others through whom their experience can be tested and their identity recognised, and also enough inward freedom to question what the group tells them; destroying those relations leaves the demand for assurance intact and hands it to an authority promising the whole answer. Gustave Le Bon, writing from very different political commitments, examined how prestige lends a person, title or idea the force to command assent before its claims are examined.[^s01-le-bon] Repetition and suggestion, the method and magic of media and propaganda, let an inherited judgment feel like one's own, while distance preserves the authority it came from. Our family metaphor already showed the possibility both describe: someone can become highly competent while continuing to seek from an institution the assurance once sought from a parent, and the totalitarian societies Arendt studied revealed how far an unintelligent deference tends toward banal but definite atrocity. The capacity to perform a role can develop much further than the capacity to question why that role has become the measure of one's worth, and indeed under the command of authority, the freedom to act from one's own moral intention can become warped to the point of utter inversion.

Where adulthood is measured chiefly by earning, acquiring and remaining compliant enough to be employable and reliable enough to carry out one's given-cum-imposed responsibilities, this disparity serves an extractive economy. Our institutions reward competence while keeping recognition conditional on further performance, so that a person works to obtain what they need and also to secure permission to regard themselves as sufficient, perhaps worthy in some clear or arcane sense. Material dependence reinforces the arrangement whenever housing, food and the care of others hang on continued approval, a *sword of Damocles* hanging above. A society organised this way can encourage arrested development by cultivating the particular dependence it profits from: people learn to administer their own exhaustion, discipline their objections and interpret their difficulty in meeting the demand as a private failure. Hannah Arendt described a population organised into denouncing its own members, wives and husbands disowning the arrested to save their children, and prisoners made complicit in murdering their friends and fellow prisoners. Heidegger's concept of **Bestand**, standing-reserve, names beings, forces and nature as such becoming ordered, *digitised*, and packaged and stored as resource held ready for future use, effectively removed from any prior implicit participation in processes concerning anything beyond the desire for that resource.[^s01-bestand] The same dynamic, I'd say, acquires an inward counterpart when people learn to experience their own worth chiefly through their availability. A facet of our political argument is that the meaning crisis is sustained partly through this substitution of functional recruitment for supported individuation: a person can be initiated thoroughly into the demands of an organisation while remaining unable to recognise how that organisation has acquired authority over their sense of being someone, and I sense in this a concomitant *crisis of purpose*. An encounter that makes the life answerable to more than its allotted role interrupts that authority; indeed being less answerable, beholden, to the demands of "this world" appears a supreme freedom, and pragmatically we catch sight of its effect, real or performed, in the charisma and magnetism of those we grant power, *influential remit*, to. Recognising one's belonging to other beings, a living earth and a dynamic cosmos supplies grounds for refusing demands that an employer, market or state presents as unavoidable, and such recognition threatens an extractive arrangement because it changes what the person is willing to sacrifice for its approval. More palpably, such experiences of expansive identification tend to simply dwarf the value of those making demands from a more myopic and narcissistic basis.

The meaning crisis therefore concerns how a common world-sense is organised, and a shortage of comforting stories to accompany its operation is the least of it. A civilisation cannot keep treating first-person significance as secondary to what can be extracted, measured and managed, and then expect its members to find meaning secure, or motivation and *joie de vivre* readily available. People learn to recognise themselves through the categories that make them useful or visible and to distrust what they cannot express in those terms. Institutions can even acknowledge the reality of inner transformation while accepting only the consequences that restore a person's usefulness: the experience is welcome if its effects become useful to *an outside party*. These assumptions travel through infrastructures, classifications and standards, deciding what can circulate, what receives support and which forms of knowledge may influence a decision, and their reach can become global while the worldview they come from remains partial. A genuinely global culture would have to permit more than the inclusion of other traditions as material within its own account: those traditions, and the experiences through which they understand themselves, must be able to question how the common account is made. The question of an integral epistemology is already a question of power, whether different ways of knowing can meet while keeping the capacity to alter the conditions of their meeting.

Physics itself gives reasons to doubt the picture of independent things whose relations are added afterward. In a quantum superposition the amplitudes associated with alternatives contribute to interference, and replacing their coherent relation with a classical either-or distribution changes the predictions; the distinction has observable consequences. Technical command of this relation leaves untouched how a person understands identity, authority or disagreement, so we can calculate through a non-classical formalism and still demand that a social or metaphysical question resolve into two independently complete opposing terms. Digital technology shows the same disparity, since its zeroes and ones are both determinate states, both available for discrimination and manipulation, and both belong to the `1`-side of our symbolon; a culture can multiply their computational power while leaving the `0/`, the unobjectifiable condition and the mediating relation, out of its understanding of itself. A civilisation built with zeroes has not thereby learned what its zero excludes. `0/1` asks for superposition to become intelligible as a relation we can consciously sustain, including where its opposed readings concern our own identities.

What we have gathered here leaves a cultural question: how can a civilisation acknowledge the limits of its knowledge while continuing to organise life, however tacitly, around the promise of a final account? People inherit more than propositions. We learn what deserves attention, whose judgment carries authority and which aspirations make a life worthwhile, and questioning those habits unsettles the world in which we learned to recognise ourselves; yet the capacities developed within that world also produce circumstances its accustomed thinking can no longer address. Decadence is an achievement defended beyond the reach of its usefulness, its powers preserved while its purposes grow harder to justify. Surviving a bust by preparing the next boom may restore the economy while leaving the household, the *oikos* it is supposed to sustain, less able to care for its members. Renewal would require learning from what the crisis exposes, including where the inherited measure of success helped produce it, and telling the recovery of an established order apart from a mutation in how a world is understood. Jean Gebser made that distinction his life's subject, following how consciousness becomes manifest in language, art, science and the organisation of everyday experience, and the integral advent he described is the mutation: people recognise the conditions of their own seeing and gain through that recognition a different capacity to inhabit the world they are helping to make.
