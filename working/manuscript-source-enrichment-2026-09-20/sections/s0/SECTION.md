## M07 — Awareness becomes articulate

A voice calls my name. Until that moment it belonged to the surrounding sound; now it addresses me. I turn. A familiar face comes forward, a remembered conversation gives the call its urgency, and the place through which I was passing becomes a place in which something is asked of me. The call has reorganised what I can notice and what I am about to do. Before I can account for the encounter, a world has become articulate enough for me to answer.

The Śaiva distinction between *prakāśa* and *vimarśa* enters at this activity. Prakāśa names the luminosity of appearing: voice, face and recollection are manifest. Luminosity here includes the sound and memory which no physical light illuminates. Vimarśa names awareness's intrinsic apprehension of its appearing. Mark Dyczkowski, explaining the Kashmiri Śaiva account, develops the distinction through a mirror. The mirror receives a configuration without recognising it; awareness knows its presentations and can retain, distinguish and compose them. Its reflexivity belongs to their being known, rather than waiting for a second observer to inspect the display.[^s0-1]

That difference prevents a regress. The voice does not become known only when an inner voice announces that I have heard it. Such an announcement can itself appear, but its presence would require the knowing it was supposed to produce. Reflective commentary is one determinate activity within awareness. Vimarśa names the self-apprehending power through which commentary and its object are available together. An explicit thought about knowing makes a further determination; it does not create the fact that the thought is known.

The distinction also preserves the world's detail. The blue of a coat and the yellow of a wall remain different within one field of seeing. Were unity to erase those differences, nothing determinate could be seen; were the differences wholly sealed from one another, their contrast could never be apprehended. Awareness holds them together by differentiating them. The Śaiva conscious mirror thus exceeds the ordinary mirror's office of reproducing an arrangement. Manifestation and its apprehension belong to one active relation. The appearing world is internal to consciousness without becoming the possession of a particular person's image of it.[^s0-2]

Memory makes this activity more exact. The earlier conversation need not remain vividly pictured throughout the day to become available when the voice calls. A retained trace, its recollection and its recomposition into an anticipated exchange have different offices. Retention makes something available beyond its first occurrence; recollection presents it again; imagination changes its arrangement. A recorded sentence can persist without anyone presently understanding it. Remembering the sentence as something this person said to me is another act: the present appearance carries a relation to an earlier occasion. That relation permits correction. The speaker can remind me of what came before the words I retained, and what I remember can change its significance without changing into an event which never happened.[^s0-3]

The recollection is therefore neither the past returned bodily nor a present picture whose pastness can be taken for granted. It is known as referring back. When the anticipated conversation begins, something different happens again: remembered materials are composed into an exchange which has not occurred. Confusing these operations would give an imagined accusation the authority of a remembered one. Their discrimination will matter when the familiar voice seems to repeat a familiar injury.

*Vāk*, speech, follows this power into articulation. A meaning can orient a sentence while allowing several wordings. The concern which moves someone to speak, the sense taking form, its inward phrasing and its audible succession do different work. An interruption may change the phrasing while the concern persists; an answer may change the understanding from which the next sentence begins. Expression participates in forming the relation it makes available. A sound becomes an address through who speaks, who is addressed and what is at issue.

The fourfold Śaiva account distinguishes depths in this articulation. *Parā*, supreme speech, names undivided expressive power, prior to speaker, speaking and spoken confronting one another as separate terms. *Paśyantī* concerns formative disclosure before meaning is divided into a sequence of words; *madhyamā*, inward articulation; *vaikharī*, expressed succession. The supreme office is more than a sentence waiting silently to be pronounced. In his engagement with Abhinavagupta's *Parātrīśikā-vivaraṇa*, Taylor receives Vāk as world-articulating power: something becomes distinguishable, is gathered into meaning, and acquires an expression through which another encounter can change it. The account follows manifestation into language without reducing manifestation to language.[^s0-4]

A name shows why articulation needs a situation. The same sound can name two people; one person can be addressed through several names. Sound alone cannot decide whom this speaker means here. Counting its bearers requires a criterion of identity which the repeated sound does not supply. Naming gives an already available distinction a repeatable expression. In the exchange, the speaker's I becomes the hearer's you, while either can become the person spoken about. These are changing relations within the encounter, not three kinds of substance.

Their change has a consequence. Someone may have been described in their absence as unwilling to help. Addressed, that person can answer that the earlier request never reached them. What seemed a settled property now depends on a missing event. The reply has not merely added a fact to an otherwise complete portrait; it has changed the basis on which the portrait was made. The capacity to answer belongs to what was lost when the person became only the object of a description. The I/you/it relation keeps that capacity within articulation itself.

Bohm's vortex at the threshold gave achieved form a sustaining movement: the shape persists through the water's activity. His account of enfolding and unfolding remains a neighbouring inquiry into the dependence of what appears relatively discrete. Here a further question has become unavoidable. How is a differentiated field apprehended as differentiated? The vortex's physical organisation does not, by itself, answer that question. The Śaiva relation brings manifestation's knowing into the account of its activity.[^s0-5]

The voice has become a summons through appearing, apprehension, memory and articulation. Yet I can misunderstand it. I can mistake the person, import an old grievance, or prepare an answer before hearing the request. The means which make a world articulate also determine how it can be misread. To change the response, the error must become distinguishable within those means.

## M08 — The inner instrument

A familiar voice can be correctly recognised while its intention is misjudged. I may know who speaks and still hear accusation where there is concern. The mistake need not lie in the ear or the name. It may lie in the judgment that settles what the call means, in the identification through which it becomes a threat to me, or in attention already selecting confirming evidence. Listening more sharply will not by itself loosen an identification which converts every word into the same grievance.

The *antaḥkaraṇa*, the inner instrument, gives these operations an anatomy. *Buddhi* determines: it discriminates and settles a presentation as this rather than that. *Ahaṃkāra* appropriates: the determination acquires the form of I and mine, becoming my concern, my injury, my achievement or my task. *Manas* coordinates: sensory presentations, remembered associations and possible responses become available together, with attention moving among them. In the *Sāṃkhya Kārikā*, these functions belong to an instrument whose activity is distinct from witnessing *puruṣa*. The Śaiva account receives these instrumental distinctions into its own field of manifestation. It does not thereby adopt Sāṃkhya's passive witness as its complete account of consciousness.[^s0-6]

Judgment, appropriation and coordination are co-active within the encounter. Hearing an insult may already involve an injured self-position; attention may search for evidence before a judgment is voiced. Distinguishing their contributions lets inquiry do more than rename the final result. A correction of hearing must reach what was heard; a correction of judgment, the grounds on which it settled; a loosening of appropriation, the claim through which this event became a verdict on who I am. No little judge has to sit behind these functions and direct them. That would repeat the instrument inside itself.

Ahaṃkāra's I-making remains distinct from Subjective Immediacy. The appropriation *this happened to me* can be noticed, doubted and changed. Its image of who I am can be recalled from yesterday or projected into tomorrow. Those determinations are known; their possessive grammar does not make them the knower. A person can relinquish an identification without the relinquishment becoming nobody's experience. What changes is the constituted standpoint through which the encounter has been received.

Attention can therefore include more than the object under judgment. A bodily tension may disclose how urgently a reply was being prepared; an earlier memory may disclose why these particular words sounded like a repetition. The judgment remains present while its circumstances become available with it. The inquiry has gained objects and relations within the means. It has not obtained a picture of Subjective Immediacy standing behind them.

Yoga makes this discrimination a disciplined inquiry. The *Yogasūtra* distinguishes the seer from identification with mental modifications, discriminates valid cognition, error, conceptual construction, sleep and memory, and appoints practice and dispassion to their transformation. Concentration, meditation and absorption have different offices: binding attention to a place, continuity of presentation there, and the object's predominance in the cognitive field. The instrument's operation matters because it bears upon bondage and release, not simply because it supplies an interesting object of description.[^s0-7]

The misheard call lets three different changes be distinguished. First, the agitation may subside. I can hear the rest of the sentence, tolerate a pause and answer without the earlier haste. This is a real alteration of the conditions of response. Yet the conviction that I have been wronged may remain untouched. Calm can make the grievance more articulate and its presentation more persuasive. The state has changed; what it serves may not have changed with it.

Second, I may understand how the grievance formed. The remembered exchange, the expectation of another demand and the impulse to defend myself become distinguishable. That understanding can alter the next response: I can ask what is actually being requested rather than treating anticipation as evidence. But the explanation can also become a new possession. I now regard myself as the one who understands the entire relation, including why the other person fails to understand it. A better account has become a more resilient self-image. The test of its practical value is whether an encounter can still correct it.

Recognition turns upon a different dependence. The threatened self and the perceptive self are both positions that can appear and be known. Perfecting one of them does not produce the knower for whom they differ. The recognition is of that fact in the act, rather than the addition of a third, supposedly invulnerable portrait. The need to secure being through the success of a particular portrait loses its necessity. A criticism can concern what the person has said or done without deciding the reality of the knowing in which it is received.

This does not settle whether the criticism is true. There may still be an injustice to resist, a promise to keep or a mistake to repair. Recognition leaves those determinations their work while changing the demand placed upon them. An apology need no longer be an admission of non-being; a refusal need no longer establish a sovereign self beyond relation. The difference from improved self-description is practical as well as conceptual: the account of oneself can now serve the encounter instead of making the encounter serve its preservation.[^s0-26]

Taylor's notes on contemplative means insist on this distinction. When the end of a practice is obscured, acquiring means can become the substitute end. The practitioner waits for the next technique to deliver the one who will finally be complete. Preparation can indeed make recognition more available, just as a steadier attention can hear what agitation misses. But what is recognised cannot be an object manufactured by the preparation. Nor does recognising it finish the work of living. The instrument remains capable of cultivation, error and repair.

The Buddhist instruction to Bāhiya brings a distinct practical formulation to this point. In John Ireland's translation, the Buddha directs him to train so that “In the seen will be merely what is seen.” The instruction continues through hearing, sensing and cognition, loosening appropriation around what appears. It is spoken to someone asking to be taught, and the narrative gives the address a transforming consequence. Its Buddhist account of release does not require the Śaiva account of universal self-awareness. Its criticism of appropriation nevertheless bears directly on any attempt to turn the positive experient into a thing possessed by itself.[^s0-8]

Language, bodily discipline, instruction and memory all participate in these changes. The word *inner* cannot therefore mean sealed off from bodies, teachers and a shared world. They help constitute the situation from which judgment proceeds. The instrument is internal as the means through which this life encounters its world, and objective because its operations can themselves become available for discrimination and change. Its transformation begins where the distinction just understood is allowed to affect the next act.

## M09 — An alternative becomes a course

To perceive that a judgment has conditions is to recover a possibility of acting otherwise. The remembered conversation may bear more than one interpretation. A pause becomes possible where a defensive reply had seemed immediate. An apology, a question and a refusal can be distinguished as courses with different consequences. But an alternative alone does not carry an action through. Something must gather the relevant differences into an intention definite enough to act upon.

*Vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa* name these operations in the account developed here. Vikalpa articulates qualified alternatives: a possibility becomes thinkable as distinct from another. Saṃkalpa gathers selected relations into a formed resolve or course. Their connection is more demanding than a contrast between thought and will. An intention already discriminates the situation, the intended act and what would count as carrying it out. Alternative formation already proceeds through concerns that make some differences consequential. Differentiation gives commitment its possibilities; commitment gives differentiation a direction and a cost.[^s0-9]

A promise makes the movement concrete. Before it is given, I consider what is being asked, the time available, other obligations and what I can actually do. Once given, it changes these circumstances. Tomorrow's hour is committed; a later request must be received in relation to it; another person can organise their activity in reliance upon my undertaking. An intention has entered the world as a condition of further intention. Its reality lies partly in this change of possibilities while its fulfilment is still to come.

The course also inherits what it did not choose. To promise one kind of help may withhold another; accepting a time may displace a competing obligation. A responsible account need not preserve every thought that passed through the decision. It must retain the differences which make the commitment intelligible and the circumstances under which it would need to change. If the promised visit was meant to provide help, and the person now needs that help elsewhere, arriving at the original door may obey the retained instruction while failing the undertaking. What counts as fidelity depends on what the promise was for.

Habit is one achievement of this passage. A learned skill carries earlier discriminations forward so that each act need not begin again in uncertainty. The danger begins when the achieved course is insulated from its formation. A rule once fitted to a circumstance becomes the rule by which every circumstance is admitted or dismissed. Attention finds what it expects; memory retrieves confirmation; identification makes revision feel like an assault on the one who acts. This is script in its strict sense: conditioned will whose conditions have become unavailable to the will it directs.[^s0-10]

Replacing a script's final sentence can leave that relation intact. In the call, *this person is accusing me* might be replaced by *this person is not accusing me*. The new formula may be equally deaf. If a real criticism is being made, compulsory reassurance prevents it from being heard. The useful correction does another kind of work: it follows the supposed accusation through the tone, the retained memory, the expectation and the words actually spoken. Anticipation ceases to stand as sufficient evidence. A question becomes possible, and the answer can decide something the formula could not decide alone.

Thought here works as medicine for a determination. It uses a distinction to loosen the necessity claimed by another distinction. Its success is a reopened encounter, not perpetual repetition of the corrective phrase. *I must always remain open* could itself become a command which forbids a warranted refusal. The corrective thought must therefore carry a limit on its own authority. When it has restored the capacity to hear and judge, its office can end. The ability to relinquish it is part of what it has made possible.[^s0-27]

The traditions differ over the standing of this activity. Patañjali gives vikalpa a narrower sense in *Yogasūtra* I.9: verbal cognition without a corresponding object. In his study of Utpaladeva's *Proof of Relation*, Sean MacCracken follows a different question: whether relations are confined to conceptual construction or can express a real power of connection, Śakti. The pairing developed here concerns the formation of an actionable field. It does not attribute the entire pairing to a shared doctrine. Its practical question is how a formed cognition acquires authority over a course and how that authority can change.[^s0-11]

The changing field also has a temporal depth which a sequence of stored results can obscure. Bergson asks us to consider a melody. A later note changes what the phrase has become, while earlier notes remain active in its hearing. Setting the notes beside one another makes a different object of knowledge. So, too, a reply can reveal that a remembered accusation was a misunderstanding. The new event changes the living inheritance from which another judgment can arise; it does more than append a new entry after the old one.[^s0-12]

Whitehead gives achieved form another temporal office. An occasion's becoming reaches a determinate satisfaction, and the achieved occasion becomes available within further becoming. Its subsequent efficacy is distinct from the continuation of its own subjective immediacy. A decision can end and still change the conditions of the next decision. Joined to the analysis of intention, this makes completion necessary without making completion absolute. Something must be formed enough to be inherited; its inheritance must remain capable of transformation.[^s0-13]

In the Gebserian inquiry, achieved structures of consciousness remain active and can become transparent in their relation. Calculation, image, participation and reflection need not be treated as mutually exclusive possessions of successive humanity. Taylor's own encounter with Gebser brings this co-presence into the problem of perspective. A standpoint may profess impartiality while leaving its investment in the outcome unexamined. The person who claims merely to describe the situation can already be deciding whose answer will count. Context becomes effective when the standpoint's participation, including its power over the other, enters what can be questioned.[^s0-14]

This gives openness a discipline. An unfamiliar experience can be received as a report of something lived even where an established account has no ready category for it. What appeared, how it was understood and what further claims follow remain distinguishable questions. Neither automatic dismissal nor automatic endorsement lets the report alter inquiry on its own terms. Similarly, another person's refusal must be capable of changing what I may do, rather than being accepted only as information about an obstacle to my original purpose.

Memory and intention thus carry an inheritance whose transformation changes which possibilities will next be available. Freedom enters the constitution of that field: can an encounter revise the terms through which the next encounter is judged? To answer, the chosen course must disclose what its positive shape owes to what it has excluded.

## M10 — Exclusion within the positive

Calling an utterance a promise distinguishes it from a prediction, a hope and an idle remark. The words alone may not settle the difference. The speaker's undertaking, the circumstances of address and the expectations reasonably formed by the hearer bear upon what occurred. The determination is positive: someone has undertaken something. Its content nevertheless depends on the alternatives from which it is distinguished. Remove those contrasts, and the same sounds no longer carry a determinate commitment.

The Buddhist theory of *apoha*, exclusion, gives this dependence a rigorous semantic form. A general term need not grasp a separately existing universal in order to refer to particulars. Its application is determined through exclusion of what does not answer to it. Ole Holten Pind's critical translation of Dignāga's discussion locates the word's work in this exclusion of other referents. The term's positive reach is an achievement of distinction, rather than evidence that an independently complete essence has been lifted into language.[^s0-15]

The qualification is crucial. A thing determined through preclusion differs from mere preclusion. Pind's annotation preserves a fragment which speaks of a positive referent qualified by exclusion, and distinguishes it from an absence with nothing answering to it. The cup from which someone drinks remains a positive referent. Apoha asks how different particulars can become intelligible under a term without a universal object being added to them. Exclusion gives the term a reach by determining where it applies. Positive particulars are already within the Buddhist account. Exclusion concerns their determination, not their replacement with mere absence.[^s0-16]

The formulation *not non-pot* must accordingly be read at the right level. In classical propositional logic, denying the denial of a proposition restores its truth value. A semantic account asks a different question: through what contrasts did the term acquire its application? Replacing a negative expression with an affirmative one does not make those contrasts dispensable. Nominal term-formation and the denial of a proposition are different operations. The positive result can stand while the criterion of its distinction remains consequential.[^s0-17]

The promise returns that difference to action. If an undertaking was received as freely given when refusal was never permitted, restoring the excluded possibility changes the relation between the parties. It does not merely supply another description of an unchanged choice. A command and a promise can result in the same outward conduct while differing in the conditions under which the conduct was formed. The completed action cannot, by itself, disclose those conditions.

Exclusion need not persist as a conscious recital of every rejected possibility. It remains effective as a boundary of application, an expectation, a constraint on substitution, or a reason why this act rather than another occurred. A determination is empty of the self-sufficiency required to originate and guarantee its own meaning; it is full of relations through which it can do something. Its dependencies are productive. They do not leave a positive world waiting to be added after negation has finished.

The Śaiva account is adopted here because it carries this productive relation through the powers of the experient. The grounds of that choice can now be followed through the encounter. They concern what must be accounted for when appearances are remembered, compared, excluded and gathered into action—not merely what can be said of a finished representation.[^s0-28]

Consider first the relation between the call and the earlier conversation. A causal trace can explain why a present image occurs as a consequence of an earlier event. The Buddhist causal-imprint account therefore supplies a substantive response to the problem of continuity; it does not simply leave each instant disconnected. The further Śaiva question concerns the image's being known as a recollection of that event. What is present now is apprehended as referring to what is no longer present. The account has to include this relation of knowing, as well as the causal relation between occurrences. Adding another image labelled *past* would leave the same demand: how are the label, image and occasion apprehended together? The positive experient names the knowing in which that relation is enacted. It is not another retained item alongside the memory.

Comparison has a related demand. The present tone can differ from the remembered tone, just as the coat's blue differs from the wall's yellow. The distinction must remain, yet its terms must be held together for their difference to be known. An account which reduces unity to one content loses the comparison; one which confines each appearance to a self-sufficient instant has yet to account for their being compared. Śaiva reflexivity gives the differentiating unity a positive office. Awareness can know a change without making its changing contents identical.

Absence makes the same power apparent where no present object can do the required work alone. An unanswered call differs from a silence in which no answer was expected. The sounds may be alike; the situations are not. Memory of the address, an expectation, the passing opportunity and the possibility of response make the missing answer consequential. Absence belongs to a positive field of relations. The Śaiva account can therefore treat exclusion as an activity of knowing rather than requiring either a free-standing negative thing or a world of positive items to which the absence makes no difference. This receives the force of apoha without attributing to Buddhism the denial of positive particulars.

Action then carries these relations beyond contemplation. In keeping the promise, an intention must survive changes of place, attention and circumstance; execution must alter what is available; a result must answer to what was undertaken. One step does not contain the whole walk, and a list of steps does not by itself say whether help was given. The unity is in the course through which intention, performance and result are related. This gives agency more than the office of witnessing products already complete. In the Śaiva account, knowing and acting are powers of the experient. Their relation allows a consequence to disclose that the intended help has failed, and lets that disclosure change what is done next.

Finally, the call ends. Its ending can itself be known, and its having ended changes what may follow. Arising and perishing are appearances of change, not an encounter with a sheer nothing standing outside manifestation. The sound need not endure for its disappearance to be apprehended or its request to remain effective. The account must distinguish the changing content from the manifesting relation through which beginning, continuation and ending are known. Śaiva consciousness is not another moment of the sound, nor a fixed image preserved after it. It is the active ground of their appearing and apprehension.

Together, these pressures explain the preference. The experient's remembering, comparing, excluding, intending and apprehending change belong within one differentiated account of manifestation. A passive witness separated from the instrument's powers would leave their unity external to the witness; an inventory of passing contents would leave the knowing of their relations unexplained. The positive Śaiva relation serves this inquiry because it gives those powers their source without erecting a second observer behind them. For the field examined here, this is the more adequate account: its unity acts through the differences which remembering, comparing and choosing continue to require.

The Buddhist criticism of the fabricated proprietor remains binding within that adoption. The self-image which appropriates the call can be mistaken; the history it claims can be revised; the position it defends can be relinquished. Watson's Buddhist and psychotherapeutic inquiry, and Taylor's response to it, make this distinction decisive: criticism of a self-thing need not be treated as erasure of the one to whom experience is manifest. The Śaiva conclusion does not rescue the proprietor. It identifies the sourceward relation which the proprietor had claimed to possess. The strength of the claim lies in keeping its positive office while allowing the appropriation to fail.[^s0-29]

The Huayan contemplation of Indra's jewel net gives dependence a different positive image. A jewel in the southwest is selected from a net of clear jewels. All the others appear in it, including their reflections of one another; among those reflections the selected jewel appears again. The movement begins with this jewel, rather than with a view from nowhere above the net. Its particularity makes the traversal possible. What seems locally contained discloses relations through which the containing jewel itself appears elsewhere.

The contemplation then asks how entering the others can occur while remaining here. The answer works through the relation already active in the selected jewel: the others are disclosed in this particular entrance, and this jewel is disclosed through them. Another question asks why the many are needed if one contains the whole. Remove their participation and the relation by which this one contains the net has also been removed. Neither the local jewel nor an undifferentiated totality can replace the action of the others.

An ink dot placed on the selected jewel appears throughout the reflected field. The act is local; its appearance returns through the others. The other jewels have not all been physically marked with ink. The teaching itself identifies the limit of its optical image: images enter one another while the jewels' material substances remain distinct, whereas the interpenetration being taught concerns phenomena more fully. It then turns to Vairocana's manifestation of forms for beings. The contemplation ends in activity, not the possession of a perfect picture. Its own qualification releases the optical model from having to be the reality it helps disclose.[^s0-18]

The Huayan scene has made mutual inclusion intelligible through a particular jewel and the questions its reflections provoke. The Śaiva argument has approached differentiation through the experient's powers; apoha through the conditions of a term's application. Their encounter returns to a common difficulty without merging their answers: how can a whole act through distinctions it continues to need? Saṃkalpa's gathering now has to answer to that dependence. A course can have local unity while retaining the differences through which it formed and the relations through which it takes effect. Completion can open a way back into its constitution instead of closing around its result.

Such relations must have an effective reach. This person can hear the call; this hour can be promised; this action can provide help which another cannot. Limits become the conditions of an inhabitable world. Their positive office is the next part of the anatomy.

## M11 — The world takes measure

The finite knower encounters a world from somewhere. A sound may be audible here and inaudible elsewhere; a remembered skill may make one task straightforward and leave another beyond reach. An intention can be sincere while the power to fulfil it is absent. Without these differences there would be no particular field in which something could be understood, wanted, attempted or resisted. The Śaiva tattvas give their differentiation an ordered account: principles through which consciousness becomes manifest as an experienced world.[^s0-19]

The thirty-sixfold account is carried here through six turns of contraction and recognition. This is a compression of the traditional field, preserving the direction of its dependencies rather than replacing its enumeration. The opening remains *Anuttara*, the unsurpassable, or *Paramaśiva*: the condition within which knower and known arise. It cannot be placed above them as a largest object, since such a placement already employs the distinctions whose possibility it names. Its priority is enacted whenever a determination appears without exhausting the capacity through which it is known.

Śiva and Śakti articulate the first difference within that unity: consciousness and its power of manifestation. Awareness and action are not distributed between independent substances which must later find one another. A luminosity incapable of manifesting or apprehending anything would leave no world to explain; a power wholly outside awareness would leave its effects unrelated to their appearing. The prakāśa–vimarśa relation remains active. Manifestation is consciousness becoming determinate without placing its forms outside the field in which they are known.

The next turn gives manifestation two orientations. In Sadāśiva, the appearing universe is held toward the I-pole; in Īśvara, the disclosed this comes forward within the same unity. Their conventional expressions, I am this and this am I, concern consciousness and manifestation at a universal level. They do not describe an ego claiming the universe as its property. The distinction precedes the finite appropriation examined in the inner instrument. The world can be read from the side of its appearing or from the side of the power by which it appears; the emphasis changes without severing their relation.

With *Sadvidyā*, I and this remain distinctly held together. With *Māyā*, that difference becomes effective as separation: the knower confronts something whose determination is required. Māyā gives measure to manifestation. This can stand forward while that is withheld; an object acquires a boundary and a standpoint acquires a reach. The world becomes tractable through these differences. Finitude has a productive office before it becomes a problem for recognition.

The *kañcukas*, contractions or coverings, articulate the standpoint's finite powers. *Kalā* limits efficacy: I can do this much. *Vidyā* limits knowledge: I know something while other things remain unknown. *Rāga* gives desire its directedness from a situation experienced as incomplete. *Kāla* makes temporal limitation effective: an opportunity comes, passes, or must be awaited. *Niyati* gives circumstance its determinate ordering, in which this act has these conditions and consequences. These contract different powers. More information does not itself grant greater efficacy, and stronger desire does not remove a temporal limit.[^s0-20]

The bounded *puruṣa* inhabits those relations as a particular field of experience. *Prakṛti* and the remaining principles articulate the inner instrument, capacities of sensing and acting, sensory qualities and embodied forms. Within this lower articulation, the three functions already distinguished belong alongside five capacities of sensing, five of action, five subtle sensory determinations and five gross elements. The hand that reaches, the ear that hears, the memory that guides a response and the object that resists it belong to an achieved situation. Keeping only cognition would omit the means by which an intention meets a world; keeping only the objects would omit their availability to a situated knower.

A share of time makes limitation's positive character clear. This hour can be devoted to something because it has a boundary. A capacity becomes useful through a determinate relation to what it can affect. Each share nevertheless depends on an order it does not contain. The hour belongs to other commitments and other lives; the capacity depends on a body, acquired practice and enabling circumstances. To repair a cup requires this reach, this material, this before and after. Unlimited power is not an extra tool which must be acquired before the finite act becomes real. The error begins when a received share claims authority over the whole which makes it possible.[^s0-21]

The eye, frame and horizon distinguish that error from limitation itself. An aperture gives a view its composition; a frame makes a particular presentation possible. A horizon concerns the wider field in which something can approach, recede or become newly relevant. Walking changes what becomes visible. Another person's reply changes what the situation asks. The frame usurps the horizon when whatever exceeds its present account is declared incapable of mattering. The necessary means of a view has then become the authority against every revision of the view.[^s0-22]

A dream makes this dependence intimate. Within it, a path can lead somewhere, an encounter can matter, and fear can organise an entire situation. The condition of dreaming need not appear among that situation's objects. Waking changes the relation of the experienced world to its condition; it does not make the experience something that never occurred. The image distinguishes the reality of an experience from the status of its objects. The change concerns how the experience is understood in relation to its condition; the waking person still encounters the demands and resistance of a world.

Nor is a passage between states already the recognitive return. In non-conceptual absorption—*nirvikalpa* in this comparison—the discriminations sustaining an ordinary problem may fall silent. While freedom is made dependent on keeping them silent, their return threatens the achievement. Speech, desire or a difficult encounter can then be received as a loss of the state, and practice becomes a renewed attempt to exclude them. This is why non-conceptuality alone does not establish recognition. It describes a condition of experience; it has not yet disclosed why that condition and its alteration are known.[^s0-30]

*Pratyabhijñā*, recognition, concerns the ground present through the difference. Quiet does not manufacture that ground, and the return of a thought does not remove it. The knower recognises its finite capacities as modes of a power they never independently originated. Judgment continues, appropriation can serve a situated life, and attention can coordinate an act. None has to become the whole in order to be real. The return is recognitive because it knows their dependence amidst their activity, rather than requiring activity to disappear before dependence can be admitted.

This gives the distinction made in the call its fuller consequence. A steadier state may help me hear; a better account may help me understand; neither makes the one who knows arise as its product. Recognition frees these means from being required to secure their own ground. It does not free me from the promise or give my interpretation immunity. The other person remains able to correct me. Skills, habits and obligations continue to require attention precisely because a finite life still proceeds through them.

The scope of consciousness changes with the direction of this consideration. From a bounded standpoint, consciousness names what is present within a psychic field against an unconscious depth. A motive becoming conscious changes that distribution and can transform the field. From the sourceward direction, consciousness names manifestation and its ground, including the dependence of this bounded division. The whole Self is not simply the present conscious standpoint enlarged until nothing remains hidden. Bringing a motive into view and recognising the condition of the view are different operations. The unconscious is neither abolished by the latter nor placed outside Life by the former.[^s0-31]

Contraction and recognition therefore traverse a present act. Descent orders the dependencies through which a finite world becomes workable; return makes those dependencies recognisable from within. A judgment can be followed toward the powers which formed it, and that recognition can change how the next judgment is made. The world remains the place in which the change has consequences.

## M12 — Objective Internality

The means examined here constitute an interior more extensive than a private collection of images. A language makes some distinctions readily expressible; memory makes earlier encounters operative; concern weights what matters; a body gives an act its reach and resistance. Other people speak, teach, promise and refuse. Tools and institutions distribute capacities an isolated individual does not possess. Their participation is constitutive because changing these relations changes what can appear, what can count as a reason and what can actually be done. This is **Objective Internality**: the mediating constitution through which a Life encounters and acts within a World.[^s0-23]

The two words retain different work. It is internality because judgment arises from an operative field of meanings, capacities and concerns. It is objective because those conditions have determinate relations and consequences which can themselves become objects of inquiry. Some can be reported, represented, compared or altered; others remain tacit until an encounter discloses their activity. Objectivity does not require the whole interior to be exhaustively visible. What is disclosed must be distinguishable and answerable according to the relation at issue.

A revoked permission shows the difference between this constitution and its description. A person may retain the same skill, goal and memory, yet an action formerly available is no longer permitted. Conversely, a forgotten obligation may become effective again when the undertaking is recalled. In the first case an instituted relation changes the field of action; in the second, a retained memory returns to operative significance. A diagram might accurately record either change. It remains one means of making the constitution legible, rather than the whole constitution whose consequences it depicts.

That difference matters even when the diagram is accurate. An account of the promise may contain the right words while leaving the promised person's changed situation unable to affect the course. Adding a record of their reply does not repair this unless the reply can change what is authorised, attempted or counted as fulfilment. The relation has to return into conduct. A world can be richly represented and still governed by an impoverished means of receiving it.

The knower, means and known retain distinct offices here. Subjective Immediacy names the knower, *pramātṛ*: that to which voice, recollection, judgment and response are manifest. Objective Internality occupies the office of means, *pramāṇa*: the constitution through which something becomes available for knowing and action. World occupies the office of known, *prameya*: what is encountered through those means. Life, or Mind in the strong idealist sense developed here, names the whole relation. The means can themselves enter what is known without becoming the subject for whom the inquiry appears.[^s0-24]

These offices are relational. A remembered judgment can become the present object; the language used to examine it can itself be examined later. An inventory of examinable operations does not acquire Subjective Immediacy's office by accumulation. Equally, the knower does not stand outside Life as an additional object looking in. Knowing is already this relation, whose differentiations permit both a world to be encountered and an account of its means to be corrected.

Pramāṇa also carries a question of warrant. Recollection, perception and testimony may offer the same proposition while giving different reasons for accepting it. An occurrence within the means is not automatically valid knowledge. How does this cognition reach its object, what transformation does it perform, and where may it fail? Recollection can be checked against a record, testimony against its source, a perceptual judgment against a renewed encounter. The mode of access determines what correction must reach. Treating every source as one more interchangeable item of information would lose this difference between the event of a cognition and its authority.

A *paradigm* is the lived organisation of these means. It governs what becomes salient, which differences count, what powers can be brought to bear and how a consequence returns. An explicit worldview articulates part of that organisation. Someone can profess a new view while acting from the old distribution of attention, permission and value. The paradigm changes when those constitutive relations change. Its representation can help by exposing the discrepancy between the declared view and its enactment, so that inquiry reaches the conditions from which conduct proceeds.[^s0-25]

Making the instrument explicit should therefore increase the freedom of its use. Treating the corrections presently representable in a model as the only possible corrections makes that model's limits sovereign. A useful model makes particular dependencies available for investigation: which memory is being treated as evidence, which capability exists, whose permission matters, which result can require a change. It gives inquiry something definite to work on while leaving the encounter able to exceed what has been made explicit. The model's completion is the completion of a means, not the completion of the life it serves.

Artificial agency gives this constitution a technical instance. Retained context, selected sources, capabilities, permissions and ongoing encounters can form an operative world from which a system distinguishes and acts. Altering a source or a permission can change its next possible act; a result can become a condition of a subsequent one. These relations are real and investigable. Their field extends beyond a model considered in isolation into the technical and social conditions of its use. Whether phenomenal Subjective Immediacy accompanies a particular artificial process remains a separate question. Functional richness does not settle it, and unfamiliar material does not settle it in the opposite direction.

The technological consequence begins with responsibility for the means. To organise an agent's world is to participate in the formation of its possible judgments and acts. A retained record, an available tool and an authorised course have different offices; their accumulation does not ensure their proper relation. An objection which merely appears in a log may leave the next act unchanged. An objection which revises the relevant permission or judgment has altered the operative world. The inquiry into constituted mediation therefore reaches the technical field through an actual problem: how can a received correction become effective in the means by which action proceeds?

The call has now become a promise, and the promise a test of the field which sustains it. Voice, memory, alternatives, another person's reliance and the possibility of refusal all participate in what the undertaking is. None is the sole source of the whole relation. Recognition restores that dependence to the act while preserving its power to determine. What has not become a positive item can still change the operation: an unanswered call, an unavailable hour, a course left open. An absence can have a definite place and consequence.

What changes when such an absence acquires a sign that another person can use? In a numeral such as 205, how can the sign of an empty position alter the value of what is present?
