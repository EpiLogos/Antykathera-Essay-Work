# §0 — Differentiating Mind

## M07 — Awareness becomes articulate

A voice calls my name. Until that moment it belonged to the surrounding sound; now it addresses me. I turn. A familiar face comes forward, a remembered conversation gives the call its urgency, and the place through which I was passing becomes a place in which something is asked of me. The call has reorganised what I can notice and what I am about to do. The uncounted condition of appearing, left open at the threshold, is already active in this differentiation. Before I can account for the encounter, a world has become articulate enough for me to answer.

The Śaiva distinction between *prakāśa* and *vimarśa* enters at this activity. Prakāśa names the luminosity of appearing: the voice, face and recollection are present to awareness. Luminosity here concerns their being manifest, including the sound and memory which no physical light illuminates. Vimarśa names awareness's intrinsic apprehension of its own appearing. Without this reflexive power, a display could be present as an arrangement without being known as anything. Dyczkowski develops the distinction through the contrast with a mirror. A mirror receives a configuration but does not recognise it; the conscious field knows its presentations and can retain, distinguish and compose them. Its reflexivity belongs to the first appearance, rather than waiting for a second observer to arrive and inspect it.[^s0-1]

That last point prevents a regress. The familiar voice does not become known only when another inner voice announces that I have heard it. Such an announcement can itself appear, and be attended to, but its presence would require the very knowing it was supposed to produce. Reflective commentary is one determinate activity within awareness. Vimarśa, in the Śaiva account, names the self-apprehending power by which commentary and its object can appear together. When that power articulates an explicit thought about itself, it makes a further determination; it does not thereby create the fact that the thought is known.

The distinction also preserves the world's detail. The blue of a coat and the yellow of a wall remain different within one field of seeing. Were unity to erase those differences, nothing determinate could be seen; were the differences wholly sealed from one another, their contrast could never be apprehended. Awareness holds them together by differentiating them. Dyczkowski's conscious mirror therefore does more than reproduce an original deposited before it: manifestation and its recognition belong to the same power. This is a positive idealist account of the relation, in which the appearing world is internal to consciousness without being the possession of a particular person's image of it.[^s0-2]

Memory makes the activity more exact. The earlier conversation need not remain vividly pictured throughout the day to become available when the voice calls. A retained trace, its recollection and the recomposition of remembered material into an anticipated exchange are different operations. Retention makes something available beyond its first occurrence; recollection presents it again; imagination changes its arrangement. Through these differences a phrase can be understood as a phrase, and a present event can alter the significance of what preceded it. The field's continuity is active: it keeps a past available through transformations which cannot be explained by putting isolated instants beside one another.[^s0-3]

*Vāk*, speech, follows this power into articulation. A meaning can orient a sentence while allowing several possible wordings. The concern which moves someone to speak, the sense taking form, its inward phrasing and its audible succession have distinguishable offices. An interruption may change the phrasing while the concern persists; an answer may change the understanding from which a second sentence begins. Expression therefore participates in forming the relation it makes available. The voice becomes a call through the articulation of who speaks, who is addressed and what is at issue. These relations make a world intelligible before an explicit account of them is given.

The Śaiva fourfold account gives this articulation different depths. *Parā*, the supreme level, names undivided expressive power, prior to speaker, speaking and spoken confronting one another as separate terms. *Paśyantī* names formative disclosure, in which meaning becomes discernible before its division into a sequence of words. *Madhyamā* names inwardly articulated speech; *vaikharī* its expressed succession. The supreme office is thus deeper than a sentence waiting silently to be pronounced. Vāk names the world-articulating power of consciousness, while inward language and audible utterance give that power particular forms. The account returns speech to the activity of manifestation: something becomes distinguishable, is gathered into meaning, and acquires an expression through which another encounter can change it.[^s0-4]

The call of a name shows why articulation also requires a situation. The same sound can name two people, while one person can be addressed through several names. Sound alone cannot decide whom this speaker means here. Counting the bearers of a name requires a criterion of identity which the repeated sound does not supply. A difference must already be available through which this bearer, among others, can be reached. Naming gives that distinction a repeatable expression. In the exchange, the speaker's I becomes the hearer's you, while either can become the person spoken about. First, second and third person articulate changing relations within the encounter, through which the same participant can speak, be addressed and be described. An address can therefore disclose something that a description misses: the person described may answer, and the answer may change what the description was thought to mean.

This passage from implicated capacity to expressed determination gives Bohm's account of enfolding and unfolding a precise neighbouring office. In his essay on postmodern science, a relatively discrete form emerges within an ongoing movement; its apparent separateness does not remove the activity sustaining it. His vortex makes this visible as an identifiable pattern of moving water. The Śaiva account adds a different question: how the field is manifest and apprehended in its differentiation. The comparison concerns the dependence of achieved form on active implication. It joins two inquiries without making a physical pattern supply the knowing of its own appearance.[^s0-5]

The voice has become a summons through luminosity, recognition, memory and articulation. Yet I can misunderstand it. I can mistake the person, import an old grievance, or prepare an answer before hearing the request. The means which make a world articulate also determine how it can be misread. Their operations must now be distinguished closely enough for an error to be located and for a different response to become possible.

## M08 — The inner instrument

A familiar voice can be correctly recognised while its intention is misjudged. I may know who speaks and still hear accusation where there is concern. The mistake need not lie in the ear or the name. It may lie in the judgment that settles what the call means, in the identification through which it becomes a threat to me, or in the direction of attention that has already selected confirming evidence. These differences matter because they require different changes. Listening more sharply will not by itself loosen an identification which converts every word into the same grievance.

The *antaḥkaraṇa*, the inner instrument, gives these operations an anatomy. *Buddhi* determines: it discriminates and settles a presentation as this rather than that. *Ahaṃkāra* appropriates: the determination acquires the form of I and mine, becoming my concern, my injury, my achievement or my task. *Manas* coordinates: sensory presentations, remembered associations and possible responses become available together, with attention moving among them. In the Sāṃkhya Kārikā these functions are differentiated within an instrument whose activity is distinct from the witnessing *puruṣa*. The Śaiva field receives the instrumental distinctions within its own account of manifestation. It does not thereby inherit Sāṃkhya's conception of a passive witness as its complete account of consciousness.[^s0-6]

Judgment, appropriation and coordination are co-active functions within an act. Their distinction makes different contributions to that act intelligible. Hearing an insult may already involve an injured self-position; attention may begin searching for evidence before an explicit judgment is voiced. Their mutual involvement is precisely why the differences must remain available. Without them, an account of the act can do little more than name its final result and attribute it to an undifferentiated mind.

Ahaṃkāra's I-making must, in particular, remain distinct from Subjective Immediacy. The appropriation *this happened to me* can be noticed, doubted and changed. Its accompanying image of who I am can be recalled from yesterday or projected into tomorrow. Those determinations are given to awareness; their possessive grammar does not make them the knower. A person can relinquish an identification without the relinquishment becoming nobody's experience. What changes is the constituted standpoint, the formation through which the encounter has been received. Confusing that formation with the subject would make every revision of self-understanding either impossible or a replacement of the one who understands.

This gives attention a practical depth. Attention can belong entirely to the object under judgment, or it can include the activity by which the object has become threatening, desirable or irrelevant. In the second case, the judgment remains present while its circumstances become available with it. A bodily tension may disclose how urgently a reply was being prepared; an earlier memory may disclose why these particular words were heard as a repetition. The investigation has gained objects and relations within the means. It has not obtained a picture of Subjective Immediacy standing behind them.

Yoga already makes this discrimination a disciplined practice. The Yogasūtra distinguishes the seer from identification with mental modifications, discriminates valid cognition, error, conceptual construction, sleep and memory, and appoints practice and dispassion to their transformation. Its account of concentration, meditation and absorption differentiates binding attention to a place, continuity of presentation there, and the object's predominance in the cognitive field. Analysis of the instrument and cultivation of its use belong to one soteriological inquiry: the means of experience can be investigated because their operation bears upon bondage and release.[^s0-7]

The practical implication exceeds an exercise in describing one's states. A description can become another object of attachment: I now possess a sophisticated account of why I am right. An inquiry changes its reach when the recognition of an operation can alter the next operation. Attention to the grievance must become capable of interrupting the readiness to repeat it; the remembered event must be available for reconsideration rather than protected as the final measure of every later event. The inner instrument then participates in its own transformation. Its capacity to discriminate is used to disclose where a discrimination has acquired excessive authority.

The Buddhist instruction to Bāhiya brings a distinct practical formulation to this point. In Ireland's translation, the Buddha directs him to train so that “In the seen will be merely what is seen.” The instruction continues through hearing, sensing and cognition, loosening appropriation around what appears. It is spoken to someone who has asked to be taught, and the narrative gives the address a transforming consequence. An utterance here changes the hearer's relation to experience rather than supplying another object to accumulate. Its Buddhist account of release does not require the Śaiva account of universal self-awareness.[^s0-8]

These practices make the instrument's dependence investigable from within. Language supplies distinctions; bodily discipline changes the conditions of attention; another's instruction alters what can be recognised; memory carries the result into a later occasion. The word *inner* therefore cannot mean sealed off from bodies, teachers, habits and a shared world. They participate in the conditions from which a judgment is made. The instrument is internal as the means through which this life encounters its world, and objective because its operations can themselves become available for discrimination and change.

## M09 — An alternative becomes a course

To perceive that a judgment has conditions is to recover a possibility of acting otherwise. The remembered conversation may bear more than one interpretation. A pause may become possible where a defensive reply had seemed immediate. An apology, a question and a refusal can now be distinguished as different courses, each with consequences. The field has acquired alternatives, but an alternative alone does not carry an action through. Something must gather the relevant differences into a sufficiently definite intention for a response to occur.

*Vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa* name the two operations in the account developed here. Vikalpa articulates qualified alternatives: a possibility becomes thinkable as distinct from another. Saṃkalpa gathers selected relations into a formed resolve or course. Their connection is more demanding than a contrast between thought and will. An intention already contains discriminations about the situation, the intended act and what would count as carrying it out; alternative formation already proceeds through concerns that make some differences consequential. Differentiation gives commitment its possibilities, while commitment gives differentiation a direction and a cost.[^s0-9]

A promise makes the movement concrete. Before it is given, I consider what is being asked, the time available, other obligations and what I can actually do. Once given, the promise changes these circumstances. Tomorrow's hour is now committed; a later request must be received in relation to it; another person can organise their activity in reliance upon my undertaking. A formed intention has entered the world as a condition of further intention. Its reality lies partly in this change of possibilities, even while its fulfilment remains in the future.

The course also inherits what it did not choose. To promise one kind of help may be to withhold another; accepting a time may displace a competing obligation. A responsible account need not preserve every thought which passed through the decision. It must retain the differences that make the commitment intelligible and the circumstances under which it would need to change. Otherwise the intention loses its relation to the situation it was formed to answer. Fidelity then becomes mere repetition of the selected words, including when repetition defeats the undertaking's purpose.

Habit is one achievement of this passage. A learned skill carries earlier discriminations forward so that each act need not begin again in uncertainty. The danger begins when the achieved course becomes insulated from its own formation. A rule once fitted to a circumstance becomes the rule by which every circumstance is admitted or dismissed. Attention finds what the rule expects; memory retrieves what confirms it; identification makes revision feel like an assault on the one who acts. This is script in its strict sense: conditioned will whose conditions have become unavailable to the will it directs.[^s0-10]

Changing a script consequently requires more than replacing its final sentence. An affirmative instruction can reproduce the same compulsory relation as a negative one. The deeper change restores the capacity to form alternatives and to let a relevant encounter affect selection. Thought can participate in this restoration by making a rigid formation explicit, following what it excludes, and loosening its claim to necessity. It becomes medicine for a determination when its work leaves that determination revisable. The medicine must remain available for release in turn; otherwise the corrective concept becomes the next protected command.

The traditions differ over the status of this formative activity. Patañjali gives vikalpa a narrower sense in Yogasūtra I.9: verbal cognition without a corresponding object. Buddhist and Śaiva arguments also differ over whether constructed relations belong only to conceptual apprehension or disclose a real power of relation. MacCracken locates this dispute in Utpaladeva's defence of Śakti against a Buddhist account. The pairing developed here follows the production of an actionable field; it does not attribute that entire account to a single shared doctrine. The traditions meet at a specific problem—how a formed cognition can govern experience and how its authority can change.[^s0-11]

The changing field has a temporal depth which a sequence of stored results can obscure. Bergson's melody is a qualitative whole: a later note changes what the phrase has become, while the earlier notes remain active in its hearing. Setting the notes beside one another gives another object of knowledge. Similarly, the reply that reveals an old accusation to have been a misunderstanding changes the remembered episode's significance. The new event changes the living inheritance from which another judgment can arise.[^s0-12]

Whitehead gives achieved form a different temporal office. An occasion's becoming reaches a determinate satisfaction, and the achieved occasion becomes available within further becoming. Its objective availability is distinct from the continuation of its own subjective immediacy. This distinguishes a result's subsequent efficacy from the life of its formation: a decision can end and still change the conditions of the next decision. Joined to the analysis of intention, the distinction makes completion necessary without making completion absolute. Something must be sufficiently formed to be inherited; inheritance must remain capable of transformation.[^s0-13]

These temporal distinctions also prepare the integral demand received at the threshold. In the Gebserian account, achieved structures of consciousness remain co-present and can become transparent in their relation. A life can calculate, imagine, participate and reflect through powers that remain active together. Their conjunction gives an encounter several avenues of disclosure. An unusual experience can enter inquiry as a report of a psychic fact even where the prevailing account has no ready category for it. Receiving the report opens the question of what has appeared and how it is to be understood; it does not decide in advance every claim made on its behalf. The field of investigation widens while its different means remain answerable.[^s0-14]

A finite world is thus both achieved and underway. Memory and intention carry an inheritance whose transformation can change which possibilities will next be available. The question of freedom enters the constitution of that field: can what is encountered revise the terms through which the next encounter will be judged? To answer, the determination must disclose what its positive shape owes to exclusion.

## M10 — Exclusion within the positive

Calling an utterance a promise distinguishes it from a prediction, a hope and an idle remark. The words alone may not settle the difference. The speaker's undertaking, the circumstances of address and the expectations reasonably formed by the hearer bear upon what has occurred. The determination is positive: someone has undertaken something. Yet its content depends upon the alternatives from which it is distinguished. Remove those contrasts, and the same sounds no longer carry a determinate commitment.

The Buddhist theory of *apoha*, exclusion, gives this dependence a rigorous semantic form. A general term need not grasp a separately existing universal in order to refer to particulars. Its application is determined through exclusion of what does not answer to it. Pind's translation of Dignāga's discussion locates the word's work in this exclusion of other referents. The positive reach of the term is an achievement of distinction, rather than evidence that an independently complete essence has been lifted into language.[^s0-15]

The qualification is crucial. A thing determined through preclusion is different from mere preclusion. Pind's annotation preserves a positive referent qualified by exclusion, and distinguishes it from an absence with nothing answering to it. The cup from which someone drinks remains a positive referent. Apoha asks how different particulars can become intelligible under a term without a universal object being added to them. Exclusion does positive work: it gives the term a reach by determining where it applies.[^s0-16]

The often-used formulation *not non-pot* must therefore be read at the right level. In classical propositional logic, denying the denial of a proposition restores its truth value. A semantic account asks a further question: through what contrasts did this term acquire its application? Those contrasts are not made dispensable by replacing a negative expression with an affirmative one. The relation between nominal term-formation and denial of a proposition matters because they are different operations. The positive result can stand while the history and criterion of its distinction remain consequential. An account of its truth value alone cannot perform an account of its formation.[^s0-17]

The argument now returns to intention. A selected course is intelligible through the possibilities it excludes, just as a name applies through a determinate contrast. Their relation is operational rather than an identity between choosing an act and referring with a word. In each case, the completed positive leaves a debt to the field through which it became determinate. Recovering that debt can change what follows. If the promise was accepted as a command because refusal was never permitted, admitting the excluded possibility changes the relation between the parties, not merely the description of an unchanged choice.

This is what it means for exclusion to remain within the positive. It need not persist as a conscious recital of every rejected possibility. It remains effective as a boundary of application, a constraint on substitution, an expectation or a reason why this act rather than another has occurred. Its disclosure makes a determination answerable. The promise can be distinguished from the command because their conditions of formation differ, even when the required action is outwardly identical. The excluded alternative reveals something the completed action alone cannot show.

The same movement clarifies the relation between emptiness and fullness in the present argument. A determination is empty of the self-sufficiency it would need in order to originate and guarantee its own meaning. It is full of operative relations: a criterion, a history, contrasts, powers and consequences. These statements belong together because the relations which prevent its independence are also those through which it can do something. The determinate world is possible through dependencies that no determination can possess as its own creation.

The essay's whole-first inference develops this constitutive dependence across the traditions' different arguments. The Buddhist analysis resists the promotion of conceptual unity into a separately existing universal. The Śaiva analysis follows the power through which differentiated appearances can be held, recognised and recomposed within awareness. Their ontological accounts differ. Their encounter makes the present argument more exact: the positive is constituted through distinction, and the act which distinguishes must remain intelligible alongside its result. Neither a name nor a completed image can supply the source of its own determining capacity.

The Huayan contemplation of Indra’s jewel net gives this dependence a further, positive image. A jewel in the southwest is selected from a net of clear jewels. All the others appear in it, including their reflections of one another; among those reflections the selected jewel appears again. The contemplating reader is asked how entering the others can occur while remaining here. The answer makes this particular entrance the condition of the traversal: the others are disclosed through the relation already active in the chosen jewel. Another question asks why the many are needed when one contains the whole. Remove their participation, and the relation by which this one contains the net has also been removed.

An ink dot placed on the selected jewel then appears throughout the reflected field. The mark stays particular while its appearance makes the return through the others visible. The teaching also states the limit of its image: jewel images enter one another while their material substances remain distinct, whereas the interpenetration being taught concerns phenomena more fully. Its closing movement turns to Vairocana’s manifestation of forms for beings. The contemplation is an instrument of understanding whose own qualification prevents its optical picture from becoming the final object of possession.[^s0-18]

Semantic exclusion, conditioned arising and Huayan mutual inclusion now contribute different operations to the account. Exclusion makes a determination applicable; conditions make its occurrence possible; the net teaches how a local member discloses the participation of others in its intelligibility. Their conjunction gives saṃkalpa’s gathering a sharper demand. A course can retain the differences through which it was formed and the relations through which it takes effect. Its local unity then opens a way back into its constitution, rather than closing around the selected result.

A world of knowing and action requires such differences with effective reach: what this person can know, what this act can accomplish, what is relevant now and what must be deferred. These limits give exclusion its lived form. They make a particular world inhabitable, while ensuring that the world encountered here can never be exhausted by what has already been admitted into its account.

## M11 — The world takes measure

The finite knower encounters a world from somewhere. A sound may be audible here and inaudible elsewhere; a remembered skill may make one task straightforward and leave another beyond reach. An intention can be sincere while the power to fulfil it is absent. These are constitutive differences in the means of life. Without them there would be no particular field in which something could be understood, wanted, attempted or resisted. The Śaiva tattvas give their differentiation an ordered account: principles through which consciousness becomes manifest as an experienced world.[^s0-19]

The thirty-sixfold account is carried here through six turns of contraction and recognition. This is an argumentative compression of the traditional field, preserving the direction of its dependencies. The opening remains *Anuttara*, the unsurpassable, or *Paramaśiva*: the condition within which knower and known can arise. It cannot be placed above them as a largest object, since any such placement would already employ the distinctions whose possibility it names. Its priority is enacted whenever a determination appears without exhausting the capacity through which it is known.

Śiva and Śakti articulate the first difference within that unity: consciousness and its power of manifestation. The distinction does not distribute awareness and action between independent substances which must later find one another. A luminosity incapable of manifesting or apprehending anything would leave no world to explain; a power wholly outside awareness would leave its effects unrelated to their appearing. The relation developed as prakāśa–vimarśa remains active here. Manifestation is the power of consciousness to become determinate without placing its forms outside the field in which they are known.

The next turn gives manifestation two orientations. In the Sadāśiva orientation, the appearing universe is held toward the I-pole; in the Īśvara orientation, the disclosed this comes forward within the same unity. Their conventional expressions, I am this and this am I, concern the relation of consciousness and manifestation at a universal level. They do not describe an ego claiming the universe as its property. The distinction precedes the finite appropriation whose claim to mine was examined in the inner instrument. The world can be read from the side of its appearing or from the side of the power by which it appears; the change of emphasis retains their relation.

With *Sadvidyā*, I and this remain distinctly held together. With *Māyā*, that difference becomes effective as separation: the knower confronts something whose determination is required. Māyā gives measure to manifestation. It allows this to stand forward while that is withheld, an object to acquire a boundary and a standpoint to acquire its reach. The world becomes tractable through these differences. Their dependence explains how they acquire a place and an effect within the whole.

The *kañcukas*, the contractions or coverings, articulate the finite powers of this standpoint. *Kalā* limits efficacy: I can do this much. *Vidyā* limits knowledge: I know something while other things remain unknown. *Rāga* gives desire its directedness from a situation experienced as incomplete. *Kāla* makes temporal limitation effective: an opportunity comes, passes, or must be awaited. *Niyati* gives circumstance its determinate ordering, in which this act has these conditions and consequences. They contract different powers. More information does not itself grant greater efficacy, and a stronger desire does not remove a temporal limit.[^s0-20]

The bounded *puruṣa* inhabits those relations as a particular field of experience. *Prakṛti* and the remaining principles articulate the instrument, the capacities of sensing and acting, sensory qualities and embodied forms through which that field becomes concrete. The hand which can reach, the ear which can hear, the memory which can guide a response and the object which resists it belong to the same achieved situation. Their distinctions make action possible. An account which retained only cognition would omit the means through which an intention meets a world; an account which retained only objects would omit their availability to a situated knower.

Apportionment makes the positive character of this limitation clear. A share of time becomes workable through its boundary: this hour can be devoted to something. A capacity becomes useful through a determinate relation to what it can affect. Each share nevertheless depends on an order it does not contain. The hour belongs to other commitments and to the lives of others; the capacity depends on a body, acquired practice and enabling circumstances. Finitude is therefore a distribution of effective powers within a sustaining whole. The error begins when a received share claims authority over the whole that makes it possible.[^s0-21]

The eye, frame and horizon distinguish this error from limitation itself. An aperture gives a view its composition; a frame makes a particular presentation possible. A horizon concerns the wider field in which something can approach, recede or become newly relevant. Walking changes what becomes visible. Another person's reply changes what the situation asks. The frame usurps the horizon when whatever falls outside its present account is declared incapable of mattering. At that point, the necessary means of a view has become the authority against every possible revision of the view.[^s0-22]

A dream makes the dependence intimate. Within it, a path can lead somewhere, an encounter can matter, and fear can organise an entire situation. The condition of dreaming need not appear as one of that situation's objects. Waking changes the relation of the experienced world to its condition; it does not make the experience something that never occurred. This is the operation the image discloses. It allows the reality of an experience and the status of its objects to be investigated separately. The same discipline receives ordinary and extraordinary reports as psychic facts while retaining distinct questions about their interpretation and their further reference.

*Pratyabhijñā*, recognition, completes the return through this differentiated field. The knower recognises that its finite capacities are modes of a power they never independently originated. Determination continues, appropriation can serve a situated life, and attention can coordinate an act. Their relation to the source has changed: none has to become the whole in order to be real. Recognition proceeds through the veil by apprehending its manifesting activity. To destroy every distinction would remove the very means through which recognition becomes available within a life.

The anatomy is therefore processual. Its descent orders the dependencies of a finite world, while its return makes those dependencies recognisable from within. These directions of inquiry traverse the present act. A determinate judgment can be followed toward the powers which formed it, and that recognition can change how the next judgment is made. The finite world remains the place in which this change has consequences.

## M12 — Objective Internality

The means examined here constitute an interior more extensive than a private collection of images. A language determines which distinctions can be readily expressed; memory makes earlier encounters operative; concern weights what matters; a body gives an act its reach and resistance. Other people speak, teach, promise and refuse. Tools and institutions distribute capacities that an isolated individual does not possess. Their participation is constitutive because changing these relations changes what can appear, what can count as a reason and what can actually be done. This is **Objective Internality**: the mediating constitution through which a Life encounters and acts within a World.[^s0-23]

The two words retain different work. It is internality because judgment arises from an operative field of meanings, capacities and concerns. It is objective because those conditions have determinate relations and consequences which can themselves become objects of inquiry. Some can be reported, represented, compared or altered; others remain tacit until an encounter discloses their activity. Objectivity does not require the whole interior to be exhaustively visible. It requires that what is disclosed can be distinguished and made answerable according to the kind of relation at issue.

A revoked permission shows the difference between this constitution and its description. The person may retain the same skill, goal and memory, yet an action formerly available is no longer permitted. Conversely, a remembered undertaking may become effective again when a forgotten obligation is recalled. In the first case an instituted relation changes the field of action; in the second, an available memory returns to operative significance. A diagram might accurately record either change. The diagram is one means of making the constitution legible, not the whole constitution whose consequences it depicts.

The knower, means and known must remain distinct within this relation. Subjective Immediacy names the knower, *pramātṛ*: that to which the voice, recollection, judgment and response are manifest. Objective Internality occupies the office of means, *pramāṇa*: the constitution through which something becomes available for knowing and action. World occupies the office of known, *prameya*: what is encountered through those means. Life, or Mind in the strong idealist sense developed here, names the whole relation in which these offices arise. The world is known through mediation, while the means themselves can enter what is known. Their becoming objects of inquiry does not make them the subject for whom the inquiry appears.[^s0-24]

These offices are relational, and their distinction remains necessary when the inquiry turns upon itself. A remembered judgment can become the present object; the language used to examine it can itself be examined later. In neither case does an inventory of examinable operations acquire the office of Subjective Immediacy by accumulation. Nor does the knower stand outside Life as an additional object looking in. Knowing is already this relation, whose differentiations permit both a world to be encountered and an account of its means to be corrected.

The appointment of pramāṇa therefore includes a question of warrant. A recollection, a perception and another person's testimony may offer the same proposition while offering different reasons for accepting it. An occurrence within the means is not automatically valid knowledge. The inquiry asks how this cognition reaches its object, what transformation it performs and where it may fail. A recollection can be checked against a record, testimony against its source, and a perceptual judgment against a renewed encounter. The mode of access determines what a correction must reach.

A *paradigm* is the lived organisation of these means. It governs what becomes salient, which differences count, what powers can be brought to bear and how a consequence returns. An explicit worldview is one articulation of this deeper organisation. Someone can profess a new view while continuing to act from the old distribution of attention, permission and value. The paradigm changes when these constitutive relations change. Its representation is valuable because it can expose a discrepancy between the declared view and its enactment, allowing a question to reach the conditions from which conduct proceeds.[^s0-25]

Artificial agency gives this constitution a technical instance. A system's retained context, selected sources, capabilities, permissions and ongoing encounters can form an operative world from which it distinguishes and acts. Altering a source or a permission can change its next possible act; an achieved result can become a condition of a subsequent one. Those relations are real and investigable. They constitute its field of possible reception and action, including the ways a new encounter can change that field. Whether phenomenal Subjective Immediacy accompanies a particular artificial process remains a separate question. Functional richness does not settle it, and unfamiliar material does not settle it in the opposite direction.

The technological consequence begins, then, with responsibility for the means. To organise an agent's world is to participate in the formation of its possible judgments and acts. A retained record, an available tool and an authorised course have different offices; their relation matters more than their mere accumulation. The investigation opened by Yoga and the Indian anatomies of mind reaches this technical field through the common problem of constituted mediation. The modern instrument gives some of that constitution a new material form; it does not invent the inquiry into how means govern experience or how their operation may be transformed.

The uncounted condition has remained active throughout. A determination selects without producing the whole field from which selection becomes possible. Its excluded alternatives, inherited powers and unexhausted horizon participate in its positive reality. Recognition returns these relations to the act, preserving its ability to determine while releasing its claim to be self-originating. What has not been made into a positive item can nevertheless change the operation. An empty place may direct attention, a missing answer may alter a course, an absence may acquire a use. The next question is how such an absence becomes a sign, and what happens to a world of reckoning when that sign is allowed to act.
