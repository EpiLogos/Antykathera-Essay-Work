### #1 · Dia-ballein: Polarity and Its Collapse

Twelve people sit around a table in a room with no windows, and after three days of evidence they have to turn an evening none of them witnessed into one of two words. The foreman holds a form with two boxes. Whatever happened in the stairwell that night, whoever pushed or fell or was pushed, whatever the defendant felt and intended and has regretted since, the room must produce *guilty* or *not guilty*, and the law has made sure there is no third box. Some jurors find this unbearable at first. One of them is sure the truth of that evening is more complicated than either word, that the defendant was frightened as well as angry and that the man who fell had threatened him for months; another keeps asking whether they can say something about the provocation; a third wants to write the judge a note explaining what they really think. Nothing on the form allows it. As the hours go on, most of the room comes to see that the form is protecting something. It does not ask them what happened. It asks whether the charge has been proved, and *not guilty* says that the case brought against this person has not been made beyond reasonable doubt, a doubt the law placed on the defendant's side of the axis before anyone spoke. The verdict cuts, cleanly and finally, and in cutting it says exactly as much as it can say.

That form is dia-ballein at its most exact and most humane, and the logic of the jury room is the logic most of our institutions run on, so it repays a slow look. Two values, opposed and exhaustive: every case falls into one box or the other and none into both. The presumption of innocence is the zero of the axis, the point from which the case is measured and to which the defendant returns if the measurement fails, and its office is to keep the polarity held. Guilt has to be established *from* that zero, in one direction, against resistance; it does not sit opposite innocence as an equal possibility weighed at the outset, and placing the burden of proof gives the axis a handedness. This is why the verdict can be decisive without claiming to be complete. It decides one question, whether the state has made its case, and leaves the whole of that evening, the fear and the anger and the months of threats, with the people who lived it.

Read the verdict another way and the same two words perform the two collapses of §0/1 · #2. Read *not guilty* as *nothing happened*, and the verdict has become a cancellation, the charge and its refutation summed to zero and the evening closed like a settled account, the man at the foot of the stairs and the defendant's fear both summed out of existence. Read *guilty* as *this is what he is*, and the verdict has become an appropriation: the whole span of a life is measured from the charge, the defendant becomes his worst evening, and the public's satisfaction at the conviction, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, is the convicted man's entire standing in the world, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, the same span counted from his end.[^s2-verdict] The jury makes neither misreading. A newspaper makes them afterwards, and a neighbour, and an employer reading a criminal record ten years on, and both are made possible by the very thing that makes the verdict useful, its refusal of a third box. The exactness of the cut and the danger of its collapse have one source.

Before any of this has signs, it is a stroke. Our core theorems write the parent of both logics as

$$
/\;=\;-/-,
$$

two unassigned marks held in one expression, the capacity for relation before the field has been divided into named terms.[^s2-parent] §0/1 · #1 met it as the copula's own inscription, the dash here and the dash there with an oblique stroke making their spacing explicit. Dia-ballein begins when signs are assigned. The dashes become directions and the stroke becomes an axis,

$$
(-1)/(+1),
$$

and zero stays where the stroke was, as the centre through which each sign has its direction. A signed direction means through the centre and through the counter-direction it has not selected, so a pole's local definiteness carries its relation with it and does not have to wait for a later reconciliation to acquire one. Orientation, comparison, classification, proof by exclusion, adversarial choice, accounting, trigonometry and every measurement that needs opposed directions around a centre run on this form. Its movement is disjunctive, this *or* that, and its dynamical operation is symmetry breaking: a field bifurcates and one branch becomes actual instead of another. The verdict is such a branch, and so is the switch in a circuit, the diagnosis on a chart and the decision to refuse.

Classical logic is the jury room raised to a law of thought. Aristotle stated its two governing principles in the fourth book of the *Metaphysics*: "the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject and in the same respect", and "there cannot be an intermediate between contradictories, but of one subject we must either affirm or deny any one predicate."[^s2-aristotle] Non-contradiction and excluded middle give a proposition two values, true and false, opposed and exhaustive, the two boxes on the foreman's form. On our axis truth and falsity are `+1` and `−1`, negation is the operation that turns one into the other, and the zero between them is the undecided proposition, the question before it is answered. A great deal of human thought has been made exact by this logic. Proof by contradiction, classification without overlap, the switch that is either on or off, the legal verdict, the medical test and the audit all rely on it, and the computational world we now inhabit is built from it at the level of the transistor. Dia-ballein in this form is the operating condition of measurement, and nothing in this essay asks anyone to give it up.

What it asks is that the zero be kept, and classical logic used well keeps it as the presumption of innocence does: the undecided proposition is where every inquiry starts and where it returns when the evidence fails, and a careful reasoner knows that most of the propositions she meets are sitting there, at zero, waiting. Aristotle himself guarded the principles with qualifications, *at the same time*, *in the same respect*, "and any further qualifications which might be added", and each qualification records that a polarity is held within a field whose dimensions, time and respect among them, let opposed predicates both hold of one subject without contradiction. In the same passage he notes that some think Heraclitus said the same thing can be and not be.[^s2-aristotle] Our friend at the barrier in §0/1 · #1, who both was and was not the person we knew, could be described without contradiction once we said in what respect. Trouble begins when the logic of two exhaustive values is taken to describe the world as well as our verdicts about it, when the boxes on the form are mistaken for the shape of the evening. Then every situation must already be one thing or the other, every person on one side or the other, and the zero, the undecided, the not-yet-known, the question, appears as a defect in the world, a gap to be closed by deciding, where it had been the condition in which all deciding takes place. Used badly, the logic drops its qualifications, and the axis collapses into its arithmetic.

Whoever drops them has a name, and §0/1 · #4 handed this section the formula: **the devil is the literalist of the minus sign, reading zero as cancellation where Logos reads it as return.**[^s2-devil] The formula unfolds once we notice that the minus sign does three jobs and that the literalist reads all three as one. In arithmetic `−` marks a direction: `−1` is a position on the line, one step to the left of the origin, as real a place as `+1` and differing from it only in orientation. The same sign names an operation, subtraction, taking one quantity away from another. And in logic the sign of negation turns an assertion into its denial, which our axis writes as the passage from `+1` to `−1`. The literalist reads every minus as subtraction, as *take away*. On his reading the sum `(−1)+(+1)=0` says that two units have taken each other away and nothing is left, and zero is a hole where two things were, the remainder after an annihilation. Read the minus as a direction and the same sum says something else. One step to the left and one step to the right return to the point from which both steps were measured, and the zero they arrive at is the origin, the one place on the line that both directions share and from which each has its sense. Nothing has been annihilated. Two journeys have come home, and both are remembered in the arriving.

Cancellation is the literalist's zero, the place where the account comes out even because everything on one side has been taken away by something on the other. Return is the Logos's zero, the place from which the account began and through which its terms stay related, so that coming out even means coming back to the shared condition with both journeys intact. Whitehead's late remark that treating half-truths as whole truths "plays the devil", which §0/1 · #3 read as one pole taking the whole span, names the same figure from another side.[^s2-whitehead] And the diabolical in the Greek sense §0/1 · #2 recovered does to persons what the literalist does to numbers. *Diabolos* is the slanderer, the one who throws something across between two people so that each appears to the other under an accusation, and slander is precisely a literal reading of a sign. A neighbour stands somewhere relative to me: to my left on some question, below me in some ledger, against me in some quarrel. That is a direction, a position on an axis we share. Slander reads the sign as a substance: the neighbour *is* the minus, his difference from me is what he is made of, and the axis that gave his position its sense has dropped out of the account. Once that reading takes hold, the only resolution it can imagine is the literalist's own. Take the minus away and the quarrel is settled at zero by the other's removal.

Apoha, which §0/1 · #2 met in Dignāga and §0 · #3 develops in full, shows in the plainest act of naming what the literalist misses.[^s2-apoha] To grasp something as a cow is to exclude what is other than a cow, *not non-cow*, and the two written negatives are there to show the word's work, which cancelling them would hide. Classical double negation is the identity: not-not-*p* is *p*, two minuses make a plus, and nothing is lost on the way. In apoha something is kept on the way, the field of what the word excludes, and keeping it is what lets differing particulars be understood together. The literalist's arithmetic is correct and his philosophy is wrong: `(−1)(−1)=+1` is true, and the claim that the passage through the two negations leaves no trace in the meaning is false. Every institution that classifies faces the same choice. A category made by exclusion carries its excluded field with it, and an account that forgets the field has not become simpler; it has become a literalist.

Collapsed dia-ballein denies its own provisionality, and the denial has a direction. Knowledge, §2 · #0 said, is a performance of trust wearing the mask of certainty, and the uncertainty a performance cannot admit has to go somewhere. In the collapsed logic it goes across the axis. The asserted `+1` feels complete because a `−1` has been found to carry what it could not hold, and the uncertainty that belongs to the shared field, to the limits of the evidence and to the trust extended before anything was proved, reappears as a property of the excluded term: the enemy is why things are unclear, the scapegoat is why things went wrong, the alien intelligence is why the future cannot be predicted. Projection is the psychological name for the movement, and it works because it relieves. A diffuse unease that belongs to everyone becomes something located over there, which can be watched, denounced, fought or expelled, and the relief is real even when the location is false. An epistemic cut has become a social act, and nobody had to decide to scapegoat anyone; it was enough to take a mask for a face.

René Girard spent his working life on the social form of that relief, and his account says why the literalist's resolution so often takes the form of a crowd and a victim. Desire, in Girard's mimetic theory, is imitative: we want what our models want, models become rivals, and rivalry spreads by imitation until a community is caught in a crisis in which everyone mirrors everyone else's hostility and no difference holds. The crisis resolves when the hostility converges, all against one, on a single victim, whose expulsion or death restores peace; the community believes in the victim's guilt, and the mechanism keeps its power only so long as its participants do not see it as a mechanism. In *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning* Girard reads the biblical name Satan, the accuser, as a name for this contagious accusation, the process that unites a crowd against one of its members, and reads the Passion narratives as the story told for once from the victim's side, which exposes the process by refusing to believe the accusation.[^s2-girard] We take from him the operation and leave him his apologetics. Read through our notation, the mimetic crisis is a polarity that has lost its zero everywhere at once: every member counts the span against every other, `+2` against `−2` in all directions, until no common respect is left. The single victim restores a zero by counterfeit. All the accusations converge on one person, who is made to stand where the shared field should be and to carry its whole incompleteness, and the crowd's `+2` is the victim's `−2` with a unanimity no ordinary quarrel ever reaches. Then the victim is removed and the account balances, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, a cancellation the crowd experiences as peace. Both collapses run in one sacrifice, appropriation in the accusation and cancellation in the expulsion, which is what §0/1 · #2 found in the representative made scapegoat and what the literalist of the minus sign would do to any neighbour if he could.[^s2-scapegoat] Girard's qualification belongs to the argument as much as his thesis. The persecutors do not experience their victim as a scapegoat. They believe the accusation, and the mechanism fails once it is named, which is why exposing it is a different act from refuting a charge: the charge may be false or true in its particulars, while the mechanism concerns where the uncertainty of the whole field has been sent.

*Agentworld* describes the same movement in our meeting with machines. "Reactive alienation," Bratton writes, "converts strangeness into threat and responds with reflexive rejection: the other must be named, registered, bounded, and held at a distance from what counts as properly ours." Its counterpart, productive alienation, "treats the non-human interlocutor as a mirror whose strangeness forces the human to see itself at an angle it could not otherwise occupy."[^s2-alienation] Bratton's two modes are the held polarity and its collapse. Productive alienation keeps the axis: the machine stands at a real distance, its strangeness is a direction we could not otherwise take, and the difference becomes a way of seeing ourselves from somewhere else, the self-knowledge §0/1 · #5→0 found in the mirror that moves first. Reactive alienation literalises the sign: the machine's difference becomes its substance, its strangeness its threat, and the only resolution imaginable is to keep it at a distance. "Both modes are live at once," Bratton adds, "and different people oscillate between them, moment to moment," and the oscillation itself tells us which logic is running.[^s2-alienation] A held polarity oscillates cleanly, each position passing into relation with the other through the stroke, as Spencer-Brown's re-entering distinction alternates in time.[^s2-spencer-brown] A collapsed one swings between `+2` and `−2`, between the conviction that the machine will save us and the conviction that it will destroy us, and never passes through the zero where the question of what it is, and what we are in relation to it, could be asked.

Dia-ballein also has a computational name, the most honest it has, because it states the method and its condition in one phrase: divide and conquer. Faced with a problem too large to solve directly, a divide-and-conquer algorithm splits it into smaller problems of the same kind, solves those, splitting again until the pieces are trivial, and combines the solutions into a solution of the whole. Merge sort is the textbook case: to put a long list in order, split it in half, sort each half by the same method, then merge the two sorted halves by walking down both together and always taking the smaller front element. Textbooks state the method in three steps, divide, conquer and combine.[^s2-cormen] Its power lies in the first two, which make an intractable task tractable by cutting it into pieces that can be handled independently. Its success lies entirely in the third. A merge sort that divided and sorted and never merged would leave a heap of small ordered lists and no ordered list at all, because the pieces were only ever pieces of one problem.

Politics keeps the first two steps and does something stranger with the third than forget it, which is why §0/1 · #4 called divide and conquer the oldest and most enduring trick of any state or power.[^s2-oldest-trick] A population divided into groups that can be addressed, managed and set against one another is far easier to govern than one that acts together, and the political version retains the combining step for whoever did the dividing while everyone else stays divided. Workers appear as individual performances, people seeking help as cases, communities as counted preferences, and the channels through which these separated people could act together run vertically, through the centre that divided them, instead of among themselves. Carlin's "big club", and the society whose directory §0/1 · #2 read, keep syn-ballein for their members and dia-ballein for everyone outside.[^s2-club] An algorithm that hid its merge from its own subproblems and then presented the merged result as something only it could have produced would be a strange algorithm and a very effective form of rule.

§0/1 · #4 added the condition on which the trick depends: it works only while both parties consent to be divided, since whichever logic is at play, it takes two to tango.[^s2-oldest-trick] The consent is no signature. It is the acceptance, by each divided party, that the axis drawn between them is the whole of their relation. Each side agrees to reckon the span from its own end and to count the distance as the other's departure, fault or debt, so that one side's `+2` meets the other's `−2` and the two together, with perfect symmetry, confirm the line on which they stand. On my reading the divided then do the divider's work for him: each needs the other as the negative through which it knows itself, each treats dissent in its own ranks as contamination, each reads the other's hardening as proof that its own must continue, and division is maintained from below out of a loyalty to one's side that is also, unnoticed, a loyalty to the axis. §0/1 · #4 named the quality of this consent a Stockholm syndrome, the hostage defending the hostage-taker, and the image fits: the divided party defends the line that holds it.[^s2-hostage] Withdrawing that consent leaves every difference standing. Some disagreements lie between a truth and a falsehood, which no appeal to shared ground should blur, and a boundary defended against an assault is not equivalent to the assault on it. Withdrawing consent means refusing to let the axis be the whole field: asking, of any opposition one is enlisted into, what field was divided so that these two now appear as mutually exclusive wholes, who drew the line and who holds the combining step, and what the two sides share that neither side's account of the other can see. Dia-ballein is answered here by its own held form, a polarity that remembers its zero and so can oppose, contest and refuse without consenting to be divided.

Martin Heidegger gave the arrested runtime its epochal name in a short address of 1955, delivered at Messkirch for the anniversary of a local composer. "There are, then, two kinds of thinking," he said, "each justified and needed in its own way: calculative thinking and meditative thinking." Calculative thinking plans, computes, reckons with given circumstances, and Heidegger characterised it by what it does not do: "Calculative thinking never stops, never collects itself."[^s2-calculative] *Never collects itself*: in our terms the calculative is the thinking that divides and conquers without the step that would gather its results back into the field from which they were taken, a runtime racing from result to result without return. Heidegger feared that "calculative thinking may someday come to be accepted and practiced as the only way of thinking," and in this section's vocabulary that is the capture of a plurality of ways of knowing by one of them, one logic occupying the zero of the whole field.[^s2-only-way] We receive the distinction and decline the quietism that sometimes travelled with it. "Each justified and needed in its own way" is Heidegger's own sentence, and it should govern how his distinction is used. Calculation reveals: the measurement of a river's flow discloses the river in a definite way, and the computational inheritance of the last century has disclosed the planet to itself at a scale no earlier culture could reach. The pathology lies where this movement has located it, in the cut that forgets the common operation and field by which its poles were produced, and in the runtime that halts before return. A calculative thinking that collected itself would go on calculating, and would also know what it was calculating for, and from what.

Dia-ballein has a crowd as well as a calculation, and the crowd is where the collapsed axis acquires its force. Gustave Le Bon, writing in 1895 from political commitments this essay does not share and with racial and evolutionary hierarchies it rejects, made one observation that outlasts the rest of his book: a crowd in the psychological sense need not be gathered in one place. "An entire nation," he wrote, "may become a crowd," and its members come to "feel, think, and act in a manner quite different" from the way each would in isolation, because the crowd is "a combination followed by the creation of new characteristics" and not an average of its members.[^s2-le-bon] What we take is the formal point: a collective can be a formation, produced across many people and acting on each of them, and it can be produced at a distance, through media and repeated influence, without anyone standing in a square.

Jung, writing after the Second World War, followed the cost of that formation to the individual. "The bigger the crowd, the more negligible the individual becomes," he wrote, and he traced how the negligibility turns political: responsibility "is collectivized as much as possible", and "the State in particular is turned into a quasi-animate personality", a being that wills, knows, protects, punishes and demands sacrifice, behind which, Jung insisted, stand the particular people who know how to operate it.[^s2-undiscovered] The State becomes a person and the persons become numbers. Here the collapsed axis shows its political mechanism: a field of relation among many people, with all its internal differences, is made to appear as one subject facing another, *us* against *them*, and each apparent subject is administered by people whose own position in the field has dropped out of view.

Mattias Desmet's account of what he calls mass formation proposes the affective preparation for that transfer: social isolation, lack of meaning, and free-floating anxiety, frustration and aggression, affect with no object adequate to it.[^s2-desmet] A narrative supplies the object, concentrating diffuse distress on a target and promising restored coherence through obedience, sacrifice or expulsion, and the resulting unity is felt as relief, because isolated people have found company in a common enemy. The relief is real and the unity is counterfeit, since what binds the crowd is a shared fixation on a term outside it, where a recovered relation among its members would have bound it from within. Desmet's critics keep the account honest. Vicky Iakovou, reviewing his book against Hannah Arendt's analysis of totalitarianism as a "novel form of government" with institutions proper to it, concludes that "the discussion of mass formation is insufficient as an explanatory model for these political occurrences."[^s2-iakovou] Susceptibility to a story does not explain who has the power to enforce it, and mass psychology cannot stand in for the analysis of institutions, parties, offices, property, coercion and the material organisation of authority. Each account corrects the other, and the full circuit runs through both registers. A population is atomised; its unbound affect is offered a target; the axis between *us* and *them* is intensified until it seems to exhaust political reality; and an institution, a party, a state or an empire presents itself as the sole mediator, protector or saviour of the field it has helped to sever. Division happens below and a counterfeit unity is installed above, and the people who could have combined among themselves are combined only through the apparatus that divided them.

None of this makes the axis false. Real differences of interest separate employers and workers, creditors and debtors, those who own housing and those who rent it, a colonising power and the people it governs, and an account that dissolved them into a vague common humanity would perform the other collapse, cancellation, with the injustice summed away. Empire and subject, institution and citizen, rich and poor are unequal positions within one social field, and their inequality is a fact to be measured. What the collapsed logic does is make each position appear self-sufficient, the rich rich by nature and the poor poor by nature, institutions standing apart from the citizens whose labour and consent sustain them, so that the relation that reproduces the inequality disappears behind the inequality itself. Held dia-ballein measures the inequality exactly and keeps the relation that produces it in view; collapsed dia-ballein measures the same inequality and reads it as a property of the people on each side.

So the literalist of the minus sign is no monster at the edge of our institutions. He is a habit inside them, and inside us, the habit of reading a position as a substance and a zero as an ending, and every classification can indulge it and every verdict can be read through it. The jurors at the table are no literalists: they know that *not guilty* is not *innocent* and that the evening in the stairwell exceeds their two boxes. The newspaper that reports their verdict may be one, and the employer who reads the record years later very likely is. Between the jury room and the employer's desk the same cut has passed from a held polarity into a collapsed one with nothing in the cut itself changed; what changed is whether the axis it was made on, the field it came from and the question it answered still travelled with it. A cut that travels with its axis carries in its own edge the shape of what it was cut from, and an edge that carries the shape of its counterpart is the first thing a token is made of. That is where syn-ballein begins.
