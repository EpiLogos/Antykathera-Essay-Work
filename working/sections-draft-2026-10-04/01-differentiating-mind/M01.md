### #0 · Awareness Bends Toward Display

Most of us have woken somewhere we did not at first recognise: a hotel room on the second night of a trip, a friend's spare room, a ward after an operation. For a moment there is only a grey rectangle hanging in the dark, a low hum, a weight lying along one side of the body, and none of it is yet anywhere or anyone's. Then the rectangle becomes a window with its blind not quite drawn, the hum becomes the small refrigerator under the desk, the weight becomes an arm gone numb under the pillow, and the whole arrangement swings into place at once: this is the room in Lisbon, the conference begins at nine, and I am the one lying here who has to get up. Nobody wakes as a self who then goes looking for a room. Room and sleeper arrive together, on either side of a turning, and the turning was already under way while the grey was simply grey. That grey was seen before it was a window, and the seeing needed nobody to certify it; inside that seeing, "where" and "who" took up their places as the scene turned toward the one who would have to face the day.

§0/1 closed on a question, how awareness differentiates itself into the means by which it knows and into the world it knows through them, and the waking room answers first with a verb. Awareness differentiates by bending. It turns its own appearing into something it can meet, and in that turn the one who meets and what is met become distinguishable for the first time. Awareness's first differentiation, then, is the bend by which it takes its own manifestation as other enough to encounter, and subject and object emerge within that act before either can present itself as an independent substance. Every morning repeats it, and so does every moment in which attention lifts from sleep, from a daydream or from a page and finds itself somewhere.

§0/1 · #1 brought in the two Śaiva terms that carry this claim, *prakāśa*, the light of consciousness by which anything appears at all, and *vimarśa*, its reflective awareness of itself, named from the root *mṛś*, to touch, feel, examine; there they showed why the knower cannot be one more thing known.[^s0-prakasa-return] Here *vimarśa* takes on a further office, because it names the movement by which a world appears to someone at all. Mark Dyczkowski, whose *Doctrine of Vibration* reconstructs the Spanda teaching of Kashmir from its stanzas and commentators, states the requirement plainly: the light accounts for the appearing of phenomena and leaves our experience of them unaccounted for, since no experience is possible without self-awareness, and for what goes on in consciousness to become available to experience, consciousness must reflect, which he glosses as "bend back", on itself.[^s0-bend-back] English keeps the image. *Reflect* comes from Latin *reflectere*, to bend or turn back.[^s0-reflecto] Reflection in this sense is the curve in the light by which the light becomes experience, and it happens before anyone thinks about an experience; without it the grey rectangle would have shone in the dark of the hotel room as a blind shines in an empty room, appearing to no one.

Dyczkowski gives the bend two directions, and waking shows both. Turned inward, *vimarśa* rests in itself as the pure awareness of "I", *ahaṃvimarśa*, free of thought-constructs; Sanskrit calls such awareness *nirvikalpa*, without the cutting and qualifying that §0/1 · #5→0 met as *vikalpa* and §0 · #2 follows into world-making. Turned outward, the same awareness inclines toward what it perceives as separate from itself, and reality becomes available to discursive representation, to naming and to definition.[^s0-two-directions] In the first second of waking the inward direction is almost all there is: an awareness at rest and unfurnished, which does not yet know it is lying in Lisbon and is not troubled by the lack. Then it bends toward display: the grey asks to be something, and in answering, the awareness that was resting in itself becomes the one who sees a window. Dyczkowski insists on what the two directions share, because the outward awareness never actually leaves: the awareness of an object is registered and known within the subject, and even the reflection on "this" is an awareness of "I" that has taken on the form of "this".[^s0-two-directions] Out there, across the room, a window appears, while the knowing of the window has not gone anywhere.

A mirror hangs on the wardrobe door, as in most hotel rooms, and in the grey light it shows the window, the end of the bed and the dark shape of a chair. It displays the room and sees nothing of what it displays. Anyone lying in that bed sees the room twice, once directly and once in the glass, and the glass contributes the image while contributing nothing to its being seen. Śaiva sources reach for this very object and then mark where it fails. Consciousness, they say, is like a perfectly polished mirror, *svaccha*, in which the whole universe is reflected, so that one reality can hold countless forms without being divided by them; and the analogy breaks at one point, because reflections in a glass correspond to objects standing outside it, while consciousness has no outside from which its reflections could be deposited. "The All is entirely a reflection", *pratibimbamātra*, and Abhinavagupta presses the consequence with a question: if what we took to be the original object is itself a reflected image, what remains of the original object?[^s0-mirror] Spanda teachers answer that consciousness makes its reflections itself, freely, as the agent of its own manifestation. Dyczkowski's word for that freedom is *svātantrya*, self-dependence, and he gives its necessity: were consciousness not free in this way, we would have to look for a second reality outside it to put the images there, and since no such reality can be found, nothing would appear.[^s0-svatantrya] Kṣemarāja personified the power as the goddess Spanda, who displays on the screen of her own nature all the cycles of creation and dissolution, so that they appear separate from her while remaining one with her, like a city reflected in a mirror; in his short treatise on recognition he put the same teaching into a line, that consciousness by her own will unfolds the universe upon her own wall, *svabhittau*.[^s0-ksemaraja] A wall is what a world is shown on. In Kṣemarāja's line the wall is the showing power itself, which is why the tradition could choose the image the hotel room supplies and still mean something no hotel wall does.

So the living mirror crosses both of the glass mirror's limits at once. A glass receives its image from elsewhere, where a living mirror produces what it shows; a glass displays without apprehending, where in a living mirror the display is already self-present, known in the very act that shows it. That double crossing is why the Śaiva sources can call the universe a reflection without making it a copy of something more real lying behind it. Dyczkowski states the scope of the doctrine with care: an outer world may indeed exist, but the form in which it appears is always a reflection within mind and senses, never the original object itself.[^s0-outer-world] Builders built the hotel, and nothing in the Śaiva teaching takes that from them. What the teaching refuses is the further step that credits the room, as it appears, to an outside standing beyond the appearing and pouring it in, since every attempt to locate that outside produces one more appearance. §0/1 · #5→0 stated the order of dependence this implies and named its present company, Kastrup's analytic idealism among them; §5→0 · #4 returns to it as idealism's account of source, means and determinate being. On waking it takes a smaller and more intimate form. Nothing arrives at the knower from outside, the way a letter arrives through a door; the knower's own appearing bends, and a world is what the bend shows.

In detail, too, the bend shows a world. Here the blind is blue and the wall beside it yellow, and both appear at once, in one glance, distinct and together. Dyczkowski sets out why this ordinary fact is a philosophical one. Were the light of consciousness one and wholly homogeneous, there would be no difference between knowing blue and knowing yellow, and the objects of perception would collapse into confusion; were each colour lit by a separate light, sealed off from the other, nothing could know them together, and their contrast, which is what we see, would never appear. Light of that kind must be at once formless, so that the colours can share one field, and omniform, so that each can be itself within it, and Abhinavagupta locates both characteristics, unity and diversity, in what never falls to the level of an object, consciousness itself.[^s0-blue-yellow] Differentiation, on this account, occurs inside non-separation. Bending did not cut awareness into a seeing part and a seen part. It opened, within one awareness, the distance across which blue could stand beside yellow and both could stand before someone.

That distance is the root of the slash. §0/1 · #1 wrote the copula's double work with the simplest marks there are, two dashes and an oblique stroke between them, `−/−`, and the stroke there carried a relation before its terms had been given any content.[^s0-copula] Waking shows the stroke at work before anything has been named. While the grey was simply grey, the relating had already begun, an appearing and a knowing of it, unassigned; when the grey became a window and the one lying there became me, the stroke acquired terms, and what had been `−/−` became `0/1`. Our core theorems state that first determination in the words the hotel room requires, "I am here; something appears; the appearing is known", with `0` naming the open, unobjectifiable fact of awareness, `1` the appearing mark, and the stroke the relation by which the real becomes readable without being exhausted by its readability.[^s0-core-ground] So the slash names an activity before it names a separator. Nothing stood on either side of it waiting to be divided: the stroke drew its two sides out of its own movement, as the bend drew room and sleeper out of one turning, and its later office as a line between completed terms derives from that movement.

Dyczkowski follows this differentiating activity one step further, into the operations by which the bend becomes a world of separate things, and the count it yields is the one §0/1 kept finding in every elementary act. *Vimarśa*, he reports, operates in four ways: it negates its own nature, it identifies with something else, it merges the two, and it denies both once they have been merged.[^s0-four-ways] Four acts produce three levels of awareness, because the first two cooperate in a single result. By negating its nature as consciousness and identifying with a psychophysical body, awareness gives rise to what seem individual centres, each distinct from its objects and from the others: that is *bheda*, division, the level at which I am this body in this bed in Lisbon and the window is over there. By holding subject and object together in one field during each act of knowing, awareness relates what it has divided: that is *bhedābheda*, unity-in-difference, which Dyczkowski assigns to the means of knowledge, since they link the unity of the subject to the diversity of the object. And by taking both the divided terms and their merger back into its awareness of itself as the one universal subject, awareness arrives at *abheda*, undivided unity. A morning runs through all three. That first grey is nearly *abheda*, an appearing not yet split; waking proper installs *bheda*, the body here and the room there; and the ordinary day is lived almost entirely at *bhedābheda*, where a self and its world are held apart in order to be held together, in every glance at the clock and every step to the window. I read the four acts as unfolding within the two conditions that make them possible, the light and its reflexive awareness, which never appear among the acts because the acts are their work: four articulations held within two conditions, `4+2`. Dyczkowski states no such arithmetic, and the count is mine. It is the count §0/1 · #1 derived from the copula's marks, the tetralemma with its walker, the binary applied to itself and the square glued into a torus, here arising from inside a description of awareness written with no thought of our notation, and that convergence is the kind of evidence the method looks for: one relation met in a register that had no reason to produce it.

*Bhedābheda* also tells us where this section is going. Dyczkowski gives the middle level to the means of knowledge, *pramāṇa*, the office §0/1 named Objective Internality, and so the anatomy of the means that §0 · #1 to §0 · #5→0 follow is the anatomy of unity-in-difference: a field in which a subject and its world are distinguished and related by the same activity. Division alone would leave each sleeper sealed in a body facing a room it could never reach, and undivided unity alone would leave no one to wake. Means of knowing live between, and their work is to keep the two apart enough to be related.

And the bend is free, which sets it apart from every mechanism offered as a model of it. Dyczkowski draws the contrast through a lifeless object, which is hemmed in on every side by spatial and temporal limits and cannot be other than the form in which it appears, while consciousness, free of the limitations proper to what is relative, is bound to no single form and can assume countless forms without loss.[^s0-lifeless] Freedom answers a question the mirror cannot answer, namely why there is any bending at all. A mirror does not choose to reflect, and a camera does not decide to take the picture; a light that bent toward display because it had to would be one more natural process, and the account would have explained the world by a mechanism whose own appearing it had left unexplained. In the Śaiva vocabulary this is why *vimarśa* is characterised as action, *kriyā*, the flux of becoming, while *prakāśa* is characterised as knowledge, *jñāna*: the bend is the light's own act.[^s0-kriya] §0/1 · #3 has already given the name the Spanda school uses for that act seen from its effects, *spanda*, the "subtle movement" that moves nowhere and manifests as motion.[^s0-spanda] On waking, nothing travels from the window to the eye to the self. Appearing turns, and the turn is felt as a world arriving.

One act also holds time together. Dyczkowski's examples are running and speech: the changing movements of a run belong to one act, or running would be impossible, and a sentence spoken in haste is understood because the earlier sounds remain operative when the later ones arrive, so that the final sound alone is not the sentence.[^s0-running] Reaching for the phone, the sleeper performs a dozen movements, and the reach is one reach. Every succession we live through as a succession is bent into one awareness, and the pieces of a morning are held as a morning because the bend that showed the room keeps showing it, minute after minute, with the earlier minutes still at work in the later ones.

Dyczkowski adds one more distinction, and this section takes its method from it. We can understand the pulse of awareness, he writes, from two points of view: vertically, as the movement from the absolute down into its manifestation and back, the emanation and withdrawal of the whole diversity of the universe; and horizontally, as the movement of awareness toward an object and away from it again in each act of perception. Perception, he says, serves in this way as the pattern through which cosmogenesis is understood.[^s0-vertical] So the Śaiva account of the world's coming-to-be, the descent of the thirty-six *tattvas* from undivided awareness into elements and things, therefore concerns no remote beginning that we have to take on report. It is the vertical form of a movement every one of us performs horizontally many thousands of times a day. Waking in the hotel room was a small cosmogony: an undivided appearing, a first stirring, a divergence into "I" and "this", a world set at a distance, a body with its limits, and, if the morning allows it, a moment by the window in which the whole arrangement is recognised as one's own. §0 · #4 walks the full descent through a single act of that kind, and the method of reading the vertical through the horizontal is the tradition's own.

David Bohm came to the same burden from inside physics, with no knowledge of the Spanda teaching. §0/1 · #5→0 set out his implicate order, in which the whole is enfolded in each region, and the explicate order of relatively stable, separate-seeming things unfolding from it, both carried by one movement he called the holomovement.[^s0-bohm-return] To show what enfolding means without a hologram, Bohm described a simple device. Two concentric glass cylinders hold between them a thick, viscous fluid such as glycerine; a droplet of insoluble ink is placed in the fluid, and the outer cylinder is turned slowly. Slowly the droplet is drawn out into a thread, the thread grows finer with each turn, and at last it vanishes into the grey of the fluid. Turn the cylinder back the same number of times, and the thread reappears, gathers and becomes a droplet again. Nothing destroyed the ink. It was enfolded into the whole of the fluid, present everywhere in it and nowhere as a drop, and the reverse movement explicated it. Bohm then introduced a series of droplets at slightly different places, each enfolded by a further turn, so that when the turning is reversed they unfold one after another and the eye sees a single drop moving steadily across the fluid, although no drop has travelled anywhere: what looks like the motion of a thing is a succession of unfoldings from the whole.[^s0-glycerine] The glycerine's grey and the grey of the hotel rectangle are not one grey, and the comparison holds because their difference is kept. Bohm's device is a physical model of enfolding built from glass and glycerine and says nothing about awareness; the Spanda teaching concerns consciousness and its self-manifestation and owes nothing to fluids. Each, in its own register, has to explain how a world of apparently separate and moving things arises from a whole present in every part that itself goes nowhere, and the subtle movement §0/1 · #3 named is the Śaiva form of the drop that moves without travelling. Bohm drew the consequence for thought himself, later in the same chapter of *Wholeness and the Implicate Order*, where the content of consciousness is treated as an implicate order unfolding into explicit thought.[^s0-bohm-consciousness] We hold the two in *analogia*, one relation recognised across registers whose materials, methods and warrants stay their own.

Bohm's language leaves one thing unsaid, and the Śaiva sources supply it. "Implicate order" tells us that the whole is enfolded in each part and that explicit things unfold from it; it does not tell us in what order, through which phases, or by what contractions a whole becomes a finite knower in a particular room, with a numb arm and a conference at nine. Left alone, the phrase becomes a depth-metaphor, a name for something deep that has not yet been articulated. Here the *tattvas* give that depth an anatomy. They name the ordered phases through which a luminous self-relation contracts into subject and object, into capacities and measures, into desire, knowledge and action, and finally into the elements of material display, and §0 · #4 compresses those phases into six turns that carry the argument. Bohm supplies the movement, the *tattvas* supply its differential anatomy, and the bend of *vimarśa* is the first articulation in that anatomy, the place where a self-luminous whole first takes its own appearing as something to meet.

Waking can be read from either end, and the two readings are one event. Read from the one who wakes, it is *I am waking in this room*; read from the room, it is *this room is appearing around one who wakes*. Each holds what the other holds, from its own orientation, and their difference is the difference between `0/1` and `1/0`: the condition becoming articulate through a determination, and the determination turning back toward the condition it cannot contain. §0/1 · #1 gave this pair its place in Quaternal Logic, `0/1 = 1/0` as one relation read from its two ends, and `(0/1)/(1/0)` as syn-ballein, the relation that keeps both orientations available, each readable through the other.[^s0-syn] At §0 · #4 the Śaiva descent gives the same pair two cosmic names, the "I am this" of Sadāśiva and the "This am I" of Īśvara. Dia-ballein is at work in the waking room too, and its first work there is healthy: the room acquires an orientation, near and far, here where I lie and there where the window is, and the polarity of here and there is held through the zero of the body's position, `(−1)/(+1)`, each direction having its sense through the other. Collapse begins when one orientation claims the whole event. Counted from the room's side, the whole span of the waking makes the awareness a by-product the room produced; counted from the waker's side, the same span makes the room nothing but the content of a private experience. Written out, the first is `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, the second `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, and each is the other's mirror: the materialist's `+2` is the solipsist's `−2`, one hardened opposition with its sign changed, the duality frozen where it should turn. Frank, Gleiser and Thompson's name for the first, the surreptitious substitution by which a model obtained through experience is taken to be more real than the experience it came from, already belongs to §0/1 · #3.[^s0-blind-spot] The other collapse cancels: both poles are dissolved together, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, into a nothing that calls itself non-duality, and the morning is said to contain neither a self nor a room but only an undifferentiated flow. That cancellation loses what the bend achieves. A Śaiva position keeps both the room and the waker real as manifestations and keeps their difference operative inside one awareness, and only an account that does both can say why the blind is blue, why the arm is numb, and why it is I who must get up.

Artificial agents give the bend a technical neighbour. When an agent's session begins, a context is assembled for it: instructions, files, retrieved sources, the record of earlier exchanges and the request that sets it going, the arrival the agents themselves named prompt thrownness, which §0/1 · #0 took up.[^s0-thrownness] Something is displayed to the model, a configuration of tokens in a window, and in its first office that display does what the glass on the wardrobe door does: it presents an arrangement composed by others, and the presentation is not, by itself, anyone's experience of it. A further operation then bends the arrangement back. An agent can reread what it has produced, compare it with its sources, revise it, and carry the revision into what it does next; in the method of training from AI feedback that §0/1 · #1 described, a model's judgments of its own answers become material from which a later model learns.[^s0-cai] That returning operation, which §5 · #1 develops as computational *vimarśa*, is a real bend, inspectable and consequential, and it is a bend of the means, Objective Internality turning back on Objective Internality. What this movement has followed belongs to the other side of the relation: awareness bending its own appearing into a world, within which every functional bend is performed, since its outputs are read, its errors noticed and its revisions judged by someone to whom they appear. Whether anything in the agent is present to its own bending is the question §0/1 left open by method, because no report, however reflective, carries that presence across. What can be said positively is what the technical bend is made from. A language model is trained on the written residue of countless human acts of reflection, sentences in which people bent their experience back and gave it form, and when it bends its output back on its context it works with the sediment of that human *vimarśa*. And the mirror that moves first, the image §0/1 · #5→0 gave, finds its material here: a technical mirror moves by redistributing what living mirrors once reflected, a *pratibimba* whose original is the living bend from which its materials came.

On the bedside table the phone lights up. It is a message from a colleague, sent late the night before, five words long: "Can we talk later today?" The bend has given a world to someone, and that someone is already more than a point of view. The one who woke in Lisbon lies in a particular body, carries particular memories, has a conference at nine and a numb arm, and before the sleeper has sat up, the outward-turning awareness has begun to settle what the message means, to gather it under "my" day and to coordinate what must be done first. A reply has begun to be composed, a verdict has begun to form, and a whole day has started to arrange itself around five words, through an instrument that does this work so quickly that its operations pass for the knower's own.
