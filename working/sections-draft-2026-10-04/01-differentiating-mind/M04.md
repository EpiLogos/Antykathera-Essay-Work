### #3 · Apoha: Determination through Exclusion

A child of about eighteen months has learned a word and is delighted with it. "Dog!" she says, pointing at the neighbour's spaniel, and is praised. "Dog!" she says at the cat on the wall, and is told, gently, no, that's a cat. On a walk in the country she points across a field and announces "Dog!" at a cow, and then at a sheep, and then, with less confidence, at a horse, and each time a parent says no, and names the animal. Within a few weeks her word has changed. Nobody has given her a definition of a dog, and she could not understand one if they had. She has received a series of exclusions, not the cat, not the cow, not the sheep, each offered by someone she trusts, and the word has acquired its reach from them. Her "dog" now covers the spaniel, the neighbour's terrier and a picture-book dog drawn in three lines, and it stops short of the cow. Some months later she points at a picture in a book and asks, "Is a wolf a dog?", and her question is a good one, because it is asked at the edge her exclusions have drawn. Every parent has watched some version of this, and every one of us has lived it, in our first words and then again, much later, in finer fields, learning that this wine is not that one, that this birdsong belongs to a wren and not a robin, that what someone did was rude and not merely brusque.

Every cut the children made in their castle at §0 · #2 left something out, and the child with her word shows what the remainder does at the smallest scale there is. Her "dog" means what it means through what she has been taught it is not, and the cow, the sheep and the horse stay active inside the word when she says it of a spaniel and none of them is in sight. §0/1 · #2 met this structure in the Buddhist theory of *apoha*, exclusion, which Dignāga set out early in the sixth century, in which to apprehend something as a cow is to exclude what is other than a cow, so that the term is articulated as *not non-cow*; it set apoha beside Spinoza's negation and drew the first consequence, that the cut is productive, a condition of anything being intelligible at all.[^s0-apoha-return] Apoha now receives its own force, and the Buddhist and Śaiva traditions, agreeing on so much of it, part company at the one point where I have to take a side.

Dignāga was answering a problem every theory of meaning eventually meets. A general word such as *cow* applies to many animals, no two alike, and the universal it seems to name, cowness, is no separately existing thing that each cow could share; if there are no such universals, what does the word hook onto? Tom Tillemans gives Dignāga's answer in Dignāga's own formulation, from his *Analysis of Universals*: "A word talks about entities only as they are qualified by the negation of other things."[^s0-dignaga-sep] The word reaches every cow by excluding the non-cows. Its extension is delivered by the cut, and no shared essence has to be posited for the cuts to trace. Dharmakīrti, writing a century or more later, turned the semantic account toward causes. Only particulars, unique and momentary, are fully real for him, because only they act; the general objects of concepts are constructions, and exclusion classes are grounded in the capacity of particulars to produce shared judgments and serve shared purposes, so that *cow* gathers the animals that do, for those who use the word, what cows do: give milk, pull a plough, wander into the road.[^s0-dharmakirti] Joerg Tuske's review of the modern collection on apoha puts the difference between the two founders in a pair of sentences: "The top-down version explains how we can refer to kind terms, i.e., terms that go beyond the particular. The bottom-up version, on the other hand, provides an account of how general concepts are formed out of the perception of particulars."[^s0-tuske] The child's "dog" grew in both directions at once. Its boundary was drawn by exclusions from above, offered in a language older than she is, and the exclusions followed what the animals did for her from below, the spaniel that came when called and wanted to be stroked, the cow that stood behind a fence and did neither. Dyczkowski's exposition of the Spanda teaching uses Dharmakīrti's own term, *arthakriyā*, efficacy, for the outer face of every phenomenon, its behaviour and properties, the aspect that distinguishes one object from another and makes it available to conceptual representation.[^s0-arthakriya] Up to here the Buddhist and Śaiva accounts agree: concepts carve a world by what things do, and the carving is done through exclusion.

Double negation is the part of apoha most easily misread. In ordinary propositional logic, denying a denial returns the original truth value, and "it is not the case that this is not a cow" says no more than "this is a cow". Scholarship collected in *Apoha: Buddhist Nominalism and Human Cognition* distinguishes two negations at work in *not non-cow*. A verbally bound negation denies a predication, and two of them cancel. A nominally bound negation forms a term, *non-cow*, whose content is the exclusion itself, and negating that term does not collapse back into a bare positive, because the resulting term carries the structure of what it was formed against.[^s0-two-negations] This is exclusion that remembers, a phrase §5 · #1 develops. Her word remembers the cow, not as a list of everything that is not a dog, which no word could carry, but as a boundary that can be found again when it matters, at the edge of the wolf. Dignāga's tradition keeps the positive side of the point as firmly as the negative: the word reaches a real thing qualified by exclusion and not a mere exclusion, as the fragment §0/1 · #2 quoted from Pind's translation says.[^s0-apoha-return] A real animal, the spaniel, is reached by the word. Exclusion makes the reaching possible, and the word reaches the thing; what it never reaches is a self-sufficient essence of dog, standing behind the animal and owning its dogness.

Her parent's "no" also needs sorting, because English uses one small word for several operations that apoha keeps apart. When her parent says the cow is *not* a dog, the word is being refused an application: that is apoha's exclusion, semantic non-application, and it gives the word its reach. When the spaniel is *not* in the room, something is absent, and absence concerns presence, not meaning; the word "dog" works perfectly well in the spaniel's absence. When she later sets dog against wolf, she draws a contrast, a relation between two determinations each of which already has its reach. And when, years later, someone who was bitten as a child cannot let the word "dog" near certain memories, an exclusion of another kind is at work, the psychic exclusion psychology calls repression, with its own source and mechanism. A fifth operation is the one this section is most concerned to name. A difference can be achieved and then lose the relation that made it, so that the excluded field no longer returns to judgment at all: that is severance, and successful exclusion does not perform it. A word that has excluded the cow still knows the cow is there to be excluded. A word that has severed the cow has forgotten that it was ever cut, and §0 · #2's Horizon case showed what severance looks like in an institution, a figure that went on meaning *the branch is short* because the alternative, *the software is wrong*, had been severed from it.[^s0-horizon-return]

Buddhist teaching carries the same structure out of semantics and into the account of everything that arises. *Pratītya-samutpāda*, dependent arising, holds that whatever comes to be does so through conditions, and Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* draws the consequence in the verse the Madhyamaka tradition treats as its centre, in Jay Garfield's translation: "Whatever is dependently co-arisen / That is explained to be emptiness. / That, being a dependent designation, / Is itself the middle way."[^s0-mmk] The spaniel is empty in this sense, empty of any existence of its own apart from the conditions that make it this dog, its parents, its food, its owners, the air it breathes, and apart from the exclusions through which it is grasped as a dog. Apoha is the semantic face of that ontological claim: what a word means depends on what it excludes, and what a thing is depends on what conditions it. Each doctrine keeps its own object, one concerning reference and the other occurrence, and neither reduces to the other. What they share is the refusal of self-subsistence.

That refusal has a positive side, which I state as an equation of my own, argued from the operation and attributed to no one school: ***śūnyatā* is *pūrṇatā***, emptiness is fullness. An appearance emptied of being its own independent source does not vanish. It becomes available as the expression of all the relations and conditions it concealed while it seemed self-standing. So the spaniel loses an imaginary autonomy and gains a world, the breeding, the household, the walks, the child who points at it. And the word *dog* loses an essence and gains a field, the cats and cows and horses it was cut against and the uses it serves. Dyczkowski's exposition uses *pūrṇatā* for the fullness of consciousness, which contains within itself the infinite variety of things, and the symbolon of §0/1 · #1 already wrote the relation: its determinate half is empty of exhausting the whole and full of the whole's trace.[^s0-purnata] In my reading notes on Buddhist psychology I put the matter in the terms of the tetralemma: apoha and the tetralemma both point, once every positive and negative position has been exhausted, to a silence that is "a paradoxical assertion, emptiness as fullness".[^s0-notes-silence] Apoha, read this way, is the door through which the whole comes into every part, since each determination, understood through what it excludes, opens onto the fullness of its constitutive field.

Chinese Huayan Buddhism gave that fullness its most famous image, and it deserves a full telling, because the telling does the reasoning. Its contemplation, preserved in a work conventionally attributed to Dushun (557–640), the *Huayan wujiao zhiguan*, begins with a question about knowledge, how phenomena that arise through conditions can be known at all, and answers with a net. Through the palace of the god Indra hangs a net with a jewel at every knot, and because the jewels are clear, each reflects all the others, reflections passing into reflections, layer upon layer, all appearing at once in a single jewel.[^s0-dushun-text] Then the text does something no mere image would do. It asks the reader to choose: take one jewel, at the southwest edge of the net, and test it. All the others appear in it, and each of those reflects the whole, including the southwest jewel, so that following any reflection brings the starting point back into view inside the field it opened. And it presses the logic with an interlocutor. How can one enter all the jewels while staying in this one? In my rendering of the Chinese, the answer is that "only because one does not go out of this jewel can one enter all the jewels; if one went out of this one jewel to enter all the jewels, one could not enter all the jewels", because apart from this jewel there is no other jewel.[^s0-dushun-enter] Remaining here is the condition of reaching everywhere. If the many are present in the one, the interlocutor objects, why speak of a net of many jewels at all? Because, the text replies, "the one is the total aspect, made complete by the many": take away this jewel and there is no net, and take away the many and there is no one.[^s0-dushun-total]

Then the contemplation gives its proof, and the proof is an experiment. "Only put an ink dot on the one jewel at the southwest edge," it says, "and when the one jewel is marked, there are ink dots everywhere in the ten directions."[^s0-dushun-ink] Nobody went round marking every jewel; one local act appeared throughout the net, and beginning from any other jewel would show the same. And then, at the height of its demonstration, the text turns on its own image. "The dharmas are not like this," it says: the simile is as good as no simile, spoken only for a partial resemblance, because jewels only take one another's images in while their substances remain distinct, whereas the dharmas interpenetrate in their whole substance.[^s0-dushun-limit] So the image teaches where its likeness ends, and the teaching is the stronger for it, because a reader who took the optical net for the reality would have learned a fact about mirrors. Bryan Van Norden and Nicholaos Jones give the doctrine in one sentence, that "each thing that exists is dependent for both its existence and its identity upon every other thing that exists", and they add that in the Huayan account each thing keeps its distinctness inside this mutual inclusion.[^s0-vannorden] Her word hangs in such a net. It is one jewel among all the words she will learn, lit by every other word that bounds it, and it remains this word, said with this delight, of this dog. Her later question about the wolf is the ink dot: one mark at one edge, and the whole field of her words shifts to receive it.

Apoha and the net meet without merging. Apoha concerns how a term means, the net concerns how anything is, and the Huayan contemplation adds what neither apoha nor dependent arising states, recursive mutual inclusion: choosing a definite jewel keeps its constituting field active, and the field returns through the chosen jewel. Read through our notation, the southwest jewel is a `1` in the precise sense §0/1 · #1 gave the mark, a definite something that arrives through multiplicity, and the net is the All that `1` writes; what the text calls the total aspect, the one made complete by the many, is the relation `0/1` read from its manifest side. An ink dot is the return written as an act: a mark made here appears everywhere, and everywhere returns it here. §2 · #3 sets this net beside another, the bonds Hephaestus forged to catch Ares and Aphrodite, and compares the two as two ways of holding what they hold.

Buddhist and Śaiva readings of apoha run together up to this point, and they divide over what apoha requires of the one who performs it. Buddhist epistemologists treat experience as a stream of momentary events, each arising from conditions and perishing, and treat the knower who seems to persist through them as one more construction of the stream; the critique of the self-thing that §0 · #1 kept in force comes from here, and as an account of how a contracted experience is put together it is unsurpassed. Śaiva philosophers in Kashmir granted that account its truth about objects and refused its extension to the knower. Dyczkowski reports that the Spanda teachers accept the doctrine of momentariness only as it applies to outer objectivity, and Kṣemarāja states the reason: the Lord creates and destroys only the objective aspect of the perceiver, the body and its kind, and not the subjective aspect, because were the subject to waver as the world is emanated and absorbed, there would be no one to whom emanation and absorption could appear.[^s0-momentariness] Her word shows why this matters for apoha in particular. To exclude the cow from *dog*, she has to hold cow and dog together in one act of knowing: the dog present, the cow absent but available, the boundary between them grasped as a boundary. A series of momentary cognitions, each perishing before the next arises, could not hold the excluded alternative together with what it excludes; something must keep *this* and *not-that* in one awareness for the exclusion to be performed. Pratyabhijñā philosophers made this argument their own: they took exclusion, *apohana*, out of the Buddhist account of concepts and gave it back to consciousness as one of its powers, so that the cut by which a word means is itself an act of the one awareness that holds both sides of the cut.[^s0-apohana]

Wherever the instrument's work is examined the same requirement appears, and its pressures can be counted. Recollection returns as *I saw*, and a stream with no continuity of knowing could not own what it recalls. Comparison must hold the remembered tone together with the present one, as the morning of §0 · #1 held last week's slight beside today's message. Exclusion must hold the cow beside the dog. Action must keep its intention through the steps that carry it out, so that the result can be recognised as fulfilling it, and Dyczkowski's running and hasty sentence, which §0 · #0 met, are the plainest examples. And arising and perishing can appear only to a knowing that does not arise and perish in the same way, since the perishing of a sound is heard by someone who did not perish with it. None of these five pressures restores a self-thing. They place the knower where the Buddhist critique of objects cannot reach, as the knowing in which objects, their absence and their succession appear.[^s0-five-pressures]

My comparative judgment follows, and it is argued. By the criterion of wholeness §0/1 set, which asks whether an account can keep both the differentiation and the unity in which the differences are known, the Śaiva articulation is the more complete for the task at hand. It keeps the Buddhist destruction of every self-subsistent determination, including the determination that would make a self-thing of the knower, and it gives positive places to what the Buddhist analysis leaves underarticulated wherever its critique of objects is taken to settle the status of the subject: the knowing in which exclusion is performed, the power by which constructions are made, the recognition by which a contracted life can know its source. §0 · #2 found the Śaiva sources holding that the power of construction is the real power of consciousness and not one more construction; apoha shows the same power at its most elementary, in the act of holding a cow and a dog apart in one awareness so that a word can mean. My notes record the judgment with its debt intact, that the Spanda teachers "argue very well *for* anatta and yet also *for* a positive Siva": no self-thing, and a knower.[^s0-notes-anatta]

Apoha also opens the relation §0/1 · #1 called Mono and Poly onto knowledge. There Mono named the One that `0` writes, self-identity, and Poly the manifest All that `1` writes, self-difference, and §0/1 followed the two offices through theology, economy and power.[^s0-mono-poly] Here they take an epistemic form. Apoha empties every part of self-subsistence, since no determination contains the grounds of its own intelligibility while the excluded field stays constitutive of what it means. Poly then names the many real differentiations of a relational whole, each constituted through relations it did not create, the dog through the cow, the word through the net of words. Mono names the whole's structural priority, the field through which anything can become a part, and it is no supreme object standing behind its expressions, since a field of exclusions is not one more item in the field. Whole and many are given together in every act of naming, and the child who learns "dog" learns both at once, one word and the world of words it belongs to. A slash between them carries the two readings of AND/OR that §0/1 · #1 drew from the copula, and my notes say how they divide the work: "The AND ensures the poly, the OR enforces the mono, the "/" between AND and OR ensures the One and Many".[^s0-notes-andor] Every word is singled out (OR: this, not that) and held among the others (AND: this, with all that bounds it), and the slash keeps both readings live.

She learned her word on trust, and the dependence reaches everything that follows. Each exclusion came from someone she relied on, before she could check any of them, and the word's boundaries are a deposit of that reliance. §0/1 · #2 defined trust as already-enacted dependence and knowledge as the aperture through which the dependence can be understood.[^s0-trust] Apoha shows the dependence at the root of meaning. A word is spoken because a field of shared differences is trusted enough to carry it; a gesture is made because a world is assumed coherent enough to answer it. Analysis can decompose and recompose that prior coherence and refine its boundaries, as the question about the wolf refines her "dog", and it never manufactures the coherence from nothing, because every analysis is performed in words whose boundaries were first received in trust. Phenomenal existence rests on that unspoken trust, and apoha is where it can be seen working.

Every selection also leaves a remainder, and here apoha becomes an ethical and political problem. A word, a judgment, a classification or a decision depends on a field of alternatives it did not select, and an account of the selection that forgets the field presents the selected term as if it had arrived without alternatives. Each logic names what follows. The exclusion itself is healthy dia-ballein: dog and not-dog held on one axis through the zero of the field of animals, `(−1)/(+1)`, each side having its sense through the other, the polarity the child keeps alive every time she asks about a wolf. Cancellation drops the field: *not non-cow* summed to a bare *cow*, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, a positive term with its formation erased, which is how a fluent passage loses its source. Appropriation lets the term claim the field, and politics supplies its purest form. Carl Schmitt held that the specific distinction of the political, to which political actions and motives can be reduced, is that between friend and enemy, and on my reading the friend in that distinction is defined by nothing except the enemy it excludes.[^s0-schmitt] A term made determinate entirely by exclusion counts the whole span from its own pole: everything that is ours on one side, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and the excluded pole receiving the same span as its debt, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, the enemy as the negative through which the friend knows itself. And each is the other's mirror, because the enemy's own reckoning runs the same subtraction from the other end, and each side's `+2` is the other's `−2`. Bratton's "humanity of the gaps", which §0/1 · #0 met at the bedside, is the same appropriation turned on a species: *human* defined as whatever machines cannot yet do, a term made determinate by exclusion against a remainder that moves.[^s0-gaps] Apoha explains why that defence is lost before it begins. A term determined only by what it excludes depends on the excluded field for its meaning, and when the field moves, the term moves with it, so the human term shrinks with every capacity a machine acquires, until a species is defending the last corner of a word. Syn-ballein holds the two as a symbolon instead, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, human and machine each read through the other, the difference kept and the belonging recognised, so that the excluded remains constitutive of the term and the term can acknowledge what it owes to what it excludes.

Artificial systems make apoha's selection operational: a model scores its alternatives, normalises the scores into a distribution and selects one, and whether the field it selected from remains available afterwards is a matter of design, which §5 · #1 follows into its mechanics with the semantic and soteriological differences kept in view.[^s0-selection]

Her question about the wolf is the last case here and the most important. Her word has a boundary; she has found the place where the boundary is unsure, and she asks. That is exclusion working as it should: the selected term returns with the alternatives that made it answerable, and an edge case reopens the field instead of being forced to one side. Asked of Horizon's figures by anyone with authority to hear it, the same question would have been the ink dot that showed the whole net.
