## §1 — The Return of Zero

<a id="M13"></a>

### #0 · A Sign Migrates Between Worlds

A child at the kitchen table is asked to write one hundred and five, and she writes 1005. She has put down what she heard: a hundred, which she already knows as 100, and then a five. The parent at her elbow sees the mistake at once and finds it surprisingly hard to explain. The hundred, the explanation goes, already has its places inside it, and the five belongs in the last of them, so the middle zero is no longer the hundred's zero at all; it stands for the tens, and there are no tens. Then why write anything there? Because without it the 1 slides into the tens' place, 105 becomes 15, and a hundred has shrunk to a ten without anyone touching it. She goes back to the page with new respect for the small circle, and perhaps a suspicion that the adults have been keeping something from her. Studies of how children turn spoken numbers into digits find this error, a number written as its spoken parts laid end to end, among the commonest of the early school years.[^s1-seron-fayol] Many of us made some version of it once, and some of us have since sat at the elbow of the one making it.

What she has met is a sign for no counted thing that changes what is counted. The zero in 105 adds nothing to the sum, since there are no tens to add, and yet the hundred is worth a hundred only because that zero keeps it in the third place from the right. Take the zero out and every mark to its left changes value while nothing has been added or removed. The circle counts nothing and positions everything. With it, absence stops being a blank outside the count and becomes a position inside it, and that is the event this section follows through history. Every number we write rests on it, and so does every calculation a machine performs.

Within a year she will read 105 at a glance, and the work of the small circle will have passed from her attention into the way she reads. §0/1 defined Objective Internality as the means through which a life knows and acts within a world, and said that a received rule joins those means by becoming a condition of receiving again, as a remembered rebuke shapes how an innocent question is heard. Place value has made the same move here, from the page into the reader. It is objective, because she could state it if asked, and internal, because she now reads through it without looking at it. The history that follows runs that movement in the other direction. What readers of numbers carried silently in themselves was slowly converted into marks that could be written down and sent to readers who did not share their training, and the child's year at the kitchen table takes the converted result back into a reader.

§0/1 closed on a promise. It had followed counting into the layer beneath naming, the *this one, another one, this one again* by which two parents keep hold of an unnamed child, and it wrote `0` for the pole that cannot enter its own count as one more item, the knowing to which every counted thing appears. Our work, it said, means to echo zero's own advent, making the uncounted condition explicit within the count. A promise of that kind is a loan. §0/1 borrowed zero as a sign for the Subject before showing where the sign came from or what it could carry, and the account stays open until the sign's history has been told and the debt repaid. Meanwhile §0 has followed awareness as it differentiates into the means through which it knows and the world it knows through them, and every determination it traced was selected from a field it did not make and could not exhaust. A determination leaves a zero behind it, the field it was cut from and the act that cut it, and that zero can be recovered where human beings first learned to write it down, in the history of number.

What we now call zero gathered several distinct inventions into one sign, each made for a different problem in a different practice. A blank can keep a place open in a column. A separator can mark an empty position inside a written number. A digit can stand among the other digits of a positional system, a number can enter arithmetic and obey rules of addition and multiplication, an empty set can serve as the first object of a formal construction, and an origin can fix the point from which coordinates are measured. Each is a different office, a permission granted to the mark by the practice that receives it, and the circle in 105 now performs several at once. They arrived separately, across more than two thousand years and in at least five traditions of reckoning, and the sign gained its power by migrating, collecting offices as it crossed from one practice to another. Call all of them "zero" from the start and the history that made the sign disappears; keep them apart and absence can be watched acquiring its powers one at a time.

The story begins in Mesopotamia, where scribes wrote numbers in base sixty with a wedge-shaped stylus on wet clay. Their notation was positional, as ours is: the same sign could stand for one, for sixty, or for three thousand six hundred, according to where it stood in the number. Old Babylonian scribes of the early second millennium BCE had no sign for an empty place. Where a position was vacant they might leave a little more space, or nothing, and a reader inferred the value from the calculation in hand, which usually made it clear. A modern transcription shows what that cost. Write the places as a sequence, and $(1,0,1)$ means

$$
1\times60^{2}+0\times60+1=3601,
$$

while $(1,1)$ means $1\times60+1=61$. If the scribe leaves no mark for the empty middle place the two numbers can look alike on the tablet, and only the problem being solved tells the reader which was meant. In the last centuries BCE, scribes of the Seleucid period began to write a separator, two small slanting wedges, where a place inside a number was empty.[^s1-babylon] It was a real achievement with a real limit. It stood only between other signs and never at the end of a number, so the scale of the whole figure, whether it meant 1 or 60 or 1/60, still depended on context, and nobody added to it, multiplied by it or treated it as a quantity. It held a place open, and that was all it did.

A scribe's difficulty here is the difficulty of context itself. A mark whose value depends on position needs a reader who knows what the position is, and for a thousand years Babylonian mathematics left that knowledge in the reader, so that the context of the calculation did the work a written zero would later do. With the separator a little of that context moved onto the tablet. In the terms of §0/1's sixfold, a part of what had been held at **#4**, the whole situation in which a mark is read, was written down as a mark, so that a reader who did not share the scribe's situation could still recover the value. Something a practitioner carries tacitly, in memory and circumstance, becomes again and again a sign that travels without its practitioner.

Not every number system needs a zero, and seeing why makes the history of zero a history of position. Roman numerals add: CCLXX is a hundred, a second hundred, fifty, ten and ten, each sign means the same wherever it stands, and a smaller sign written before a larger is subtracted from it by convention. A system of that kind has no places and so no empty places, and a Roman merchant who wanted two hundred and seventy wrote what was there and omitted what was not, with no risk that the omission would alter anything he had written. Its lack of a zero follows from its design, in which a mark's value is fixed by its shape. Only when value depends on position does absence acquire consequences, because in a positional system what is not there decides what everything else is worth, and that is why zero belongs to the history of place-value notation and to no other.

Greek mathematics achieved its geometry, its theory of proportion and its proofs of the infinitude of primes without positional notation and without a zero. Greek numerals were letters of fixed value, so the problem of an empty place never arose in ordinary arithmetic. Hellenistic astronomers met it in another setting, since they had inherited Babylonian sexagesimal fractions for their tables of angles and times. In Ptolemy's *Almagest* of the second century CE and the astronomical papyri around it, a small circle, often written with a bar above it, marks an entry in a table where a measurement has no degrees, or no minutes, though it has other parts.[^s1-ptolemy] The circle works nearer to punctuation than to number: it says that a measure of the specified kind is absent, so that a reader can tell "no minutes" from a damaged or missing entry. Several origins have been proposed for the sign and none is settled. The question whether the Greeks had zero is badly posed, because they had a mark for an absent measure inside a table, which is one of zero's later offices, and no number zero that could enter calculation, which is another. A single verdict on both would erase exactly the difference this history needs.

In India the decisive transformation took place, and it took place in language before it took place in arithmetic. By 499 CE, when Āryabhaṭa composed his *Āryabhaṭīya*, Indian astronomers worked with a decimal place-value system and Āryabhaṭa states its rule in words: "from place to place each is ten times the preceding", in the rendering of his translator Walter Eugene Clark.[^s1-aryabhata] What the notation looked like on the working surface is less clear. Clark, discussing the word *kha* in Āryabhaṭa's alphabetic numerals, takes it to mean "space" or "place", records that *kha* later became one of the commonest words for zero, and adds that it is still disputed whether a written symbol for zero was in use in Āryabhaṭa's time: computation may have run on a board ruled into columns, with a blank column standing for zero. If so, the Indian astronomers of 499 met the Babylonian situation again. There the empty place was kept by the reader's board and the reader's habit, not yet by a sign, and the sign that would replace the blank column was already present in the language, in the words with which verse recorded the numbers.

Indian mathematicians wrote in verse, to be memorised and recited, and to fit numbers into metre they used words for digits: the moon, of which there is one, could stand for 1; the eyes or the hands for 2; and so on through a large conventional vocabulary. For zero the verse-makers chose the words for the sky, for space, for the open and the empty: *kha*, the hollow or aperture; *ākāśa*, the space in which things appear; *ambara*, the sky; and *śūnya*, the empty.[^s1-kha-words] A word for space could name the absence of a quantity because absence was already intelligible as the openness in which something might be placed. Kṛṣṇa, a commentator on Bhāskara's algebra in the early seventeenth century, glossed these terms, and Henry Thomas Colebrooke put the gloss into English in a note: they are "synonyma of vacuum or etherial space: nought or cipher; a blank or the privation of specific quantity."[^s1-colebrooke-kha] Zero is the privation of *specific* quantity, the absence of this or that amount, and a privation of that kind leaves the space in which amounts appear exactly where it was. Space does not grow larger when an object enters it or smaller when the object leaves. A vocabulary that could think emptiness in this way was ready to give a column with nothing in it a name of its own.

*Śūnya* carries the deepest of these word-worlds. As an adjective it means empty or void, and its abstract noun *śūnyatā*, emptiness, had become centuries before Āryabhaṭa the central term of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka philosophy. For Nāgārjuna, writing around the second century CE, emptiness is not nothingness: whatever arises in dependence on conditions is empty of any self-standing nature, and that emptiness is itself a dependent designation, the middle way between asserting things as substances and denying them altogether.[^s1-nagarjuna] To call a thing empty in this sense is to say it is constituted by relations it does not contain, so that emptiness is the condition under which anything can appear and function. This is the lesson §0/1 drew from apoha, where a word's meaning lives by what it excludes, and from the tetralemma, whose fourth corner, NEITHER, refused both verdicts and exposed the act that produced them. In the philosophical literature of the same civilisation the word that became the mathematical zero already named the relational emptiness in which determinations arise. No text tells us that a mathematician chose *śūnya* because of Nāgārjuna, and the argument needs no such text. It needs only the fact that the sign entered calculation carrying a word its culture already used to think the ground of all determination.

In Mesoamerica, with no contact that anyone has shown, Maya scribes invented zero for themselves. They counted in twenties, and Sylvanus Morley, whose 1915 handbook still lays out the system clearly, argued that a sign for zero was indispensable to the count, because any numeral system that rises to a second order of units needs a character saying that no units of a given order are present; signs for zero occur in both the codices and the inscriptions.[^s1-morley-sign] Maya zero took an office the Indian sign did not have at first, because the Maya used it to count elapsed time. Maya scribes wrote the first day of a month as Zero Pop, and Morley gives the reason in the Maya's own terms as he understood them: "The day recorded is yesterday because to-day can not be considered an entity until, like the hour of astronomical time, it completes itself and becomes a unit, that is, a yesterday."[^s1-morley-pop] A day is counted when it is over. Morley read the same sign in dates that close a period as "ending", and gave the conception behind it: "According to the Maya conception of time, when a period had ended or closed it was at zero, or at least no new period had commenced."[^s1-morley-end] On that reading one mark carries both the closing of a cycle and the point from which the next is counted, completion and beginning together, and §0/1 wrote that coincidence as `5→0`, the completion of a sixfold turning into the ground from which the next begins. I take the correspondence as an argued reading of Morley's account, which dates from 1915 and which later epigraphy has refined. Zero was invented independently in India and in Mesoamerica, each time for a different problem, and the Maya invention is already an instrument for counting what has ended.

Dated inscriptions let the Indian history hold some firm points. A stone inscription from Sambor on the Mekong, in present-day Cambodia, dated in the Śaka era to the year 605, which is 683 CE, writes its date with a dot for the zero, and it is the earliest securely dated zero in the Indian family of numerals.[^s1-sambor] At Gwalior, in a small temple inscription of 876 CE, the number 270 is written with a hollow circle in the place that we still use.[^s1-gwalior] The Bakhshali manuscript, a birch-bark mathematical text found near Peshawar in 1881, uses a dot for zero throughout, and the radiocarbon dating announced by the Bodleian Library in 2017 assigned some of its leaves to as early as the third or fourth century. Five specialists in Indian mathematics replied that the date of the earliest leaf cannot date the copying of the manuscript, which the latest leaf bounds, and that the manuscript's zeros already work as numbers in calculation, beyond holding places.[^s1-bakhshali] The earliest date does not decide the argument. By the eighth and ninth centuries a sign for the empty place was in ordinary written use over a wide area, as one digit among ten, and it travelled with the other nine.

Well before those inscriptions the sign had acquired an office the Babylonian separator never held. Brahmagupta, writing in 628, gave rules for how zero behaves when it meets positive and negative quantities in addition, subtraction and multiplication, so that zero became a quantity arithmetic must reckon with and ceased to be only a mark for a vacant place.[^s1-brahmagupta] §1 · #1 follows those rules, and the strange theological image Bhāskara set beside one of them five centuries later. I rest my claim about zero's origin on the order of events. In India the history ran from a philosophical and cosmological vocabulary of emptiness, through a positional notation, into explicit arithmetic rules, and the sign arrived in the count still bearing the name of the openness in which counting happens. Zero came into mathematics from metaphysics in that sense. A philosophical doctrine did not cause the arithmetic; the mathematical sign was born inside a language where the empty was already a positive term, the condition of appearance, and its later history in the West would strip that term away and then, at the limits of formal reason, be forced to recover it.

From India the numerals moved west through the scholars of Baghdad. Around 825 CE Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī wrote a treatise explaining the Indian method of reckoning with nine figures and a zero. Its Arabic original is lost, and the treatise survives in a single Latin version of the twelfth century that opens *Dixit algoritmi*, "Algoritmi says."[^s1-khwarizmi-dixit] Its account of zero is explicit, and it matches the Babylonian scribe's difficulty step for step. When no number stands in a place, the Latin text says, the practitioners put there a little circle in the likeness of the letter *o*, "so that by this they would know that the place of the units was empty and that nothing of number was in it except the little circle which, as we have said, occupies it": *circulum paruulum in similitudine .o. litere, ut per hoc scirent quod differentia unitatum esset uacua*.[^s1-khwarizmi-circle] The circle occupies the empty place so that it can stay empty without being mistaken for a gap, a sign whose whole work is to hold a vacancy open. Copyists turned the author's name into a word for the method itself, and from *algorismus*, the art of reckoning with Indian figures, comes our *algorithm*. Every procedure a computer executes, and the whole vocabulary in which we describe what artificial intelligence does, owes its name to a ninth-century scholar who wrote to teach the use of zero.

Arabic writers called the empty place *ṣifr*, the empty, rendering the meaning of the Sanskrit *śūnya*. In Latin the word became *cifra* and later *zephirum*, and from these two routes English received both *cipher* and *zero*: Italian shortened *zephirum* to *zefiro* and *zero*, while *cipher* went on to mean a numeral in general, a person of no account, and a secret code.[^s1-ciphers] The double life of *cipher* records how the sign looked from two sides. A mark that counts nothing and yet fixes the value of everything around it looked, to those who did not know its rules, like a secret, and to those who did, like the key to every calculation.

Leonardo of Pisa, later called Fibonacci, learned Indian reckoning as a boy at Bugia on the North African coast, where his father served as public scribe in the customs house for the Pisan merchants who gathered there. His father had him brought to the town to study the art of the abacus, and Leonardo found the teaching so much to his taste that in 1202 he composed the *Liber abbaci*, the book that did most to carry the method into Latin Europe.[^s1-leonardo-bugia] Its first chapter sets out the nine figures in descending order, 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1, and then adds the tenth: *cum hoc signo 0, quod arabice zephirum appellatur*, "with this sign 0, which in Arabic is called *zephirum*", any number whatever can be written.[^s1-leonardo-sign] The sentence keeps the nine and the zero apart. Leonardo's nine are *figure*, figures; his zero is a *signum*, a sign, introduced as the thing that lets the nine do their work. Leonardo also tells us that the new method met a rival, and which he judged better, when he says he reckoned "the arcs of Pythagoras", the counting board, "almost an error" beside the method of the Indians.[^s1-leonardo-board] On the counting board an empty place had always been visible as a line with no counters on it, and nobody who used one needed a sign for nothing, since the board kept the places. Writing the calculation out on paper required the sign, because paper keeps no places of its own. Zero was the price of leaving the board, and what the pen-reckoner gained by paying it was a calculation that could be checked afterwards, copied into a ledger and carried away, every step still on the page. Through the counting house, in the service of exchange, the sign entered European life, and the contest between board and pen was settled over the following centuries by commercial practice more than by philosophy.

A familiar story adds that medieval Christians feared zero as a diabolical sign and that authorities banned the new numerals for that reason. Philipp Nothaft, a historian, has examined the story and argues that the evidence for a demonised zero and for general bans is thin, and that the narrative was largely constructed by modern writers.[^s1-nothaft] Particular restrictions on figures in account books, where a 0 could be altered into a 6 or a 9, belong to the history of fraud prevention in particular places and show no culture afraid of nothing. That correction matters to an essay about the diabolical because the myth of the satanic cipher is itself an act of *dia-ballein* in the oldest sense, a throwing of accusation across a distance. A modern account sets itself up as enlightened by charging an earlier culture with superstition, and the charge has travelled more readily than the evidence. A reception story about a sign needs its source as much as the sign does, and here the received story is the less trustworthy of the two.

As the sign reached Europe, painters were learning to construct a picture around the vanishing point, the empty place on the horizon toward which every line of a perspectival scene converges, which organises the count of the whole scene while holding nothing. Brian Rotman, in *Signifying Nothing*, argued that zero, the vanishing point and the imaginary money of early modern finance arrived together as three expressions of one semiotic change: each is a sign about other signs, a meta-sign that organises a system of signs from a position the system cannot occupy, and each brought with it a new kind of subject, the one who can stand outside the system and see it whole.[^s1-rotman] §0/1 reached the same point from the side of painting, where the eye at the apex of Alberti's pyramid is the knower made into a place. Rotman's meta-subject and our Subject nonetheless differ. For Rotman the subject who reads zero is produced by a signifying practice, a position the system of signs creates for its user. For our argument the Subject is the condition under which any such position can appear at all, and no account of how a position is produced exhausts the knowing in which that account is understood. Both accounts meet at one sign and part over what it implies, and our notation, which writes the Subject as `0` and its produced positions as determinations in the `1`, can hold Rotman's insight without taking on his conclusion.

Migration bought the sign extraordinary freedom. A Muslim astronomer in Baghdad, a Christian merchant in Pisa and a Hindu temple official in Gwalior did not share a metaphysics, and they did not need one to agree that 270 plus 30 is 300. A calculation could pass between people who disagreed about God, about the nature of space and about the meaning of emptiness, and they could check one another's work by following the steps. That independence of the sign from any compulsory metaphysics is one of the greatest achievements in the history of human reason, and it is the condition of everything modern science and computation have done with number. It also cost something. *Śūnya*, which in its home language named the emptiness in which all things arise, travelled as *ṣifr* and *cifra* and *zero*, words that meant only the empty place; its philosophical life did not accompany the figure into the counting houses of Pisa, because the figure did not need it to work. I call that loss the first advent's sacrifice: mathematical universality was bought by leaving behind the symbolic world in which the sign had been meaningful. That sacrifice was necessary and it was real, and neither half of that sentence can be dropped. A history that only mourned would miss what exactness made possible, and one that only celebrated could not explain why the exact sign returns, many centuries later, at the limits of formal reason, asking to be read again.

Our own time is watching the sign migrate once more. A large language model does not receive numbers as a child does, digit by digit in their places; it receives text cut into tokens, fragments chosen because they recur often in the data the model was trained on, and the tokeniser decides how a number is divided. GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, which Aaditya Singh and DJ Strouse studied, carry separate tokens for numbers of one, two and three digits and cut a long number into such pieces from the left, so that 1005 arrives as 100 and 5, the child's error made by the machine's own division of its input.[^s1-tokens] Place value, the thing the child at the kitchen table had to learn, is therefore absent from what the model is given and must be rebuilt from patterns in its training text. Singh and Strouse found that the same models calculate far better when the digits are grouped from the right, in threes as a comma would group them, because the groups then fall on the places. The child wrote what she heard, and the model wrote what its tokens let it see. Each new receiving practice, the Babylonian tablet, the Greek table, the Sanskrit verse, the Latin manual and the tokeniser, grants the sign some offices and withholds others, and to learn what a mark can do we have to ask each time what its receiver permits.

Seen whole, the migration gives §0/1's question a historical body. We asked there how thought can mark the condition it cannot count as one more object, and history shows that human beings learned, slowly and in several places, to put absence in a position: first as a gap the reader filled from context or a column the board kept empty, then as a separator, a circle in a table, a word for the sky, a dot in a row of digits, a little *o* that occupies a vacant place, and a figure in a merchant's manual. At each stage the sign took over work a practitioner had done in silence, and what had been carried tacitly in the reader's situation became a mark that could travel without the reader. Zero is therefore a sign whose history is the writing-down of context, the gradual conversion of what a calculation presupposes into something the calculation can contain. The conversion cannot be completed, because the act that writes the context down stands in a further context of its own, as division by zero will show. Nobody who wrote a dot for zero in a Sanskrit manuscript was anticipating `0/1`, and none of them needed to be for the sign they handed on to carry the relation we are recovering.

A place held open is still only a place. What gives zero its force is that arithmetic can pass through it: a calculation can add zero, multiply by zero and, finally, try to divide by zero, and someone has to say what happens then. Brahmagupta and Bhāskara said it in verse and with full seriousness, and §1 · #1 follows what they said.

### #1 · Śūnya Becomes Operational

In 628, at Bhillamāla in western India, the astronomer Brahmagupta finished a long treatise in Sanskrit verse, the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, the "correctly established doctrine of Brahmā". Its business was the motions of the planets and the computation of eclipses, and its eighteenth chapter, on algebra, holds a short run of verses that changed what a number could be.[^s1-brahmagupta-date] His pupils did not so much read those verses as learn them line by line, until a rule could be recited in the middle of a calculation the way a craftsman reaches for a tool without looking. They lay down the behaviour of three kinds of quantity. Brahmagupta called positive quantities *dhana*, fortunes or assets, and negative quantities *ṛṇa*, debts, and set beside them a third, *śūnya*, the empty, and then stated what happens when any of the three meets another.[^s1-dhana-rna] Fortune and debt are household and market words, the vocabulary of what one has and what one owes, and the rules read in part like a careful merchant's bookkeeping: a debt taken from nothing becomes a fortune, a fortune taken from nothing becomes a debt, and a fortune added to an equal debt leaves nothing at all.

Henry Thomas Colebrooke's English of 1817 renders the rules for addition and multiplication in this way: "The sum of cipher and negative is negative; of affirmative and nought is positive; of two ciphers is cipher. [...] The product of cipher and negative, or of cipher and affirmative, is nought; of two ciphers, is cipher."[^s1-brahmagupta-rules] His "cipher" and "nought" both translate *śūnya*, and his "affirmative" and "negative" stand for fortune and debt. With numbers on a page the verses can be followed. Add nothing to seven and seven remains; add nothing to a debt of seven and the debt remains; multiply seven by nothing, or a debt of seven by nothing, and nothing is left. With these two rules zero stopped being the mark of a vacant position and became something arithmetic must pass through, a quantity with definite effects on whatever it meets. That is what it means to say that *śūnya* became operational: in Brahmagupta's verses the sign received the permissions of a number.

Already the two rules show zero acting in two different ways. In addition zero is an identity, leaving the other quantity exactly as it was, so that seven plus nothing is still recognisably seven. In multiplication it absorbs: it sends every quantity to the same result, so that six times nothing and seventeen times nothing are both nothing,

$$
6\cdot0=0=17\cdot0,
$$

and the result keeps no trace of which number went in. Six and seventeen remain different numbers, and the product has stopped recording the difference. §0/1 · #1 met a small version of this with Kripke's dice. An account that kept only the total of a throw lost the difference between die A showing one with die B showing two and the reverse, though the two throws were different states of the pair, and the account had changed what it retains: the total answers one question exactly and leaves another unanswered. Multiplication by zero is the extreme case. It retains nothing of its input, and a reader who sees only the product cannot recover what was multiplied. Kaplan draws the consequence, which is that anyone who tried to cancel the common zero in $6\cdot0=17\cdot0$, as one cancels a common factor of three, would conclude that six equals seventeen.[^s1-kaplan-wall] The refusal to cancel a zero is the first wall the arithmetic of zero runs into, and §1 · #4 finds the same wall standing in every number system that has tried to remove it.

Fortune and debt also carry the two logics in the verses. A positive and a negative quantity added together, the first verse says in Colebrooke's English, give "their difference; or, if they be equal, nought."[^s1-brahmagupta-sub] A fortune added to an equal debt leaves nothing, and that is the first collapsed operation of dia-ballein, written as a rule of arithmetic in the seventh century:

$$
(-1)+(+1)=0.
$$

Debt and fortune cancel, and the zero they produce is the zero of a settled account, where nothing is owing on either side and nothing of what passed between the parties survives in the balance. Subtraction gives the second collapsed operation, appropriation, in both its chiralities. A debt taken from a fortune yields their combined magnitude as a fortune, and a fortune taken from a debt yields it as a debt:

$$
(+1)-(-1)=+2,\qquad(-1)-(+1)=-2.
$$

The whole span between the two signs is counted to one side, and the side that receives it is fixed by the pole from which the reckoning starts. In a single transaction the creditor who counts the debtor's whole debt as his gain holds a $+2$ that is the debtor's $-2$, one span read from its two ends.

Commentators show the healthy form of the same relation more clearly than the verses do. Kṛṣṇa, glossing Bhāskara's algebra, explains that the sign of a quantity is a relation to a reference point: "here negation is of three sorts, according to place, time, and things. It is, in short, contrariety."[^s1-krsna-contrariety] What a man owns is positive to him and negative to the one whose property it is. For place, Kṛṣṇa takes Benares as the reference. Patna lies fifteen *yojanas* east of it and Prayāga, at the meeting of the Ganges and the Yamunā, eight *yojanas* to the west, and the distance between the two cities, twenty-three *yojanas*, is "not obtained but by addition of the numbers". Benares is the zero of this little polarity. Patna and Prayāga are contraries because each is defined by its direction from the same place, and the interval across the zero that relates them is found by adding their magnitudes. That is `(−1)/(+1)` as a case of travel: zero kept as the axis that gives each pole its direction, and the span between the poles measured through it. It is dia-ballein doing the work for which the essay holds it indispensable, because a fortune is a fortune only relative to the zero from which a debt runs the other way. Brahmagupta's verses therefore hold side by side, at the moment zero entered calculation, the polarity through zero and its two collapses, cancellation and the appropriated span. Whether a culture reads the zero of its accounts as the axis that relates its terms or as the nothing to which their collision reduces them is a question every later account of zero has to answer. Brahmagupta gave correct rules for signed quantity and they remain correct; reading them through the logics is the essay's own step.

Ledgers travelled with the numerals. In 1494 the Franciscan mathematician Luca Pacioli printed in his *Summa de arithmetica* the first published account of double-entry bookkeeping, the Venetian method in which every transaction is entered twice, once as a debit and once as a credit, so that the books balance when the two columns come to the same total.[^s1-pacioli] The balance of a double-entry ledger is Brahmagupta's rule at the scale of an enterprise: each fortune is matched by a debt, and when the books close the difference between them is nought. A balanced account answers how much remains owing and says nothing of what passed between the parties, which is what §0/1 found a cancelled account to conceal. At the foot of a balanced ledger stands the zero of cancellation, and the modern economy rests on its reliability. That is a real achievement, and it is the reading of zero this history has to complicate, since the same sign, followed to its edge, turns out to hold something no ledger can balance.

Division is where the verses run into trouble, and Brahmagupta met the trouble head on. Division asks for a return: twelve divided by three is four because four multiplied by three gives back twelve, so a quotient answers to the multiplication that would undo it. Replace the three by zero and the question becomes what can be multiplied by zero to give back twelve, and multiplication by zero has already made that unanswerable, since every candidate multiplied by zero gives zero. Brahmagupta stated two rules. "Cipher, divided by cipher, is nought," runs Colebrooke's translation, and "positive, or negative, divided by cipher, is a fraction with that for denominator", a form the commentators called *tac-cheda*, "having that for denominator".[^s1-brahmagupta-div] Later mathematics rejected the first, because every number multiplied by zero gives zero and no single number answers the question it poses, and declined to evaluate the second. Brahmagupta refused neither question. He gave the empty divisor a rule, and in doing so committed arithmetic to saying something about the one operation that zero makes impossible to reverse.

Five centuries later Bhāskara II, the most celebrated of the medieval Indian mathematicians, took up the problem in two books. His *Līlāvatī*, a book of arithmetic in verse that addresses its reader throughout with questions, gives the rules for zero in two couplets, and one of them answers the difficulty about multiplication. The product of zero is nought, Bhāskara says in Colebrooke's English, "but it must be retained as a multiple of cipher, if any further operation impend. Cipher having become a multiplier, should nought afterwards become a divisor, the definite quantity must be understood to be unchanged."[^s1-lilavati-retained] Multiplication by zero destroys the record of what was multiplied, so Bhāskara keeps the record. A number multiplied by zero is written as that number with its zero beside it and held in suspense, and if a later step divides by zero the two cancel and the number returns. Bhāskara's example then tests the reader. What number, multiplied by zero, added to half itself, multiplied by three and divided by zero, makes sixty-three? Fourteen, since fourteen and its half make twenty-one and three times twenty-one is sixty-three, once the zero that multiplied and the zero that divided have been allowed to cancel; and Colebrooke records that the method was in frequent use in astronomical calculation.[^s1-lilavati-example] Ordinary arithmetic cannot do this, because it computes the product at once and then has nothing left to divide. Bhāskara postpones the computation and keeps zero as a marked operation, not a finished value, so that a quantity can pass through zero and come back. It is a small and practical instance of what this essay calls retention: the relation is kept through the moment at which an ordinary account would have closed it.

His *Bījagaṇita*, the "seed-computation" or algebra, which the Sanskrit tradition dates to 1150, returns to the zero denominator itself.[^s1-bijaganita-date] The rule keeps the expression and declines to evaluate it. In Colebrooke's English, "a quantity, divided by cipher, becomes a fraction the denominator of which is cipher", and Bhāskara names such a fraction *khahara*, the quantity whose divisor is *kha*, the empty space.[^s1-khahara] Then he says something no modern textbook says. Of this quantity with zero for its divisor, he writes, "there is no alteration, though many be inserted or extracted; as no change takes place in the infinite and immutable God, at the period of the destruction or creation of worlds, though numerous orders of beings are absorbed or put forth."[^s1-khahara-god]

The image sets the reader a definite act of thought. Adding a quantity to another ordinarily makes the second larger, and taking one away makes it smaller; that is what it means for both to be quantities on one scale. Bhāskara's expression with zero in its denominator does not change when finite quantities are added to it or taken from it, and he finds the model for that invariance in God, who is neither enlarged by the creation of worlds nor diminished by their destruction, though whole orders of beings come forth and return. In modern terms the *khahara* behaves like an infinite quantity, which a finite addition cannot alter. In Bhāskara's terms it behaves like the divine, of which creation is no part added to a whole. Both readings are in the passage, and each is needed for the other. As mathematics the rule asserts how an expression behaves under addition and subtraction, and as theology the comparison tells the reader how to conceive that behaviour, by setting the changing many against a unity they cannot measure. Arithmetic does not demonstrate a doctrine of God, and an image does not prove arithmetic. What the passage records is a mathematician who, at the moment the mathematics of zero reached its edge, reached for the language of the source from which worlds arise and into which they are reabsorbed.

His commentators saw what kind of quantity this was, and Colebrooke's translation of their notes shows them thinking toward the infinite with care. Gaṇeśa calls the *khahara* indefinite, unlimited or infinite, "since it cannot be determined how great it is". Ranganātha argues that the smaller the divisor, the greater the quotient, so that zero, "being in the utmost degree small, gives a quotient infinitely great".[^s1-commentators] Kṛṣṇa makes the decisive step. "If it can be specified, that the amount of the quotient is so much, it has not been raised to the utmost: for a quantity greater than that can be assigned. The quotient therefore is indefinitely great, and is rightly termed infinite."[^s1-krsna-infinite] That is an argument that the infinite cannot be any specified quantity, since for every specified quantity a greater can be named. It reasons from a shrinking divisor to a quotient that grows without bound, the reasoning European calculus would later make rigorous as a limit, and it concludes that the endless quantity lies beyond every determination that could be made of it.

Kṛṣṇa gives the case a physical form, and it is a good one. The numerator of an infinite fraction can be varied by adding or subtracting a finite quantity, he says, "but whether the finite numerator of a fraction, whose denominator is cipher, be more or less, the quotient of its division by cipher is alike infinite." His illustration is the shadow of a gnomon, which at sunrise and sunset is infinite, and "is equally so, whatever height be given to the gnomon".[^s1-gnomon] A taller pole casts the same endless shadow when the sun stands on the horizon. Height is the finite numerator and the horizon is the zero, and whatever we do to the numerator the quotient stays beyond measure; the rule that nothing finite alters the *khahara* is a statement about shadows as well as about God. Kṛṣṇa's infinite stands to every specified quotient as the Subject stood in §0/1 · #1 to every description: whatever is determined falls short of it, and falls short precisely because it is determined.

Indian rules for expressions with a zero denominator form a regime of their own. Amartya Kumar Dutta has reconstructed it, and on his reconstruction such expressions are retained and carried through further operations, while the familiar moves of cancellation and cross-multiplication are not permitted for them and their evaluation waits until the computation has removed the zero from the denominator.[^s1-dutta] Bhāskara's suspended zero-multiple, cancelled by a later division, is a case of that postponement. Kaplan's cancellation argument therefore decides the question inside the laws of an ordinary field, where a zero denominator cannot be treated as a quotient without collapsing all numbers into one; Dutta shows what a regime with restricted operations can keep, a written object under special rules, without that collapse. Together the two findings supply the demand §1 · #4 presses: judge an operation under the laws it claims, and say where those laws differ from the ones doing the judging.

Kaplan's page holds a sentence that I took, the first time I read it, as more than a warning. If one really could divide by zero, it says in effect, all numbers would be the same. §1 · #4 derives the mathematics. The sentence also states, without intending to, the wager of this essay in negative form. A register in which all numbers were the same would be one in which multiplicity had collapsed into oneness. Arithmetic forbids division by zero to prevent that collapse, and so arithmetic is not the register in which the many can be seen as one without ceasing to be many. Bhāskara's image points to a register in which they can: the divine whole, unchanged as worlds come forth and return, holds the many as its own manifestation without being reduced to their sum. Number alone could not hold both, and the history of zero is in part the history of mathematics finding, at its own edge, that it needs a further symbolic register to say what its prohibitions protect. That register is what `0/1` will write.

§0/1 arrived at the same relation from the other side. Whitehead's formula for the creative advance, §0/1 · #3 said, is that the many become one and are increased by one, and what grows is the many: each new occasion is one more, while the One that `0` writes is neither added to nor diminished, the uncarved block staying one while every carving joins the many. Bhāskara's God, unaltered through the putting forth and absorbing of worlds, is the same relation in the language of twelfth-century Indian cosmology, and his *khahara* is the place where that relation touched arithmetic. Older Indian scriptures had stated it in an arithmetic of their own. A verse of the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*, still recited as an invocation, runs: *pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idam pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate / pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate*, "That is full; this is full; from the full the full comes forth; when the full is taken from the full, the full alone remains."[^s1-upanisad] The verse is no theorem about infinite quantities, and Bhāskara does not cite it. It does show that the culture in which the zero denominator received its theological image had long spoken of a whole from which wholes come forth without its diminution, a whole whose arithmetic is not the arithmetic of parts. In Bhāskara the language of that whole and the language of a calculation met in one rule.

Chronology fixes the meeting more exactly. The *Bījagaṇita* dates to 1150. Abhinavagupta, who gave Kashmir Śaivism its fullest systematic form, is conventionally placed between about 975 and 1025, so that roughly a century and a quarter separates his death from Bhāskara's algebra.[^s1-abhinavagupta-date] His tradition produced the most developed account in Indian philosophy of a consciousness that is the source of all manifestation and is not changed by manifesting. In it, as §0/1 set out through Dyczkowski, *prakāśa* is the light of consciousness, the plain fact of appearing, and *vimarśa* its reflexive awareness of itself, so that whatever appears and the power through which it appears belong to one self-articulating whole. Śiva and Śakti, the Subject in its speakable aspect and its power to differentiate and take on identities, stand in §0/1's terms as Mono and Poly, one source and its many. §0 followed that tradition's anatomy of how a single awareness contracts into the finite knower and its world. Bhāskara, an astronomer working in another discipline and another region, is not shown to have known Abhinavagupta's works, and the argument does not depend on his having done so; the year 1150 is not the date of zero's invention either, since Brahmagupta's rules are five centuries older.

What the dates give is a civilisational juxtaposition. Within one intellectual world, over little more than a century, a philosophical school gave systematic form to the relation between an unchanging source and its many manifestations, and a mathematical tradition, meeting the zero denominator, described its behaviour in the image of that relation. Those two practices had different objects and different methods, the Śaiva work metaphysical, phenomenological and soteriological, Bhāskara's computational and astronomical, and each made the relation between finite determination and an exceeding source thinkable in a different way. Their co-presence in one civilisation is the historical fact on which our return of zero rests. Jung named conjunctions of this kind, meaningful in their correspondence and joined by no cause that can be traced, synchronicity.[^s1-synchronicity] The word applies here in its strict sense: the Śaiva doctrine did not cause the algebra and the algebra did not cause the doctrine, and their meeting in one century and one culture is meaningful because each discloses, in its own register, the relation the other states. Gebser's co-presence of structures gives the fact its methodological form, since different ways of knowing can remain operative together without one becoming the hidden explanation of the other, and their difference is what lets them illuminate each other.

The history of zero moved from metaphysics into number and, later, from number back into ontology. In the Indian setting the sign was born inside a language where the empty was the condition of appearing, and it acquired its first arithmetic rules in a culture that could describe its strangest behaviour in the image of the unchanging source. When the sign travelled west it carried the rules and left the image. The rules made it universal, and the image stayed home. Our return of zero brings back what stayed home, now with everything the rules have since made rigorous, and it can do so honestly because the rules and the image were both present in the history, separately and together.

Brahmagupta's pupils learned the verses before they could have proved them, and used them to compute eclipses whose timing could then be checked against the sky. They received the rules on the authority of a teacher and a text and relied on them in practice, and only as the calculations kept succeeding did reliance become knowledge of why the rules worked. §0/1 · #2 called such prior reliance *fides*, the trust already in force before any arbitration. Nobody who first used the rule that a fortune plus an equal debt is nothing had derived it from axioms; the axioms came more than a thousand years later and were chosen so that the rules would follow. The trust came first and the warrant was built to fit it, and the warrant, once built, also revealed where the trust had gone wrong, in the rule that zero divided by zero is zero. Examination changed what the tradition was prepared to entrust, without making the original reliance a mistake.

A rule stated precisely enough can be followed by someone who does not understand what the quantities mean, and the following will still be correct. A pupil who has memorised that the product of cipher and affirmative is nought can multiply by zero correctly with no view about the nature of emptiness, and in that independence of correct procedure from understanding lies the condition of all mechanical computation. Algorithms, which carry al-Khwārizmī's name, are rules of this kind, and the arithmetic a language model performs for every word it produces runs to billions of operations that follow them, delivering correct results to an executor who need not know what the results are about. Brahmagupta's verses are among the first written rules that let zero be handled this way, and they show its cost. Followed as faithfully as the others, the rule for zero divided by zero was wrong, and nothing in the procedure could detect that. The error had to be found by someone asking what the rule meant.

By the twelfth century zero had been placed, named, given arithmetic rules and pressed to the edge where division fails, and at that edge an Indian mathematician had set it beside the image of the unchanging source of worlds. Its next step is formal. In Europe, at the turn of the twentieth century, logicians asked whether number itself could be built from nothing and found that it could, provided one counted the nothing.

### #2 · The Empty Set Generates One

A folder on the desktop reports that it contains one item. Opened, it holds a single other folder, and that folder is empty. Yet the outer folder is not empty, though there is nothing in it but an emptiness. Delete the inner folder and the outer one becomes empty as well, and the count it reports drops from one to none. Nobody tidying a hard drive thinks this is philosophy, yet the little dialogue box that says "1 item" has done something exact: it has counted an empty container as one thing, and made a one out of nothing but the act of putting nothing inside something.

Mathematicians write that act with two signs. An empty set, a collection with no members, is written $\varnothing$. A set whose only member is the empty set is written $\{\varnothing\}$, and it is not empty, since it has exactly one member:

$$
\varnothing\quad\longrightarrow\quad\{\varnothing\}.
$$

Nothing has been added to the empty set. A new relation has been formed around it, and the braces show where the one appears. At the end of the nineteenth century and the beginning of the twentieth, logicians took a step of this kind as the beginning of the whole sequence of numbers. If one can be built from nothing by counting the nothing, two can be built from nothing and one, and so on without end, and the natural numbers need no material of their own beyond the empty set and the operation that gathers it.

Gottlob Frege was among the first to see this clearly. §0/1 met him twice, once explaining why "the morning star is the evening star" tells us something that "a = a" does not, and once receiving Russell's letter about the catalogue of all catalogues that do not list themselves. In *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* of 1884, his attempt to say what numbers are without appeal to intuition or psychology, he defined a number as something that belongs to a concept: the number of Jupiter's moons belongs to the concept "moon of Jupiter", and two concepts have the same number when the things falling under them can be paired off one to one.[^s1-frege-number] The definition needed a first number, and Frege found it in a concept under which nothing can fall. "Since nothing falls under the concept 'unequal to itself'," he wrote in §74, "I explain: 0 is the number which belongs to the concept 'unequal to itself'."[^s1-frege-zero] He anticipated the objection that such a concept contains a contradiction and answered with some humour, recalling wooden iron and the square circle: they do no harm, he said, so long as one does not suppose that anything falls under them, and all logic asks of a concept is that it be sharply bounded, so that for every object it is settled whether the object falls under it. He also said why he had chosen this concept. Any concept under which nothing falls would have served, but he wanted one whose emptiness logic alone could prove, and self-inequality offered itself most conveniently, since on Leibniz's definition of identity it is purely logical that every object is equal to itself.[^s1-frege-empty] Having zero, he defined one in §77 as the number belonging to the concept "equal to 0", under which exactly one object falls, namely zero itself, and each further number followed by counting the numbers already obtained.[^s1-frege-one]

The choice leads back to the copula. §0/1 · #1 wrote the copula's double work with the simplest marks there are and found that the sign for non-equality, `≠`, is the sign for equality with the cut drawn through it. Frege's zero is the count of that cut turned on a thing and itself. It is the number of whatever is not equal to itself, and since nothing is, the count is zero, so the first number of his arithmetic counts a self-relation that fails everywhere. His one then counts whatever is equal to that zero, and the only thing that is, is zero. In the order of Frege's construction the first thing ever counted is zero itself: one is reached by counting the nothing that failed self-difference produced. I take this to be more than an elegant trick of definition, and Frege's reason for the choice shows why. He did not need self-inequality in particular; he needed an emptiness that logic could certify without looking at the world, and the one concept whose emptiness follows from logic alone is a thing's failure to be identical with itself. Every other empty concept, the golden mountain or the present king of France, is empty because of how the world happens to be, whereas self-inequality is empty because of what identity is. Looking for the logical ground of number, Frege found that the first number must be produced from the impossibility of a thing differing from itself, and that the first unit counts the result of that impossibility. §0/1 traced through the copula's double work the identity every object has with itself, and it is the ground from which his whole arithmetic was drawn: zero counts its failure, and one counts zero. That reading is mine and not Frege's, and his own reason for the choice supports it.

Frege's further ambition, to derive these concepts and their numbers from purely logical laws, ran into the paradox §0/1 · #3 followed. His principle for turning concepts into collections allowed Russell's self-defeating collection to form, and the second volume of the *Basic Laws* went to press with an appendix conceding that a foundation had given way. Number built from nothing by counting survived the paradox in the set theory that grew up to repair it. In 1923 John von Neumann, not yet twenty, gave it the form mathematics still uses: each number is the set of all the numbers before it.[^s1-neumann] Zero is the set of all numbers before zero, of which there are none, so zero is the empty set. One is the set whose only member is zero. Two is the set of zero and one, three the set of zero, one and two:

$$
0=\varnothing,\qquad 1=\{0\}=\{\varnothing\},\qquad 2=\{0,1\}=\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\},\qquad 3=\{0,1,2\}.
$$

Each number is generated from the one before it by keeping all its members and adding the number itself as one further member. Written compactly, the successor of a number $n$ is

$$
S(n)=n\cup\{n\}.
$$

Zero has no members to keep, so its successor is $\{0\}$, which is one. For one, the member kept is zero and the member added is one, which gives $\{0,1\}$, which is two. Every number holds all its predecessors, so zero is a member of every number after it and the sequence carries its beginning into every later stage.

Von Neumann's was not the only way. Ernst Zermelo had earlier taken the successor of a set to be simply the set containing it, so that his numbers ran $\varnothing$, $\{\varnothing\}$, $\{\{\varnothing\}\}$, each holding only the one before, and both constructions yield a sequence that behaves exactly as the natural numbers do.[^s1-zermelo] That tells us what kind of result we have. An empty set and the successor operation give a representation of the numbers, a way of building objects that do everything the numbers must do, and mathematics chooses among representations by what they make easy. Von Neumann's became standard because each of his numbers is literally the collection of the numbers it counts past, so that three is a set with three members and the order of the numbers is the order of membership. Nor does the construction claim that numbers are sets in some deeper sense. It shows that the natural numbers can be generated from an empty beginning and one operation of gathering, and that is what this section needs.

Von Neumann's two invites comparison with a count §0/1 made, and the two counts must not be run together. §0/1 took the two single terms $0$ and $1$, related the binary to itself to obtain the four ordered pairings $00$, $01$, $10$ and $11$, and kept both levels together to reach $2+2^{2}=4+2$: six, the shape of a distinction that includes its own self-relation. Von Neumann's two is a different object, the set $\{0,1\}$ whose two members are the two numbers before it, and it counts succession, so that two is what comes after one and holds everything that came before. §0/1's six counts self-application and the ordinal sequence counts succession, and neither derivation can stand in for the other. Both begin from the same pair, zero and one, and both find that the pair generates more than itself once it is held together and no longer treated as two separate items.

Read with a reverence it never asked for, the ordinal two is zero and one held together, $2=\{0,1\}$, and the ordinal one is the void gathered into a unit, $1=\{\varnothing\}$, a set whose entire content is the empty set it holds. The one is no second thing standing beside the zero. It is the zero bound into a collection, the act of holding that makes the empty countable as one, and the first one in von Neumann's sequence is in that sense the mark of a relation to zero before it is anything else. Our core theorems name it the **symbolic linker**, the `1` that links an articulated field back to the ground it cannot contain.[^s1-linker] §0/1 gave the two marks two senses of *one*: zero bears the singular One, self-identity, and one bears the manifest All, self-difference. Here the second is made from the first, since the one that counts is zero held, and in the set-theoretic register the link is literal: one contains zero as its member and is distinct from it, and every later number carries zero inside it as the first of the members it has gathered.

Three relations that ordinary speech runs together under the word "contains" must be kept apart, and the construction works only because they are. Zero is a member of one, since one is the set whose member is zero. Zero is also a subset of one, since every member of zero is a member of one, there being no members of zero to check, and the empty set is a subset of every set. And zero is not identical to one, since one has a member and zero has none. Membership, inclusion and identity do different work. Membership carries each number into the next, inclusion keeps the earlier numbers inside the later, and the difference between a set and its members stops the whole sequence from collapsing back into its beginning. Finite repetition of the successor reaches every finite number but does not by itself produce the collection of all of them; set theory needs a separate axiom, the axiom of infinity, to assert that such a collection exists. That beginning is generous without being unlimited, and the point at which the finite sequence asks for an infinite completion is one to which §1 · #4 returns, where infinity enters arithmetic as the price of dividing by zero.

The empty set is an object of mathematics, defined within a set theory and as exact as any object there is. §0/1's Subject is not another empty object, and our `0` does not denote the empty set. When we write the ordinal construction and follow it, the set, the braces, the rule and the act of following the rule are all available for examination, and each examination is a knowing; representing that knowing adds a further object to what is examined and does not make the knowing a member of any set. Set and Subject belong to different levels, and running them together would destroy the distinction the construction helps us see. What the construction gives us is a formal neighbour to the relation the essay has derived: a case in which a beginning that is nothing remains present in everything generated from it, and in which the first determinate unit is the act of holding that beginning. Mathematics supplies the case under its own laws, Number itself, built with the greatest care modern logic could bring to it, begins with an empty set and the one that holds it, and `0/1` writes that relation.

Kaplan's history sets beside the ordinals, in the same chapter, two other beginnings from nothing, each under a different law.[^s1-kaplan-empty] An empty sum, a sum of no terms at all, is defined to be zero, because adding zero leaves any sum as it was: if one adds a list of numbers and then appends no further numbers, the total must not change. An empty product, a product of no factors, is defined to be one, for the same reason, since appending no further factors must leave a product unchanged and the number that leaves products unchanged is one. Multiplying by zero gives zero because a zero factor absorbs everything, while multiplying by no factor gives one because an absent factor changes nothing, and ordinary speech calls both "multiplying by nothing" where mathematics asks what was actually done. In these constructions absence has no value of its own. It takes its value from the operation that holds it, and the value it takes is that operation's identity, the element that leaves every other element identical with itself.

Mathematics calls zero the additive identity, and the name carries the connection. An operation's identity element is the one element that leaves every other element as it is, the operation's own preservation of sameness, the place where acting changes nothing. In addition that place belongs to zero. §0/1 wrote `0` for the Subject because the Subject is self-identity, the One that remains itself through every determination without becoming one of them, and the arithmetic word for zero names the same office in its own register: zero is what lets a thing, added to anything, remain itself. *Identity* names the logical relation of a thing to itself, the arithmetic element that preserves every sum, and the office our notation gives the uncounted pole, and in each case identity is what is present in every operation without altering what the operation acts on. That arithmetic also assigns the identity of multiplication to one, and makes the empty product one, shows the relation from the other side. Where the operation gathers many factors into a product, its identity is the unit, the one every product already includes.

Kaplan's third scene changes the material again, from number to logic.[^s1-kaplan-nor] In the logic of true and false, take a single operation, "neither … nor", and write it as an arrow pointing down, so that $p\downarrow q$ is true exactly when both $p$ and $q$ are false:

$$
p\downarrow q=\neg(p\lor q).
$$

Apply it to the same proposition twice, "neither $p$ nor $p$", and the result is true exactly when $p$ is false, so the operation has produced negation. Deny the denial and disjunction appears; deny the two separate denials and conjunction appears:

$$
\neg p=p\downarrow p,\qquad p\lor q=(p\downarrow q)\downarrow(p\downarrow q),\qquad p\land q=(p\downarrow p)\downarrow(q\downarrow q).
$$

With negation, conjunction and disjunction in hand, every truth function of any finite number of propositions can be built, by writing for each combination of inputs on which the function should be true a conjunction that selects just that combination, and joining the selected combinations with "or". Repeated "neither … nor" therefore generates the whole of propositional logic. Charles Sanders Peirce saw this around 1880, in a manuscript left unpublished long after his death; Henry Sheffer proved in 1913 that a single connective, written with a stroke, could serve as the one primitive of Boolean algebra; and Wittgenstein made joint denial the operation from which, in the *Tractatus*, every proposition is generated from the elementary propositions.[^s1-sheffer] What had seemed two or three primitive operations of logic needed only one, and that one is a double refusal.

Read against what §0/1 found in exclusion, the generator shows the same work in a new material. Apoha made a word determinate by what it excludes: *cow* is understood as *not non-cow*, and the double negative carries the word's whole work of gathering its instances. The tetralemma's fourth corner, NEITHER, refused both verdicts and exposed the act that produced them. Here a logic is built entirely from refusal, and every assertion that can be compounded from simple propositions can be reached by saying "neither this nor that" often enough and in the right arrangement. Kaplan reads the reduction as the key to Wittgenstein's whole book: if every proposition is built from joint denials, what language states is reached by way of what it rules out, and what it cannot state can only be shown by the direction in which its statements point.[^s1-kaplan-wittgenstein] That reading of the *Tractatus* is Kaplan's, and §0/1 met Wittgenstein's distinction between saying and showing in his own words. Beneath it lies a formal fact that gives the tetralemma's NEITHER and apoha's exclusion a neighbour in the logic of truth values. A whole field of affirmation can be generated from denial, provided the denials are arranged, and the arrangement does the work no single denial can.

Sheffer's result holds for truth functions, the ways the values true and false can be combined, and the slash of `0/1` relates a determination to a condition that is not one more input with a truth value. No Boolean connective, however complete, can be that slash, since every connective takes determinate values and returns a determinate value. Even so, the neighbour teaches. Sheffer's primitive was written as a stroke between two letters, and the stroke between two terms is the oldest notation in this essay's history. "A Hair perhaps divides the False and True," runs a line of Edward FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát*, and Kaplan quotes it at this point in his book.[^s1-hair] The image fits Sheffer's stroke, a hairline mark from which the difference between true and false can be generated in every combination, and our slash is the same thin mark asked to do something further: to divide and join at once, and to hold the relation between the false and true on one side and the condition of their being known on the other.

Every digital machine now built rests on this generator. A single kind of logic gate, a circuit that outputs true only when both its inputs are false, or its twin that outputs false only when both inputs are true, is enough to build any digital circuit, and engineers have built whole computers that way. Designers of the Apollo Guidance Computer that flew to the Moon chose one integrated circuit, carrying three-input NOR gates, as the only logic element of the machine, and its first version used some four thousand of them.[^s1-apollo] The machine that navigated a spacecraft between the Earth and the Moon computed everything it computed by arranging one refusal, "neither this nor that nor the other", thousands of times over. Chips running a language model are not built from one gate type, but everything they compute could be, and every number the model handles, including the numbers that stand for its words, reduces in the end to arrangements of a repeated refusal. §0/1 observed that a civilisation built with zeroes has not thereby learned what its zero excludes, and the machine is that observation in material form. All its affirmation is generated from "neither … nor" applied to determinate values, and the condition under which any of those values means something to anyone is not among the values it combines.

Each of these constructions begins from almost nothing under its own law. The ordinal construction builds the numbers from the empty set by gathering it and keeps every earlier number inside every later one. Empty sum and empty product take their values from the identities of their operations, and show that absence is never simply absent, since the operation that holds it gives it its value. Joint denial builds the whole of logic from refusal, and shows that a field of affirmation can be generated from what it excludes. They are three different results under three laws, and what they share is a pattern §0/1 anticipated: a spare beginning becomes generative through an operation that retains it, so that what is generated carries its beginning inside it. In each the beginning is something mathematics treats as an object, the empty set, the identity element or the false value, and in each the generative power lies in the operation and in what it keeps.

One can now serve as a linker, a determinate unit through which a relation to zero is held and carried into everything that follows, and its power depends on what it links. Trouble comes when the one forgets that it is a holding, when the singleton that holds the void takes itself for the source of its own unity and the mark of a relation passes for the thing related. §1 · #3 follows the sign that marks that forgetting.

### #3 · The Crossed Zero

Someone is kneeling behind the sofa with a torch, reading the network password off the sticker on the back of a router, and a friend is typing it in across the room. Halfway through they stop. Is that a zero or the letter O? The sticker's typeface draws both as the same tall oval, the password will be refused if they guess wrong, and so they try one and then the other, and the second attempt connects. Many of us have been in some version of that room. Writing has had this difficulty as long as it has had the sign. The twelfth-century Latin of al-Khwārizmī's treatise already says that the little circle for an empty place was drawn "in the likeness of the letter *o*",[^s1-khwarizmi-o] and once letters and digits came to share one keyboard, one printer and one line of code, a written nothing could be mistaken for a sound. People who wrote programs by hand on coding sheets for punch-card operators, and the designers of the typefaces on early terminals, often settled the matter by striking the zero through with a diagonal stroke, so that 0 became a crossed figure no one could read as a letter. Shops never agreed on it, and in some the stroke went through the letter and left the zero alone.[^s1-slashed]

A crossed circle turns up in three other places, each with its own job. In Danish and Norwegian, Ø is a letter, a vowel with a sound of its own, written as an O with a stroke through it, and it names islands, families and the word for island itself. In mathematics, ∅ is the sign for the empty set, the collection with no members from which §1 · #2 built the numbers. In engineering drawings, ⌀ marks a diameter, the line through a circle's centre. These four signs look alike, and the international standard for encoding characters gives each its own place, because they are different signs that happen to share a shape.[^s1-unicode] The empty set's sign has a particular history. When the group of French mathematicians writing as Nicolas Bourbaki opened their treatise on set theory in 1939 with a summary of results, they needed a symbol for the empty set, and André Weil, the one member who knew the Norwegian alphabet, proposed its crossed O.[^s1-weil] The group had set itself the task of rewriting the whole of mathematics on a single foundation, and the foundation it chose was the theory of sets, whose first object is the empty set, the set from which the numbers themselves can be built. So the sign at the base of the most ambitious foundational project of twentieth-century mathematics is a borrowed letter, a vowel from a northern alphabet, crossed, chosen because one member of a committee happened to know it.

These histories are small, and each carries the problem of the crossed zero. An engineer's slashed zero is a zero marked so that it will be read as a number, since the stroke is added to nothing to secure its identity as the sign of nothing against its likeness to something else. A Norwegian letter is a sound written with a crossed circle. Weil's empty set turns that vowel into an object as exact as any other set, with a crossed circle borrowed from a language for its sign. In each case a stroke crosses an empty figure, and in each the crossing makes the empty figure determinate, a *this* rather than a *that*. A stroke does not fill the zero. It marks the zero as one kind of thing among others, so that it can be counted, typed, read and told apart.

§0/1 gave our own crossed zero, `Ø`, one definite meaning, and the history shows its structure in a form small enough to hold. In following the archetypal images and complexes through which a self tries to secure itself, §0/1 · #3 wrote the first occlusion as the zero of the Subject crossed by the stroke of its own mediation: the slash through which a world is seen fuses with the one who sees, so that the formed interpretation passes for the one who interprets, the eye mistaking a sky for its gaze. In `0/1` the zero is the unobjectifiable pole of knowing, the one is the determinate world, and the slash is the activity, the means, through which the one appears to the zero. In `Ø` the slash has collapsed into the zero, which is dia-ballein's cancellation of the `0/`: the stroke through which the one appears is summed into the zero it served, and a relation vanishes into a thing. Something is still being seen, recognised, wanted and defended, so the means is still at work, but it has fused with the subject-pole, so that the whole figure appears as one determinate thing, a self, where it should appear as a relation. A stroke drawn through nothing makes nothing readable as a definite sign, and that is a real gain, without which the password could not be typed. A cost arrives when the marked figure is taken for what it marks, when the reader forgets that the stroke was added to a zero and takes the crossed figure for a positive character among the letters. §0/1 · #4 pictured the same crossing in the eye at the apex of Alberti's pyramid. In a perspectival picture the vanishing point on the horizon and the eye at the apex are two zeros, one holding nothing at the far end of every line and the other holding the whole scene at the near end, and the picture works by letting the second be forgotten.

At this first appearance in the history of zero, `Ø` has one job: it shows representation crossing a zero-space it cannot fill. A stroke passes through the open circle, makes it determinate, lets it be counted and distinguished and used, and the circle remains open, because the stroke has put nothing inside it. Occlusion begins when the crossed figure is read as a filled one. What happens when the stroke is recognised as mediation, how the world on its other side comes to be seen as a field, and how both are recognised together before the return to `0/1`, is the sequence §4 · #3 follows, where Lacan's barred subject and the philosophers who each took one side of the crossing give every step its psychic body. Here the crossing happens and is not yet seen.

§1 · #2 left us with the one as linker, the singleton that holds the void, $1=\{\varnothing\}$, the first unit whose entire content is the empty set it gathers. That one has a counterpart in language. Lacan called the signifier that fixes a chain of meaning in place, the word around which a field of other words is arranged, the master signifier, and §0/1 · #3 met its function as the *point de capiton*, the quilting point, the upholsterer's button that holds the stuffing of a cushion where it is.[^s1-lacan] A name of God, a nation, a party, a self-description, "the sensible one" or "a successful person": each can serve as a quilting point that arranges a life's other meanings around it. A field's first signifier cannot draw its authority from another signifier already inside that field, any more than the first one in von Neumann's sequence can draw its content from a number already counted. It institutes the count by cutting into a no-thing it never exhausts. So the set-theoretic one and the master signifier perform one structural function in two registers, each a determinate mark that binds a field to a ground it cannot itself contain.

Each is legitimate as a linker, and each is liable to the failure `Ø` writes. A master signifier fails when it tries to be the ground it gestures at, when "God" or "the nation" or "my identity" is taken to be the source of the meanings it holds together, and every challenge to it is felt as an attack on reality itself. A quilting point that knows itself provisional holds without that claim, a stand-in that knows itself a stand-in and stays oriented toward a ground it does not contain. Our core theorems state the difference in one sentence I keep as it stands: the slide is real because the threader does not slide. Lacan showed that meaning slides beneath the signifier, that no word holds its meaning still, and that the quilting points which hold it are contingent and can always be undone. That sliding is real, and what it does not reach is the one for whom it slides, the threader of the chain, who is never one more link in it. A master signifier that takes itself for the threader appropriates the sense of a whole field for one link, counting the span from its own end, and it will slide like every other link, and a linking one that knows it is a holding, the singleton that knows the void it gathers is not its own content, can hold for as long as it is needed and be released when a better holding is found. That is the whole difference between the crossed zero and the one held in ratio to its zero, between a `1` with its `0/` struck off, asked to serve as its own ground, and a `1` that remains `0/1`, the mark doubly oriented and joined to the ground it carries.

Once the zero is crossed, the gap §0/1 opened with passes easily for a residue. Both the god of the gaps and the humanity of the gaps make one error: each identifies a subject, divine or human, with whatever capacities are not yet explained, and a residue shrinks as explanation grows. Once the slash fuses into the zero, the means of knowing appears as the knower, and every explanation of the means then looks like a step toward explaining the knower. Empirical gaps close, mechanisms become inspectable, and capacities migrate from organisms to machines without touching the distinction between the knowing and what it knows, and what grows clearer as they do is the means, how a determination is produced, inherited, revised and returned. What knows was never the last unexplained function in the inventory; it seemed to be because the zero had been crossed. §0/1 gave the immutable gap its formal shape through Gödel's sentences, and it is the gap between the zero and any stroke that crosses it; it relocates with every enlargement of the stroke because no stroke, however long, fills the circle it crosses.

Now the stroke is a problem in its own right: the means of determination, without which nothing could be counted, read, typed or known, and which tends to present itself as what it serves. Mathematics has a precise form for the problem, and it appears when the one, the determinate unit, tries to return to the zero it crosses by dividing by it.

### #4 · Zero Keeps One Foot Outside Mathematics

Somebody in every mathematics class types one divided by zero into a calculator to see what will happen, and the class leans in. Back comes "Error", or "E", or on a newer machine "Math ERROR", and the teacher explains that you cannot divide by zero. Someone asks why not, and the honest answer takes longer than the lesson has left. The calculator has not failed. It has reported, in the only word its display has for the purpose, that the question it was asked has no answer under the rules it was built to follow, and the class has met, without anyone saying so, the place where a system of calculation has to declare what its rules are.

Take an ordinary field, the kind of number system the class uses every day, in which there are addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by anything other than zero, and in which zero and one are different numbers. Dividing one by a number $b$ means finding the number $q$ with $b\cdot q=1$, so dividing one by zero would mean finding a $q$ with

$$
0\cdot q=1.
$$

The difficulty is not that such a $q$ is hard to find. Whatever $q$ is, the laws of the system rule it out. By the distributive law,

$$
0\cdot q=(0+0)\cdot q=0\cdot q+0\cdot q,
$$

and subtracting $0\cdot q$ from both sides leaves $0\cdot q=0$, for every $q$ in the system. To ask that $0\cdot q$ also equal one is to ask that zero equal one, which the system was built to deny, and adding a new element $q$ while keeping the same laws would not help, because the argument applies to the new element too. Zero divided by zero fails differently. Every $q$ satisfies $0\cdot q=0$, so the question has too many answers and the rule of division cannot pick one. A nonzero number divided by zero has no answer, and zero divided by zero has no single answer, and the word "undefined", which covers both, hides the two different reasons. Brahmagupta's rule that zero divided by zero is zero, met in the verses of 628, picked one answer out of the many the question admits, and later arithmetic declined to make that choice.

§1 · #1 promised a derivation of Kaplan's remark that dividing by zero would make all numbers the same. Suppose we insisted on giving zero an inverse anyway, keeping all the other laws of the system and adding a number $z$ with $0\cdot z=1$. Then for any number $a$ at all,

$$
a=a\cdot1=a\cdot(0\cdot z)=(a\cdot0)\cdot z=0\cdot z=1,
$$

so every number equals one, and since $0$ is a number, $0=1$ as well. Everything in the system collapses into a single element, which is at once zero and one and every other number. Mathematicians know this object as the zero ring, the trivial ring with one element, and it is what the construction of fractions yields when zero is allowed among the denominators.[^s1-zero-ring] Kaplan's remark is therefore correct: dividing by zero without changing any other law identifies everything with everything.

The zero ring is the collapse of the One and the All, which coincide without remainder: there is one element, and it is every number, so that unity and multiplicity have been identified by abolishing the difference between them. That is fusion, the loss of the seam by merger, the cancellation `(−1)+(+1)=0` run through every pair until nothing can be told apart, which the symbolon was made to resist, since a token whose halves had melted into one piece could no longer prove anything by their fit. An ordinary field takes the other course. It refuses division by zero and in refusing keeps every number distinct from every other, at the price that the determinate one can never return to the zero by division, since `1/0` has no value. That refusal is dia-ballein doing its proper work. It cuts, it keeps the difference between zero and one sharp, and it states the cut as a law, so that every calculation proceeds knowing where the edge is. Our notation asks for neither collapse. `0/1 = 1/0`, in the relational register §0/1 established, is no equation between two numbers and assigns `1/0` no value in a field; it says that one relation can be read from either end, with the difference between the defined and the undefined retained as the seam through which the two readings meet. Ordinary arithmetic keeps the seam by refusing to cross it, and the zero ring loses the seam by crossing it without remainder. Syn-ballein, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, keeps the seam and crosses it as a relation, and the question to put to any proposal, mathematical or otherwise, is the one the symbolon puts: where did the seam go? It can go into a refusal that preserves difference by forbidding return, into a merger that permits return by abolishing difference, or into a relation that holds both.

Between those two extremes mathematics has built a series of systems that give division by zero a meaning by changing something else, and each shows the frame becoming visible at zero. The real projective line adds a single point at infinity to the numbers, so that a nonzero number divided by zero can be assigned that point, at the price that the point at infinity cannot itself be added to infinity or multiplied by zero in a way that keeps the familiar laws.[^s1-projective] The complex numbers, completed in the same way, become a sphere, the Riemann sphere, on which the plane wraps around until all its far directions meet at a single point at the top, so that zero and infinity become the two poles of one globe and the map that sends every number to its reciprocal turns the sphere upside down, exchanging zero and infinity, and is defined everywhere.[^s1-riemann] What has become total there is a map of points; division as an operation still fails when both of its parts vanish. Further systems go further. In the algebraic structures called wheels, division by zero is always defined, and the price is that familiar laws such as $x/x=1$ and $0\cdot x=0$ hold only in weakened forms.[^s1-wheels] In the structures called meadows, every number has an inverse and zero is its own inverse, so that $0^{-1}=0$, and the price is that multiplying a number by its inverse gives one only when the number is not zero.[^s1-meadows] Each system keeps some of the old laws and gives up others. None abolishes the difficulty: each relocates it and states where it has gone.

A point at infinity has a history that returns us to the painter's room. Perspective organises a picture around a vanishing point, the empty place on the horizon toward which parallel lines in the scene converge, so that railway tracks that never meet on the ground meet there on the canvas. In 1639 the architect and engineer Girard Desargues took the painters' practice into geometry and proposed that parallel lines should be treated as meeting at a point at infinity, one such point for each direction, so that any two lines in a plane meet somewhere and the theorems of geometry no longer need exceptions for parallels.[^s1-desargues] Projective geometry grew from that proposal, and the projective line on which a nonzero number divided by zero has a value is its descendant. So the painters' vanishing point became the mathematicians' point at infinity, and that point became the place where `1/0` could be put. Rotman set zero beside the vanishing point, and the two meet here in one construction: the empty place on the horizon, which organises a picture while holding nothing, is the place where the quotient by zero lands once the space has been enlarged to receive it. §0/1 · #4 found the knower made into a place at the near end of Alberti's pyramid, and projective geometry found the far end, the vanishing point, and gave it the value of the forbidden quotient.

This is the sense in which zero keeps one foot outside mathematics. Zero is wholly mathematical: it is written, counted, operated on, and indispensable to every positional notation and every formal construction of number. Yet the operation that returns a determinate quantity to zero, division, cannot be completed without choosing or changing the space in which completion is sought, so the attempt exposes the commitments of the whole system. A calculation that meets zero in its denominator forces the system to say what it is: a field, which refuses; a projective line, which adds a point; a wheel or a meadow, which changes its laws. "Outside" here means frame-disclosing. That edge is mathematical through and through, and at it every determinate account of number shows what it had presupposed.

Our own machines have had to make this choice explicitly, because a computer that divides by zero cannot ask a teacher what to do. IEEE 754, the standard for floating-point arithmetic that governs almost every processor now built, gives division by zero a definite law.[^s1-ieee] It provides two zeros, a positive and a negative, which compare as equal but remember the direction from which a calculation arrived at zero: one divided by positive zero is positive infinity, one divided by negative zero is negative infinity, and the processor raises a flag to record that a division by zero occurred. Zero divided by zero, which has no single answer, gives a special value called NaN, "not a number", and NaN has a property no number has: it is not equal to itself, so a program that asks whether NaN equals NaN is told that it does not. William Kahan, the principal architect of the standard, defended the signed zero in a paper whose subtitle is "Much Ado About Nothing's Sign Bit", and the phrase fits, since the whole design turns on letting nothing carry a sign.[^s1-kahan]

The signed zero gives the polar axis a memory at its turning point. Dia-ballein's healthy polarity, `(−1)/(+1)`, holds two signed quantities on one axis through a zero that connects them and gives each its direction. In ordinary arithmetic that zero has no sign of its own, and the axis simply turns there. IEEE 754 writes two zeros, one for each side, and so lets the axis remember, at the point of its turning, from which pole a quantity approached. That memory matters when the quantity tries to return through zero: divide by that zero and the result runs off to the infinity on the side from which the quantity came. So the machine has written the chirality of the approach into nothing itself, so that the return through zero keeps the orientation of the journey, much as a loop on the torus, which §0/1 followed, comes home to its starting point while its winding records which way it went round. NaN gives the other limit a sign. Frege's zero, we saw in §1 · #2, was the number of the concept "not identical with itself", the count of what fails to equal itself, of which there is nothing, and the machine's NaN is the one value that does fail to equal itself, the result of asking zero to divide zero. Frege derived the first number from the impossibility of self-difference, and the engineers marked the empty quotient with the one value that tests unequal to itself, a rule of comparison that echoes Frege's self-inequality at the edge where nothing meets nothing.

Anyone who has trained a neural network knows what happens when that edge is reached by accident. Training measures how wrong a model's predictions are by a number called the loss, plotted as it falls over hours or days; then a single division by a quantity that has shrunk to zero, or a logarithm of zero somewhere in the arithmetic, produces a NaN, and because any calculation involving NaN yields NaN, the corruption spreads through the model's parameters within a few steps and the run is lost. Engineers protect against it by keeping a small number between every denominator and zero. Adam, the optimiser used to train most large models, divides each update by the square root of a running average and adds to that square root a constant written ε, set by default to $10^{-8}$, so that the denominator can approach zero and never reach it, and the normalisation layers inside the models add a similar ε, by default on the order of $10^{-5}$, before taking a square root.[^s1-adam] These constants are design decisions at the edge the calculator displayed as "Error". Each declares a law for what happens near zero, choosing to keep a distance from the zero and not to reach it, and every large model in use today carries such constants in its optimiser and in each normalisation layer. Machines that now reconstruct language from number depend, at countless points, on a deliberate refusal to let a quantity return all the way to nothing.

Calculus met the same edge three centuries earlier, and its history shows the foot outside mathematics being brought in by a change of frame. Newton and Leibniz, the same Leibniz who would read the binary digits as an image of creation, both computed the rate of change of a quantity as the ratio of two increments allowed to shrink until they vanish, so that a derivative looks like a quotient of two quantities that have become zero, nothing divided by nothing. The method worked, and nobody could say what the vanishing increments were. In 1734 the philosopher and bishop George Berkeley published *The Analyst*, whose subtitle addresses it to "an infidel mathematician" and asks whether the objects, principles and inferences of the modern analysis are more distinctly conceived or more evidently deduced than the mysteries of religious faith. The increments, he observed, are "neither finite Quantities nor Quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the Ghosts of departed Quantities?"[^s1-berkeley]

The phrase has outlived the argument, but the argument was precise. A quantity that is divided by and then set equal to nothing has been treated as something and as nothing in one calculation, and the method's success did not make the treatment coherent. Berkeley was asking the mathematicians to admit that their certainty rested on a trust they could not certify, the trust Russell would later want mathematics to replace and §0/1 · #3 traced through Gödel's theorems. The answer took more than a century. Augustin-Louis Cauchy in the 1820s and Karl Weierstrass after him rebuilt the calculus on limits, so that the derivative is no longer a ratio of vanished quantities but the value a ratio approaches as its increment shrinks, and the division by zero is never performed. Their standard definition asks that for every small positive quantity, traditionally written ε, a corresponding closeness can be found, and the engineers of the optimiser, following a convention that descends from that definition, use the same letter for the small constant they keep in their denominator.[^s1-limits] Kṛṣṇa's argument about the *khahara*, met beside Bhāskara's algebra in §1 · #1, had reasoned in the same direction centuries earlier: the smaller the divisor, the greater the quotient, and no specified quotient is the greatest, so the endless quantity is approached through a process and never reached as a value. In 1966 Abraham Robinson showed that infinitesimals, nonzero quantities smaller than every positive real number, could be given a consistent meaning in an enlarged number system, where the increment is never zero, the division is legitimate, and the standard part of the result recovers the derivative.[^s1-robinson] For a real $x$ and a nonzero infinitesimal $\varepsilon$,

$$
\frac{(x+\varepsilon)^{2}-x^{2}}{\varepsilon}=2x+\varepsilon,
$$

and taking the standard part gives $2x$, the derivative of $x^{2}$. Substituting zero for the increment before dividing would give $0/0$. Robinson's construction put Berkeley's ghosts on a consistent footing: zero, which had begun as a punctuator in a Babylonian column, became through calculus the limit that the most powerful method of modern science approaches without ever reaching.[^s1-kaplan-calculus] What differentiation loses along the way, the constant that integration cannot recover, is the matter of §3 · #0.[^s1-constant]

§0/1 named the gap that recurs at every such edge, and defined the immutable gap, after following Gödel, as the non-coincidence between what a theory can express and what it can establish, which relocates with every enlargement of the theory and returns through it, so that it was never one withheld fact awaiting discovery. Division by zero is the mathematical form of that gap at the level of number, and the history just followed shows it relocating as Gödel's did. A field refuses `1/0`; the projective line adds a point and then cannot divide infinity by infinity; the wheel defines every division and weakens $x/x=1$; Robinson's infinitesimals make the derivative a true quotient and must then take a standard part to return to the real numbers. Each enlargement of the frame makes the old impossibility possible and produces a new one, because the operation that returns a determinate quantity to its zero always asks the system to account for the condition of its own computation, and that condition cannot be one more of its numbers. Inside a field the result is a proof, and its warrant is mathematical. Identifying this frame-disclosing edge with the immutable gap is the essay's own argued claim, bounded as it was for Gödel: the mathematics does not prove our notation; it gives the return of the determinate one upon its ground a mathematical form in one register and shows that form recurring through every attempt to remove it.

Psychoanalysis supplies a second history of the same structure, which §4 · #0 follows.[^s1-freud] It admitted into a science a term for what consciousness does not contain, as arithmetic admitted zero, gave it rules of interpretation, and found at the edge where consciousness tries to account for the condition of its own activity that the term could not be mastered as one more content. It is a parallel of operations, since Freud's admission of the unconscious and arithmetic's admission of zero have their own objects, methods and evidence: a determinate system takes in a sign for what it cannot contain, gains great power by operating with it, and at its edge is made to disclose its own frame.

The calculator's "Error" looked like a dead end, the point past which there is nothing to be said. Followed through the field, the zero ring, the projective line, the sphere, the wheel and the meadow, the floating-point standard, the training run and the calculus, the edge turns out to be among the most informative places in mathematics, where a system shows what it assumes, what it protects and what it has chosen to give up. Zero, which entered arithmetic as an empty place and then as a number with rules, ends its mathematical history as the term at which every number system is made to declare itself. A sign that can do that has earned the right to stand for the condition §0/1 borrowed it to name, and the loan can now be returned.

### #5→0 · The Loan Returns

A friend borrows a book and keeps it for most of a year. When it comes back there is a train ticket marking a page near the end, the spine has softened, and in the margins, lightly in pencil, there are underlinings, a question mark beside one paragraph, an exclamation beside another and, in one place, a sentence of the friend's own that argues with the author. It is the same book, and anyone could check that every printed word is as it was. It is also not the book that was lent, because it now carries the reading it went through, and its owner, opening it, meets the author and the friend at once and reads the familiar pages differently. Many of us have lent a book and had it returned in that state, and some of us have returned one so. Nobody calls the pencil marks interest, though in the oldest sense of the word they are exactly that. §0/1 · #4 traced *interest* to *inter-esse*, what lies between, and saw that the charge on a loan prices the between itself and counts it to one side. A book returned with its margins full brings back what lay between lender and borrower without pricing it: the reading, the relation, the thinking that passed through the object while it was away.

§0/1 borrowed zero. It wrote `0` for the pole of knowing that cannot enter its own count as one more object, and `0/1` for that pole's relation to the determinate world it knows, before showing where the sign came from or what it could bear. It was a loan in the full sense. §0/1 could use the sign because readers already knew zero from school and trusted that it meant something, and §0/1's own account of trust says that such reliance is real and must in time become answerable. Zero has now been followed from a gap a Babylonian reader filled from context to the term at which every modern number system declares its laws, and it comes back to §0/1 carrying what passed through it on the way: the same sign, every stroke as it was, and a history of offices it did not have when it was lent.

The two signs §0/1 borrowed do exact mathematical work together in a construction a German mathematician and a French clockmaker found independently in the nineteenth century. Write the two expressions `0/1` and `1/0` side by side and treat them not as quotients but as pairs of numbers, a top and a bottom. Between any two neighbouring pairs, $a/b$ and $c/d$, insert a new pair formed by adding the tops and adding the bottoms,

$$
\frac{a+c}{b+d},
$$

which is called the mediant of the two. Between `0/1` and `1/0` the mediant is $1/1$. Insert again, between `0/1` and $1/1$ and between $1/1$ and `1/0`, and the row becomes

$$
\frac01,\ \frac12,\ \frac11,\ \frac21,\ \frac10,
$$

and once more,

$$
\frac01,\ \frac13,\ \frac12,\ \frac23,\ \frac11,\ \frac32,\ \frac21,\ \frac31,\ \frac10.
$$

Continued for ever, the process puts every positive fraction in the row exactly once, in lowest terms and in its proper order.[^s1-stern-brocot] No division has been performed, and in particular nothing has been divided by zero: the mediant adds the parts of the pairs and never asks what `1/0` is worth. It is called the Stern–Brocot tree, after Moritz Stern, who described it in 1858, and the clockmaker Achille Brocot, who found it a few years later while looking for gear ratios that would approximate awkward fractions.[^s1-stern-brocot-history] A clockmaker who needs a train of gears to turn at a ratio no pair of small wheels can give exactly must find the fraction with small numbers that comes closest, and the tree lays out every such approximation in order. Each step down it gives a closer fraction with larger numbers, so a craftsman can stop wherever the teeth become too many to cut. In Brocot's hands the tree that grows between `0/1` and `1/0` made a mechanism keep time, and its two starting points are the two expressions this essay has been calling the two orientations of one relation.

Two properties make the construction work, and both can be checked by hand. First, the mediant always lies between its parents. If $a/b<c/d$, with positive bottoms, then $bc-ad>0$, and

$$
\frac{a+c}{b+d}-\frac ab=\frac{bc-ad}{b(b+d)}>0,\qquad \frac cd-\frac{a+c}{b+d}=\frac{bc-ad}{d(b+d)}>0,
$$

so the new fraction is greater than the left neighbour and less than the right. Second, neighbours in the row always satisfy $bc-ad=1$, a property that holds for the starting pair, since $0\cdot0$ subtracted from $1\cdot1$ gives one, and that each insertion preserves. Because of it every fraction the construction produces is already in lowest terms, since any number dividing both $a+c$ and $b+d$ must divide $c(b+d)-d(a+c)=bc-ad=1$. As `1/0` has positive top and zero bottom, the first proof does not cover it, and it needs no covering: it is the right-hand edge of the whole field of positive fractions, and its office in the construction is to bound, not to be evaluated.

`0/1` is arithmetic's most docile fraction, equal to zero and defined everywhere. `1/0` is arithmetic's one forbidden gesture, the quotient §1 · #4 showed no field can supply. Set side by side as a boundary pair, they generate between them, without either being consumed, every positive ratio there is. As a quotient the forbidden gesture produced only "Error", and as a boundary it becomes the condition of a whole field. Stern and Brocot read nothing philosophical into this, and nothing in the construction requires it. It shows that the two orientations of the essay's relation, the one that computes and the one that cannot, are already in plain mathematics the two edges between which every ratio is generated, and that the relation between them is generative because neither is reduced to the other. §0/1 wrote `0/1 = 1/0` in the relational register and kept the arithmetic seam between the defined and the undefined. In the Stern–Brocot tree that seam is productive, its two sides held apart as edges so that the whole positive field of ratio can grow between them.

The loan can now be returned, because the sign has acquired the offices §0/1 needed it to bear. When §0/1 wrote `0/1`, it said that `0` names the pole that cannot enter its own count as one object among others, that `1` names determination, the first formed and identifiable something, and that the stroke is the relation in which they are spoken together, dividing and joining at once. Each of those statements now has a history behind it. Zero kept a place open in a Babylonian column so that the marks around it held their value, and the `0` of our notation keeps open the place of the knower, so that whatever is known holds its position relative to someone. Zero became, in Brahmagupta's verses, the additive identity, the quantity that leaves every other quantity as it is, and the `0` of our notation is self-identity, the One that remains itself through every determination without altering what it determines. Von Neumann's construction gathered zero into the first one, so that the first determinate unit is the act of holding zero, and the `1` of our notation is that holding, the determinate mark whose entire content is its relation to a ground it does not contain. Zero crossed by a stroke could be mistaken for a letter, a vowel or an object, and the crossed `Ø` of our notation records the same mistake made with the self, the stroke taken for what it crosses. Zero in a denominator made every number system declare its laws, and the `1/0` of our notation is the determinate turning toward its ground and finding there, in place of one more value, the frame in which all its values were defined. History has now warranted each term of the notation, and the warrant runs both ways, since the notation makes the history legible as its own prehistory and the history gives the notation the exactness of everything mathematics learned to do with zero.

§0/1 named two senses of *one* that the two marks carry: zero bears the singular One, self-identity, and one bears the manifest All, self-difference. That distinction is older than the notation. A sentence in the twelfth-century Latin of al-Khwārizmī's treatise, the very text that carried the little circle west, says that "one is the root of every number, and is outside number": root, "because through it every number is found", and outside number, "because it is found by itself, that is, without any other number".[^s1-khwarizmi-one] The one that counts and the One that is not counted are named together there, in one word, before zero had been asked to bear the second. History has since sorted them into two signs. Bhāskara's *khahara* is the One as arithmetic could approach it, a quantity unaltered by everything inserted into it or taken from it, which he could describe only by the image of the God unchanged through the creation and destruction of worlds. The zero ring is the All as arithmetic is forced to refuse it, every number collapsed into one element, the many identified with the one by the abolition of their difference. Between these two, ordinary number lives by refusing both: it will not divide by zero, so its one never returns to the zero, and it will not let zero equal one, so its many never collapse. The two senses of *one* are what that double refusal protects. Number cannot hold the One and the All together without either severing them or fusing them, and the history of zero shows mathematics reaching at its edges for another register in which they can be held: Bhāskara's theology, Frege's logic of self-identity, the projective point that unites all far directions, the boundary pair from which every ratio grows.

Every return can be tested by what it does with the break it meets, and the history of zero lets us watch that test performed in arithmetic before we use it anywhere else. When a determinate `1` turns back toward the zero it carries, it first meets that zero as its own division, `1/0`, which arrives as a break: a quotient no number supplies, the "Error" on the calculator. The break can be refused, as a field refuses it, by forbidding the operation and carrying on as though nothing had been asked, which is dia-ballein's proper cut: difference is preserved and return is forbidden. It can be fused, as in the zero ring, where the whole system collapses into one element, cancellation `(−1)+(+1)=0` carried through every pair, and the return succeeds by abolishing every difference. The other collapse of dia-ballein, appropriation, would hand the span to one end and leave the return in the appropriator's hands. Or it can be carried, retained through the operations that follow until it can be recognised for what it is. Bhāskara's *Līlāvatī* shows the third course in the plainest arithmetic. A number multiplied by zero is not computed away; it is kept as "a multiple of cipher",[^s1-lilavati-carried] the zero written beside it and carried through the following steps, and when a later division by zero arrives the two meet and the number returns unchanged. Zero has been retained under the one through the whole calculation, and the division that would otherwise have been a break becomes the moment at which what was carried is recovered. That is the shape of the recognition §0/1 wrote as `0/1` and completed as `(0/1)/(1/0)`, syn-ballein in plain arithmetic: the break is carried instead of being refused or fused, and carrying it lets the source be recognised in the place where the calculation seemed to fail. Bhāskara's rule does not prove our notation, and it shows a twelfth-century arithmetic that had learned to keep a zero in play through the operation that would otherwise have destroyed the record of what it held.

Our notation is itself subject to the test, and a small exercise makes the point. On a blank sheet of paper we write `1`, a first mark. We acknowledge the paper on which it stands by writing `0` beside it and drawing the stroke between them, so that the sheet now carries `0/1`. Now the whole inscription is a figure on the page, one more determinate mark, and relative to the paper it is a `1`: the written zero is a mark in the field of marks, as §0/1 said, and its work is to keep the unobjectifiable operative in what can be written. Then we erase the figure, and the marks go while the sheet and the hand remain as they were, and whoever erased the figure can see, distinguish and write again, knowing now what the inscription was doing. `5→0`, the Möbius return, has the same shape. An achieved figure gives itself back to the condition from which it was drawn, and what returns is no blank but a capacity that has learned something from the figure it made.

Śaiva sources make the inversion of the digits exact and let us place the means where it belongs. In the Trika, §0/1 read Śiva, Śakti and Nara as the *I*, the *you* and the *it* of one consciousness, the subjective, the subjective-objective and the objective aspects of one reality, and set them in `0/1` as Subjective Immediacy at the zero, Objective Internality at the slash and World at the one. Śiva is the Subject; Śakti is his power, the power to differentiate, to take on identities as means of knowing and as objects of knowledge; Nara is the bound and manifest world. Zero's history shows why the distinction between Śiva and Śakti matters so much and is so easily lost. That power of differentiation, the slash, is the psyche, the mind, the whole working apparatus of knowing, and Western mathematics developed that power to its extraordinary exactness in rules, constructions, completions and machines. Taken by itself, the power presents its products as everything there is, and the zero of the knower becomes in its accounts either one more product or a void. Returned to its place, it is recognised as Śakti, the power of a Subject it does not itself constitute. Zero's return is the return of the West, with all its exactness, to the subject from which its exactness proceeds, and it is equally the return of zero to the metaphysical domain in which it was first named, now carrying everything the West made of it.

Binary arithmetic shows how much depends on the register chosen, because its two digits have been read in both. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz worked out the arithmetic of base two in the last decades of the seventeenth century and understood it from the first as a theological image. In May 1696 he sent Duke Rudolph August of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel a short piece on the "wonderful origin of all numbers from 1 and 0", which, he wrote, represents as in a mirror the creation, or the origin of things from God and otherwise from nothing. In a New Year's letter of 1697 to the same duke he proposed a medal to commemorate the discovery, bearing the inscription *Imago Creationis*, the image of creation, and the motto *omnibus ex nihilo ducendis sufficit unum*, "to draw all things from nothing, one suffices": all numbers can be written with one and nothing, as God drew all things from nothing.[^s1-leibniz] When he published the system in 1703 he added a correspondence he had learned of from the Jesuit Joachim Bouvet, then in China, between the binary numbers from zero to sixty-three and the sixty-four hexagrams of the *Yijing*, the *Book of Changes*, each hexagram a stack of six lines, broken or whole.[^s1-hexagrams] The sixty-four hexagrams are two raised to the sixth power, the number §0/1 reached when it took the binary through six positions, and §3 returns to that count. Leibniz read the digits in a particular way: for him one was God, or unity, and zero was nothing, the void from which creation drew all things, so that the binary imaged creation *ex nihilo*, in which everything that exists is composed of being and its absence.

Our notation reads the same two digits the other way round, and the inversion states most clearly what the return of zero has changed. In `0/1`, zero is not nothing. It is the One, the pole of self-identity that "Subject" and "God" both sign, and one is the All, the manifest field of determinations through which that pole becomes articulate. Leibniz's binary puts God at the one and nothing at the zero, and so reads creation as the one drawing all things out of a void that contributes only absence. In that reading zero is externalised, set over against the one as its opposite, and the whole of what exists is counted as so many ones distinguished from so many nothings. Computation later took up the same digits without the theology, as two determinate states of a switch, and in that use both digits belong, as §0/1 said, to the `1`-side of the symbolon: the machine's zero is one more determinate state, and nothing in the machine plays the part Leibniz gave to God or the part our notation gives the uncounted pole. Our reading puts the One at the zero and finds in the one its manifestation, so that the binary becomes the relation of a source to its expression, in which the zero is present in every one as the ground it carries. Both readings are supported by the same two digits, and binary logic is therefore ambiguous at its root. Zero can be read as nothing, set over against the one as its opposite, or as ground, the condition through which every one is what it is. That ambiguity poses the next question: once zero and one are retained together, why does binary reason so often externalise zero and set two signed ones against each other? §2 distinguishes the two logics of two that answer it.

A tenants' meeting votes ten to nine to repair the lift, and the ten is exact. The caretaker, who lives in the basement and was never on the register, objects, and the objection does not dispute the sum. It asks whether the register was the right unit, and the answer changes what the next count includes. Latin *ratio*, from *reri*, to reckon or think, carried that double sense, a calculation and the account a person gives of it, and the history of zero is in part the history of ratio learning that every reckoning stands in a frame it did not reckon. Once the terms of a count are stated, they too can receive an answer, and the decisive gain is often a changed criterion that lets something previously excluded count, as a zero once let an empty column count.

We received the sign zero as children before we could examine what supported it, §0/1 borrowed it on the same trust, and this section has examined some of what supports it, and the examination has changed what we can entrust to it without showing that the first reliance was a mistake. §0/1 called the first reliance *fides* and the renewed entrustment that follows examination *credere*, from which our words for credit come. A loan is the plainest case of *credere*: a lender entrusts something to a borrower because the borrower's word is credited, and the return completes the relation by showing the credit to have been warranted. Zero's return completes §0/1's loan in that sense and does more, because what returns is a sign the borrower can now use with knowledge of its history. The history certifies what the sign has been made to do, and the use remains a commitment: naming the Subject with it is an act of the one who names, answerable to what the naming discloses.

At the kitchen table the child writes 105 correctly the second time, with the small circle in the middle holding the tens open so that the hundred keeps its place. She does not know that the circle she has written was named for the sky, given rules in verse, carried west by merchants, built into the foundations of number, crossed to tell it from a letter, and made the edge at which every number system declares itself. She does not need to know, any more than the merchants of Pisa needed to share the metaphysics of Bhillamāla. The sign works without its history. History matters to those of us who ask, as she will one day ask, what the circle is and why something that counts nothing should hold everything else in its place, and the answer it returns is that the circle marks the place of the one who counts, present in every count and never counted, whose position decides the value of every mark it does not fill.
