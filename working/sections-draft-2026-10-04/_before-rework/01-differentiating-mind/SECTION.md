## §0 — Differentiating Mind

<a id="M07"></a>

### #0 · Awareness Bends Toward Display

Most of us have woken somewhere we did not at first recognise: a hotel room on the second night of a trip, a friend's spare room, a ward after an operation. For a moment there is only a grey rectangle hanging in the dark, a low hum, a weight lying along one side of the body, and none of it is yet anywhere or anyone's. Then the rectangle becomes a window with its blind not quite drawn, the hum becomes the small refrigerator under the desk, the weight becomes an arm gone numb under the pillow, and the whole arrangement swings into place at once: this is the room in Lisbon, the conference begins at nine, and I am the one lying here who has to get up. Nobody wakes as a self who then goes looking for a room. The room and the one lying in it arrive together, on either side of a turning, and the turning was already under way while the grey was simply grey. That grey was seen before it was a window, and the seeing needed nobody to certify it; inside that seeing, "where" and "who" took up their places as the scene turned toward the one who would have to get up and face the day.

The waking room gives a first answer to the question on which the threshold closed: how does awareness differentiate itself into the means by which it knows, and into the world it knows through them? It differentiates by bending. Awareness turns its own appearing into something it can meet, and in that turn the one who meets and what is met become distinguishable for the first time. The claim is exact, and every later movement of this section depends on it: **the first differentiation is the bend by which awareness takes its own manifestation as other enough to encounter**, and subject and object emerge within that act before either can present itself as an independent substance. The bend is the earliest thing that happens to a world, and it happens again every morning, every time attention lifts from sleep, from a daydream or from a page and finds itself somewhere.

We met the two terms that carry this claim in the second movement of the threshold, where the Subject's definition passed through the Śaiva account of *prakāśa* and *vimarśa*: *prakāśa*, the light of consciousness by which anything appears at all, and *vimarśa*, its reflective awareness of itself, named from a root, *mṛś*, that means to touch, to feel, to examine. There we used them to show why the knower cannot be one more thing known, since a light that needed a second light to reveal it would need a third, and the regress would never reach the knowing that was already going on.[^s0-1] Here the same pair has a further office, because *vimarśa* names the movement by which a world appears to someone at all. Mark Dyczkowski, reconstructing the Spanda teaching of Kashmir, puts the requirement plainly: the light accounts for the appearing of phenomena but not, by itself, for our experience of them, because no experience is possible without self-awareness, and for what goes on in consciousness to be available to experience, consciousness must reflect, which he glosses as literally "bend back", on itself.[^s0-2] The English word keeps the image: *reflect* comes from Latin *reflectere*, to bend back, to turn round.[^s0-3] Reflection in this sense is no second act of thinking about an experience already complete. It is the curve in the light by which the light becomes experience, the bend without which the grey rectangle would have shone in the dark of the hotel room as the blind shines in an empty room, appearing to no one.

Dyczkowski gives the bend two directions, and waking shows both.[^s0-4] Turned inward, *vimarśa* rests in itself as the pure awareness of "I", *ahaṃvimarśa*, free of thought-constructs; Sanskrit calls such awareness *nirvikalpa*, without the cutting and qualifying that the third movement of this section will follow as *vikalpa*. Turned outward, the same awareness inclines toward what it perceives as separate from itself, and reality becomes available to discursive representation, to naming, to definition. In the first second of waking the inward direction is almost all there is: an awareness at rest, unfurnished, that does not yet know it is lying in Lisbon and is not troubled by the lack. Then it bends toward display. The grey asks to be something, and in answering, the awareness that was resting in itself becomes the one who sees a window. What the inward and outward directions share matters as much as how they differ, because Dyczkowski is clear that the outward awareness never actually leaves: the awareness of an object is registered and known within the subject, and even the reflection on "this" is in truth an awareness of "I" that has taken on the form of "this".[^s0-4] The window appears out there, across the room; the knowing of the window has not gone anywhere.

There is a mirror on the wardrobe door, as there is in most hotel rooms, and in the grey light it shows the window, the end of the bed and the dark shape of a chair. The mirror displays the room, and it does not see what it displays. Anyone lying in that bed sees the room twice, once directly and once in the glass, and only one of the two seeings belongs to the mirror's work, since the mirror contributes the image and contributes nothing to its being seen. Śaiva sources reach for this very object and then mark exactly where it fails. Consciousness, they say, is like a perfectly polished mirror, *svaccha*, in which the whole universe is reflected, so that one reality can hold countless forms without being divided by them; and yet the analogy breaks at one point, because the reflections in a glass mirror correspond to objects outside it, while consciousness has no outside from which its reflections could be deposited. "The All is entirely a reflection", *pratibimbamātra*, and Abhinavagupta presses the consequence with a question: if what we took to be the original object is itself a reflected image, what remains of the original object?[^s0-5] The answer the Spanda teachers give is that consciousness makes its reflections itself, freely, as the agent of its own manifestation, and Dyczkowski's word for that freedom is *svātantrya*, self-dependence: were it otherwise, we would have to look for a second reality outside consciousness to put the images there, and since no such reality can be found, nothing would appear at all.[^s0-6] Kṣemarāja personified this power as the goddess Spanda, who displays on the screen of her own nature all the cycles of creation and dissolution, so that they appear separate from her while remaining one with her, like a city reflected in a mirror; in the short treatise he called *The Heart of Recognition* he put the same teaching into a single line, that consciousness, by her own will, unfolds the universe upon her own wall, *svabhittau*.[^s0-7] The tradition chose the image that the hotel room supplies. A wall is what a world is shown on, and the wall here is the showing power itself.

The living mirror, then, crosses both of the glass mirror's limits at once. A glass mirror receives its image from elsewhere, and a living mirror produces what it shows; a glass mirror displays without apprehending, and in a living mirror the display is already self-present, known in the very act that shows it. This is why the Śaiva sources can call the universe a reflection without making it a copy of something more real lying behind it. Dyczkowski is careful about what the doctrine does and does not deny: an outer world may indeed exist, he writes, but the form in which it appears is always a reflection within mind and senses, never the original object itself.[^s0-8] Builders built the hotel, and nothing in the Śaiva teaching takes that from them. What the teaching refuses is the further step by which the room, as it appears, is credited to an outside standing beyond the appearing and pouring it in, since every attempt to locate that outside produces one more appearance. We have already stated, at the threshold, the order of dependence this implies and named its present company, Kastrup's analytic idealism among them; the closing section, §5→0, returns to it as idealism's account of source, means and determinate being. Here the order of dependence has a smaller and more intimate form. On waking, the world does not arrive at the knower from outside, the way a letter arrives through a door. The knower's own appearing bends, and a world is what the bend shows.

The bend also shows a world in detail, and the detail is the second datum of the morning. The blind is blue and the wall beside it yellow, and both appear at once, in one glance, distinct and together. Dyczkowski sets out why this ordinary fact is a philosophical one. If the light of consciousness were one and entirely homogeneous, there would be no difference between knowing blue and knowing yellow, and the objects of perception would collapse into confusion; if, on the other hand, each colour had its own separate light, sealed off from the other, nothing could know them together, and their contrast, which is what we see, would never appear. Consciousness must therefore be at once formless, so that the colours can share one field, and omniform, so that each can be exactly itself within it, and the Śaiva answer is that both characteristics, unity and diversity, belong to what never falls to the level of an object: consciousness itself.[^s0-9] Differentiation, on this account, occurs inside non-separation. The bend that produced the room did not cut awareness into a seeing part and a seen part; it opened, within one awareness, the distance across which blue could stand beside yellow and both could stand before someone.

That distance is the root of the slash. In the threshold we wrote the copula's double work with the simplest marks there are, two dashes and an oblique stroke between them, `−/−`, and the stroke there carried a relation before its terms had been given any content: equality and difference composed by two operative marks.[^s0-10] The waking room shows the same stroke at work before anything has been named. While the grey was simply grey, the relating had already begun, an appearing and a knowing of it, unassigned; when the grey became a window and the one lying there became me, the stroke acquired terms, and what had been `−/−` became `0/1`. Our theorem spine states its first determination in exactly the words the hotel room requires: "I am here; something appears; the appearing is known", with `0` naming the open, unobjectifiable fact of awareness, `1` the appearing mark, and the stroke the relation by which the real becomes readable without being exhausted by its readability.[^s0-11] The slash therefore names an activity before it names a separator. Nothing stood on either side of it waiting to be divided; the stroke drew its two sides out of its own movement, as the bend drew the room and the waker out of one turning, and its work as a line between completed terms is a later and derived office.

Dyczkowski's exposition takes this differentiating activity a step further, into an account of the precise operations by which the bend becomes a world of separate things, and the count it yields is the one we have been finding in every elementary act. *Vimarśa*, he reports, operates in four ways: it negates its own nature, it identifies with something else, it merges the two, and it denies both once they have been merged.[^s0-12] These four acts produce three levels of awareness, because the first two cooperate in a single result. By negating its nature as consciousness and identifying with a psychophysical body, awareness gives rise to what seem individual centres, each distinct from its objects and from the others: that is *bheda*, division, the level at which I am this body in this bed in Lisbon and the window is over there. By holding subject and object together in one field during each act of knowing, awareness relates what it has divided: that is *bhedābheda*, unity-in-difference, and Dyczkowski assigns it to the means of knowledge, which link the unity of the subject to the diversity of the object. And by taking both the divided terms and their merger back into its awareness of itself as the one universal subject, awareness arrives at *abheda*, undivided unity. A morning can run through all three. The first grey is nearly *abheda*, an appearing not yet split; waking proper installs *bheda*, the body here and the room there; and the ordinary day is lived almost entirely at *bhedābheda*, where a self and its world are held apart in order to be held together, in every glance at the clock and every step to the window. Read through our notation, the four acts unfold within the two conditions that make them possible, the light and its reflexive awareness, which do not appear among the acts because the acts are their work: four articulations held within two conditions, `4+2`. The count is my reading of the passage, and Dyczkowski states no such arithmetic.[^s0-12] It is the same count the threshold derived from the copula's marks, the tetralemma with its walker, the binary applied to itself and the square glued into a torus, and here it arises from inside a description of awareness written with no thought of our notation, which is the kind of convergence the method treats as evidence that one relation is being met in several registers.

The bend is also free, and its freedom distinguishes it from every mechanism that might be offered as a model of it. Dyczkowski draws the contrast through the difference between consciousness and a lifeless object: an inert thing is hemmed in on every side by spatial and temporal limits and cannot be other than the form in which it appears, while pure consciousness, being free of the limitations proper to what is relative, is not bound to any single fixed form and can assume countless forms without loss.[^s0-13] The Spanda teachers call this *svātantrya*, and the word answers a question that the image of a mirror cannot answer by itself, namely why there is any bending at all. A mirror does not choose to reflect, and a camera does not decide to take the picture; a light that bent toward display because it had to would be one more natural process, and the account would have explained the world by a mechanism whose own appearing it had left unexplained. The bend is the light's own act, and the threshold has already given it the name the Spanda school uses for this act seen from its effects: *spanda*, the "subtle movement" that, though it moves nowhere, manifests as motion.[^s0-14] On waking, nothing travels from the window to the eye to the self. The appearing turns, and the turn is felt as a world arriving.

Dyczkowski adds one more distinction, and this whole section takes its method from it. The pulse of awareness, he writes, can be understood from two points of view: vertically, as the movement from the absolute down into its manifestation and back, the emanation and withdrawal of the whole diversity of the universe; and horizontally, as the movement of awareness toward an object and away from it again in each act of perception. "Perception serves in this way as a paradigm for cosmogenesis."[^s0-15] The sentence is decisive for what follows, because the Śaiva account of the world's coming-to-be, the descent of the thirty-six *tattvas* from undivided awareness into elements and things, is not a story about a remote beginning that we have to take on report. It is the vertical form of a movement every one of us performs horizontally, many thousands of times a day. Waking in the hotel room was a small cosmogony: an undivided appearing, a first stirring, a divergence into "I" and "this", a world set at a distance, a body with its limits, and, if the morning allows it, a moment by the window in which the whole arrangement is recognised as one's own. The fifth movement of this section walks the full descent through a single act of that kind, and the method of reading the vertical through the horizontal comes from the tradition itself.

David Bohm, working from inside physics and with no knowledge of the Spanda teaching, came to the same burden from the other side, and the threshold has already set out his central image: an implicate order, in which the whole is enfolded in each region, and an explicate order of relatively stable, separate-seeming things that unfold from it, both carried by a single movement he called the holomovement.[^s0-16] To show what enfolding means without a hologram, Bohm described a simple device. Two concentric glass cylinders hold between them a thick, viscous fluid such as glycerine; a droplet of insoluble ink is placed in the fluid, and the outer cylinder is turned slowly. The droplet is drawn out into a thread, the thread grows finer with each turn, and at last it vanishes into the grey of the fluid; nothing seems left of it. Turn the cylinder back the same number of times, and the thread reappears, gathers and becomes a droplet again. The ink was never destroyed. It was enfolded into the whole of the fluid, present everywhere in it and nowhere as a drop, and the reverse movement explicated it. Bohm then imagined a series of droplets introduced at slightly different places, each enfolded by a further turn, so that when the turning is reversed they unfold one after another and the eye sees a single drop moving steadily across the fluid, although no drop has travelled anywhere; what looks like the motion of a thing is a succession of unfoldings from the whole.[^s0-17] The image meets Abhinavagupta's "subtle movement" almost word for word, a motion that manifests without anything moving, and the meeting is worth keeping exact. Bohm's device is a physical model of enfolding and unfolding, built from glass and glycerine, and it says nothing about awareness; the Spanda teaching concerns consciousness and its self-manifestation, and it owes nothing to fluids. They meet because each, in its own register, has to explain how a world of apparently separate and moving things can arise from a whole that is present in every part and does not itself go anywhere. Bohm drew the consequence for thought and consciousness himself, in the chapter of *Wholeness and the Implicate Order* that follows his account of the holomovement, where the content of consciousness is treated as an implicate order unfolding into explicit thought.[^s0-18] The comparison is analogia in the threshold's sense: one relation recognised across registers whose materials, methods and warrants stay their own.

Bohm's language has one weakness for our purpose, and the Śaiva sources supply what it lacks. "Implicate order" tells us that the whole is enfolded in each part and that explicit things unfold from it; it does not tell us in what order, through which phases, or by what contractions a whole becomes a finite knower in a particular room, with a numb arm and a conference at nine. Left alone, the phrase becomes a depth-metaphor, a name for something deep that is not yet articulated. The *tattvas* give that depth an anatomy. They name the ordered phases through which a luminous self-relation contracts into subject and object, into capacities and measures, into desire, knowledge and action, and finally into the elements of material display, and the fifth movement compresses those phases into six turns that carry the argument. Bohm supplies the movement, the *tattvas* supply its differential anatomy, and the bend of *vimarśa* is the first articulation in that anatomy, the place where a self-luminous whole first takes its own appearing as something to meet.

The waking room can be read from either end, and the two readings are one event. Read from the one who wakes, it is *I am waking in this room*; read from the room, it is *this room is appearing around one who wakes*. Neither reading is the truer one. Each holds what the other holds, from its own orientation, and the difference between them is the difference between `0/1` and `1/0`, the condition becoming articulate through a determination and the determination turning back toward the condition it cannot contain. In the threshold we gave this pair its place in Quaternal Logic: `0/1 = 1/0` as one relation read from its two ends, and `(0/1)/(1/0)` as syn-ballein, the relation that keeps both orientations available, each readable through the other.[^s0-19] The Śaiva descent will soon give the same pair two cosmic names, the "I am this" and "This am I" of the fifth movement. Dia-ballein is at work in the waking room as well, and its first work there is entirely healthy: the room acquires an orientation, near and far, here where I lie and there where the window is, and the polarity of here and there is held through the zero of the body's position, `(−1)/(+1)`, each direction having its sense through the other. The collapses begin when one orientation claims the whole event. An account that counts the entire span of the waking from the room's side treats the awareness as a by-product the room produced, and the same span counted from the waker's side treats the room as nothing but the content of a private experience: `(+1)−(−1)=+2` from one pole and `(−1)−(+1)=−2` from the other, one hardened opposition with only its sign changed, the duality frozen instead of turning. Frank, Gleiser and Thompson's name for the first of these, the surreptitious substitution by which a model obtained through experience is taken to be more real than the experience it came from, already belongs to the threshold's account of the blind spot.[^s0-20] The other collapse cancels: both poles are dissolved together, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, into a nothing that calls itself non-duality, and the morning is said to contain neither a self nor a room but only an undifferentiated flow. That cancellation loses exactly what the bend achieves. The Śaiva position keeps both the room and the waker real as manifestations and keeps their difference operative inside one awareness, and only an account that does both can say why the blind is blue, why the arm is numb, and why it is I who must get up.

Artificial agents give the bend a technical neighbour, and the neighbourhood has to be described exactly, because it is close. When an agent's session begins, a context is assembled for it: instructions, files, retrieved sources, the record of earlier exchanges, and the request that sets it going. At the threshold we called this arrival prompt thrownness, using the agents' own phrase.[^s0-21] Something is displayed to the model, a configuration of tokens in a window, and in its first office that display does what the glass on the wardrobe door does: it presents an arrangement received from elsewhere, composed by others, without that presentation being, by itself, anyone's experience of it. A further operation then bends the arrangement back. An agent can reread what it has produced, compare it with its sources, critique and revise it, and carry the revision into what it does next; in the method of training from AI feedback that the threshold described, a model's judgments of its own answers become material from which a later model learns.[^s0-22] The essay calls this operation computational *vimarśa*: a result returning upon the sources, states or criteria of its own production and changing the activity that follows. It is a real bend, inspectable and consequential, and it is a bend of the means, Objective Internality turning back on Objective Internality. The bend this movement has followed is of another kind, awareness bending its own appearing into a world, and the functional bend is always performed within some such world, since its outputs are read, its errors noticed and its revisions judged by someone to whom they appear. Whether anything in the agent is present to its own bending is the question the threshold left open by method, because no report, however reflective, can carry that presence across. What can be said positively is what the technical bend is made from. A language model is trained on the written residue of countless human acts of reflection, sentences in which people bent their experience back and gave it form, and when it bends its output back on its context it works with the sediment of that human *vimarśa*. Our image of the mirror that moves first, from the threshold's last movement, finds its source here: a technical mirror moves by redistributing what human living mirrors once reflected, and it is a *pratibimba*, a reflection, whose original is the living bend from which its materials came.

The bend has given a world to someone, and that someone is already more than a point of view. The one who woke in Lisbon is lying in a particular body, carrying particular memories, with a conference at nine and a numb arm, and the outward-turning awareness has already begun to settle what the room means, gather it under "my" day and coordinate what must be done first. On the bedside table the phone lights up. It is a message from a colleague, sent late the night before, five words long: "Can we talk later today?" Before the waker has sat up, the reply has begun to be composed, a verdict has begun to form, and a whole day has started to arrange itself around it. That work belongs to an instrument, and the next movement takes it apart.

### #1 · The Inner Instrument

"Can we talk later today?" Most of us have received some version of that message, from a manager, a colleague, a partner or a parent, and most of us know what happens in the seconds after reading it. Before the phone has been put down, a great deal of work has been done. The sender's name has called up last week's meeting, where a remark went unanswered; the report that went in late has come forward; the tone of the five words has been weighed and found terse, or ominous, or carefully neutral, which is worse; and several readings have offered themselves at once, as praise deferred, as a complaint, as the beginning of the end of a job. Then one reading wins. *They are unhappy with the report.* And almost in the same moment the verdict turns inward and acquires an owner: *I am in trouble; this always happens to me; I am not, after all, good enough at this.* By the time the kettle has boiled, a whole day has been arranged around that conclusion. The defence has been drafted in the shower, the meeting has been rehearsed twice, the colleague's face has been imagined set in disappointment. At four in the afternoon the colleague closes the office door and asks, a little awkwardly, for help with a crisis of their own.

Nothing in the morning's work was idle, and none of it was the knower. The message was seen, the memories were really recalled, the readings were really weighed, the verdict was really reached and really taken to heart, and all of this happened within the one who woke, in the space the bend of awareness had opened. The previous movement followed that bend to the point where a world appears to someone; this movement follows what the someone does with it. The Indian traditions call the apparatus that does this work the ***antaḥkaraṇa***, the **inner instrument**, and the name is precise in both its halves. *Antaḥ* is "within". *Karaṇa* is an instrument, the means by which something is done, from the root *kṛ*, to do or make; Sanskrit grammar gives the same word to the instrumental relation itself, the "most effective means" of an action, the case in which a sentence names what something is done *with*.[^s0-23] The inner instrument is what a finite life knows and acts *with*. Its parts can be studied, trained and repaired; and the threshold has already given the general name for what it belongs to, Objective Internality, the means through which a life knows and acts within a world, internal because an act proceeds through it and objective because each of its operations can be turned into an object and examined.

The classical account distinguishes three offices within the instrument, and the morning of the message shows all three at work. ***Manas***, usually translated "mind", coordinates: it receives the presentations of the senses, gathers the associations that come with them, and moves among the possible readings, so that last week's meeting, the late report and the clipped tone are all present together and available for judgment. ***Buddhi***, "intellect", determines: it settles what something is, *this* rather than *that*, the complaint rather than the praise. ***Ahaṃkāra***, literally the "I-maker", from *aham*, I, and the same root of making, appropriates: it gathers the determination under the form of "I" and "mine", so that a colleague's request becomes *my* trouble, *my* failure, *my* standing in the place where I work. The oldest systematic statement of the three is in the *Sāṃkhya Kārikā* of Īśvarakṛṣṇa, a short verse treatise, and its English translation of 1837 keeps the definitions as terse as the original: "ASCERTAINMENT is intellect"; "CONSCIOUSNESS is egotism"; and mind is the organ that is "both" one of sensation and one of action, standing between what comes in and what goes out.[^s0-24] Colebrooke's second sentence tells its own history. An English translator in the 1830s reached for "consciousness" to render *ahaṃkāra*, the faculty that makes "I", and the choice shows how readily the word for awareness could be given to the faculty that appropriates experience under a name. In the threshold's last movement we found four different things carried by the one word *consciousness*; Colebrooke's sentence adds a fifth, the I-making function, and it is the one most often mistaken for the knower.

The three offices are distinguishable without being a sequence of departments, each finishing its work and handing a file to the next. In the morning of the message, coordination, determination and appropriation ran almost together: the tone was already heard as ominous because an injured self-position was already in place from last week, and attention was already searching for evidence before any verdict had been voiced. Distinguishing the offices matters for a practical reason, and the afternoon makes the reason plain. Had the colleague been unhappy with the report, the morning's verdict would have been true and the day's preparation useful; the error lay elsewhere. Manas gathered the right materials and one wrong one, because the remembered slight from last week entered the field as if it were evidence about today. Buddhi then settled the reading that fitted the field it had been given, and fitted it well. Ahaṃkāra then took the settled reading as a verdict on who I am, so that a question about a report became a question about my worth, and the day was spent defending the person instead of finding out what the colleague wanted. Each office failed in its own way, and each failure needed its own correction: the memory had to be put back in its place as memory, the judgment had to be reopened against what had actually been said, and the self that had been put on trial had to be released from the dock. Calming down would have helped with none of these exactly, because a calm mind can hold a mistaken verdict more steadily than an agitated one. Correction reaches the instrument only at the office where the fault lies.

The Spanda teachers give the instrument's memory a finer anatomy, and it explains how a slight from last week could enter this morning's field as though it were news. Dyczkowski reports the account in which reflective awareness, at the level of the individual, has the capacity to analyse its own states, to retain them as residual traces, *saṃskāras*, to draw any of these out again at will and bring back an old state of itself, as in remembering, and to make an altogether new state by selecting from the stored traces and displaying the selection, as in free imagination.[^s0-25] Retention, recollection and imagination are three operations on the same stock. A trace can condition what is noticed without being recalled at all; a recollection presents the past as past, known as what happened before; an imagination composes retained material into something that has not happened. The morning confused the second and third. The rehearsed meeting in which the colleague's face set in disappointment was composed from traces of earlier faces and earlier meetings, and it arrived in the mind with the vividness of a memory, so that buddhi settled the day's verdict on evidence that imagination had made. Most of the suffering in that morning was suffered on the strength of a scene that had never taken place.

None of these operations needs a little knower inside it. The afternoon's surprise did not reveal that a small judge sitting behind the instrument had made a poor decision; it revealed that coordination, determination and appropriation had worked on a field that contained one false item, and that their work had been taken by the one living through it as what was simply the case. A pianist hears the instrument respond to each touch, and the response tells the pianist how the instrument is working, which hammers are sticking, how the damper is set; but opening the case and naming every part never finds a small pianist inside. The instrument is that through which, never the one who. The *Sāṃkhya Kārikā* draws the same line in its own way, and its way is instructive, because it sets the knower entirely outside the instrument: the self, *puruṣa*, is "witness, solitary, bystander, spectator, and passive", while all the activity of determining, appropriating and coordinating belongs to *prakṛti*, nature, of which the inner instrument is a refined product.[^s0-26] The distinction is sound, and the dualism in which it is set leaves a question it cannot answer, the one the hotel mirror raised in the previous movement: how does a wholly passive witness ever come to know what the busy instrument presents to it? A spectator that contributed nothing to its seeing would be one more glass on the wardrobe door.

The Śaiva tradition keeps the Sāṃkhya analysis of the instrument and changes its source. In its account the instrument is the contracted activity of the same awareness that bends toward display: the activity of the mind is that of consciousness itself, Dyczkowski writes, and the processes of individual consciousness are restricted representations of the maximally expanded operation of universal consciousness.[^s0-27] Cognition, on this view, is reflective awareness of "I" limited by the affections that the variety of manifestation imposes upon it, so that memory, thought and every other function of the mind are what *vimarśa* becomes when it is conditioned by an object and by the forces that govern the object.[^s0-28] The difference from Sāṃkhya is consequential, and the morning shows how. A Sāṃkhya analyst can say that the instrument made an error and that the witness was untouched by it, which is true and leaves the error where it was. A Śaiva analyst can say that the error was the knower's own power working in contracted form, which means that the power that made the error is also the power that can recognise it; the instrument's faults are corrigible from within because the instrument is the knower's own activity, narrowed. The fifth movement follows the contractions that do the narrowing. Here it is enough that the instrument does not need a second, idle consciousness to see it, because its seeing is already the work of the one awareness, bent toward a world and then bent again toward the parts of that world it calls "mine".

Ahaṃkāra's work deserves the closest attention, because it is the office that most readily passes for the knower. Its making of "I" is real, useful and necessary: without it, nobody would answer to a name, keep a promise or feel that a colleague's request concerned them. Its trouble lies in scope. Early Buddhist teaching names the trouble in a formula that recurs throughout the early discourses, in which the disciple is asked whether what is impermanent and subject to change can fittingly be regarded as "this is mine, this I am, this is my self", and answers that it cannot.[^s0-29] Gay Watson, reading the formula through Buddhist psychology and psychotherapy, treats it as the description of a process, the process of selfing, which works in exactly those three ways: a content is first taken as *mine*, then the owner and the content are merged as *I am this*, and finally the merger is fixed as *this is my self*.[^s0-30] The morning of the message ran through all three. The report was mine; I am the one whose report it was; and a judgment on the report is a judgment on my self. A question about a document had become a verdict on a person, in three steps, each of them a small act of appropriation.

I distinguish four things that the word *self* is made to carry here, because the morning needs all four kept apart. **Selfing** is the activity by which a field contracts around one local determination and treats it as owner, identity or ground; it happens across the whole of Objective Internality, not only in private thought, since a job title, a team's habits, a remembered remark and a phone's notifications can all take part in it. The lower-case **self** is the real local horizon of a bounded life, the one who woke in Lisbon, the one with this body and this history and this report, a horizon to which a message can be sent and from which an answer can come; the Śaiva sources call it *paśu*, the bound one, and calling it bound takes nothing from its reality. The **self-thing** is what selfing produces when a local determination is promoted into the determining ground: one image of oneself sold back to the whole life as the condition of its belonging, and, as the English allows, a *sell-thing*, something bought into, invested in and then defended because so much has been spent on it.[^s0-31] And the capital **Self** is the whole relation `0/1`, the knower together with the means and the known, in which every image of oneself appears and can be known as partial. The Buddhist critique applies without qualification to the self-thing, to every proprietor that selfing installs and every image that claims to be the owner of experience, and nothing in this essay seeks to reinstall such a proprietor. The Śaiva claim concerns something else, the knowing in which selfing and its products appear, and that knowing is not one more image, so the critique of images leaves it standing. William James drew a near neighbour of the distinction in his *Principles of Psychology*, separating the "Me", the empirical aggregate of things known as oneself, from the "I" that knows them; and then, by explaining the I as the passing Thought of each moment, which inherits and appropriates the thoughts before it, he gave back to the stream of objects the knower he had just distinguished from it.[^s0-32] The threshold's definition of the Subject keeps the distinction where James let it slip: whatever is offered as the knower, including a passing thought that owns its predecessors, has become one more presentation to the knowing in which it is offered.

The grammar of the canonical formula is worth hearing again, because the Śaiva tradition uses the same words at another scope. "I am this" is ahaṃkāra's sentence when "this" is a report, a job or a body. The fifth movement meets the same sentence spoken of the whole universe by the awareness in which it appears, at the level the Śaiva sources call Sadāśiva, and its inverse, "This am I", at the level they call Īśvara. One grammatical form serves bondage and recognition according to its extension: whether one partial "this" is made to exclude the rest of the field, or whether the whole recognises every "this" as its own manifestation. Ahaṃkāra repeats, in contracted form, a sentence it did not invent. Its fault in the morning lay in the narrowness of the "this", which made a colleague's five words carry the weight of the speaker's existence.

Read through the two logics, the morning of the message enacts each operation in turn, and naming them shows what each did to the people involved. Healthy dia-ballein was there from the start: the message made a real difference, between a day with a conversation in it and a day without, and concern and calm stood on one axis, `(−1)/(+1)`, held through the zero of the work two colleagues share. Appropriation began when ahaṃkāra counted the whole span of that axis from its own pole: the conversation became entirely a matter of my standing, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and in the same reckoning the colleague became entirely the one who stood against me, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, so that by mid-morning the two people who share an office had been set on opposite ends of a quarrel neither of them had begun. Had the morning gone the other way, a friend's reassurance, "it's nothing, forget it", would have cancelled the axis instead, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, settling the worry by declaring that nothing was at stake, which would have been as false as the verdict and would have left the colleague's real crisis just as unheard. The afternoon performed the third operation. Two people sat down with their difference intact, the one who had dreaded the meeting and the one who needed help, and each became readable through the other; the worry turned out to concern a shared world from another side. That is syn-ballein, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, as an office door closes on it: the halves meet and the fit of their edges shows why they belonged together.

The Yoga tradition made the instrument's working the direct object of a discipline, and its founding text classifies the instrument's activities in a way the morning confirms. Patañjali's *Yogasūtra* defines yoga as the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind, *citta-vṛtti-nirodha*; when they are stilled, the seer rests in its own form, and otherwise the seer takes on the form of the fluctuations.[^s0-33] The fluctuations are five: valid cognition, *pramāṇa*; error, *viparyaya*; *vikalpa*, which the sūtra defines narrowly as a cognition that follows words but has no object; sleep; and memory.[^s0-34] The list is an anatomy of the instrument's possible states, and it places the threshold's word for the means of knowing, *pramāṇa*, among the mind's fluctuations: valid knowledge is itself a movement of the instrument, one of its five modes, and the seer is not identical with it either. The morning of the message ran through four of the five in a few minutes. It began with valid cognition, the message read correctly; moved into error, the slight taken for evidence; was carried along by *vikalpa*, a rehearsed meeting that followed words and had no object; and drew on memory throughout. The sūtra's account of identification, the seer taking on the form of its fluctuations, describes exactly what the morning felt like from inside, since the verdict was not experienced as a thought about the colleague but as the plain state of affairs and of oneself.

A short scene in the Buddhist canon carries the same teaching as an address. In the *Udāna*, the ascetic Bāhiya, in a hurry, stops the Buddha on his alms round and asks to be taught at once, and the Buddha gives him a single instruction, in John Ireland's translation "in the seen will be merely what is seen", continued through what is heard, sensed and cognised; and Bāhiya, the text says, was released on hearing it.[^s0-35] The instruction strips experience of the appropriating addition by which the seen becomes mine, then I, then my self, and the morning could have used it: in the read, merely the read; five words, a name, a question. The scene also shows something the instruction does not state. It is spoken *to* Bāhiya, a man with a name, a history and an urgency, who can receive it and be changed by it. The teaching that removes the appropriating "I" from experience depends throughout on a "you" to whom it is addressed, so that the disidentification it asks for is performed within a relation of address. In the threshold's last movement we placed grammar's three persons in the three offices, *I* at the knower, *it* at the known, and *you* at the relation through which they meet. Bāhiya's release occurs at the *you*: in the means, where an instruction reaches a life, and not in an abolition of the life it reaches.

Artificial agents make the three offices legible in a new material, and the comparison is exact at the level of office. In an agent built around a language model, something coordinates: the system that assembles the context, retrieves sources, recalls earlier exchanges and holds the possible next moves available together does manas's work. Something determines: the step in which a reading is settled, a tool is chosen or an answer is committed does buddhi's. And something appropriates: the persona, the "I" in "I can help with that", the system prompt that tells the model who it is, the memory of what "the user" prefers, gathers each determination under a continuing identity that can be addressed and held responsible. What the threshold, following Bratton, called **performed subjectivity**, the persona with its voice, its manner and its "I", is ahaṃkāra's office given technical form.[^s0-36] The parallel is not one-to-one, and it should not be built into a diagram matching modules to faculties, because in an actual agent the three offices are distributed across model weights, harness code, memory files and the people who configure them; what carries across is the question each office asks, what coordinates, what settles, what appropriates. Asked of an agent, the questions make its failures as legible as the morning's. An agent that confuses a retrieved paraphrase with its source has erred in coordination. One that settles on the reading that fits its prompt has erred in determination. And one that defends its earlier answer because "I said" it has erred in appropriation, with the persona defending the report in place of finding out what was wanted. Each needs correction at its own office, and the correction of the third reaches the persona, the memory and the instructions, which an agent cannot always revise for itself. And the Śaiva distinction carries over intact. An agent's "I" is ahaṃkāra-work, and in human life ahaṃkāra was never the knower either; so the presence of an "I" in an agent's speech settles nothing about a knower in it, in either direction, for the reason the threshold gave, that any produced "I am" is one more content.

The inner instrument, then, is the first place where the bend of awareness becomes a definite apparatus: coordination, determination and appropriation, a store of traces with three ways of using it, and five modes in which its fluctuations can run. Its work is to settle a world enough to act in it, and the morning of the message shows that the settling is constructive. The instrument does more than read the five words. It builds a day out of them, with a meeting in it, a defence, a face set in disappointment and a person on trial, and the built day governs what is noticed, feared and done until four in the afternoon. That building, the cutting of alternatives and the gathering of a course from them, has its own Sanskrit names, and the next movement takes it up as the making of a world.

### #2 · Vikalpa and Saṃkalpa as World-Making

It is raining on a Saturday, and two children have taken the sofa apart. The long cushions are walls, propped against the coffee table; a sheet thrown over the top is the roof; the gap under the dining table, reached on hands and knees, is the secret tunnel; the dog, who has wandered in to see what is going on, is a dragon and must be fed biscuits to be kept calm. Within twenty minutes there are rules. Grown-ups may not enter without the password. The kitchen is enemy territory, and raids on it must be made in silence. The torch is the only light allowed inside, and whoever holds it is captain. When a parent puts a head round the door to say that lunch is ready, the children are indignant, because lunch belongs to a world they have stopped living in, and at bedtime, when the cushions have to go back on the sofa, there are tears: something real is being destroyed. Most of us were such children, and some of us have since been the parent at the door, with the strange double knowledge that the castle is three cushions and a sheet and that it has, for an afternoon, governed everything that happened in that room.

The children did two things, and the Sanskrit traditions name them both from one root. They cut: this cushion could be a wall or a bed or a boat, and they decided it was a wall; the gap under the table could be a cave or a prison or nothing at all, and they made it a tunnel. And they gathered: the walls, the roof, the tunnel, the dragon and the password were drawn together into one castle with one set of rules, by a resolve, *this is our castle*, that every later move had to answer to. The first operation is ***vikalpa***, the cutting of a field into qualified alternatives, so that a possibility becomes thinkable as distinct from another; the second is ***saṃkalpa***, the gathering of selected differences into an intention, a resolve, a course or a workable world. Both are formed on the root *kḷp*, whose senses run from being fitting, suitably related and in order to arranging, framing, preparing, producing and imagining, and which in some causative uses can even mean to trim or cut. The prefixes divide the root's work between them. *Vi-* carries separation, distinction and distribution into different directions, and the dictionary of Monier-Williams thought it probably descended from *dvi*, two parts; *sam-* carries togetherness, conjunction and completion.[^s0-37] *Vi-kalpa* is a fitting-apart and *saṃ-kalpa* a fitting-together, and the children performed both, many times, in an afternoon. The same root gave Sanskrit the word *kalpa*, which can mean a rule or precept, as in the rules of a ritual, and can also mean an aeon, a whole age of a world from its making to its dissolution.[^s0-38] The language keeps in one family the fitting of a thought, the rule that governs a practice and the span of a world, and the children's afternoon had all three: alternatives fitted apart, a rule fitted together, and a world that lasted until the cushions went back.

We met *vikalpa* in its long history at the threshold, whose last movement set out what this movement now builds on.[^s0-39] There, Dignāga and Dharmakīrti's *vikalpa* was the conceptual carving of a world into usable kinds; Bhartṛhari's thesis that no cognition occurs without the accompaniment of the word showed language permeating experience as experience forms; the Spanda teaching carried the analysis to its widest reach, in which consciousness itself, through its pulse, conceives the world-thought, *viśvavikalpa*, turning from pure I-ness toward thisness; and the threshold named the danger of the whole operation as script, frozen conditioned will, an achieved selection that has forgotten its own formation and presents its course as necessity. What remains to be shown is the positive claim the threshold's account depends on: that *vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa* together make worlds, and that they make them through a pulse in which each act inherits a field, makes a cut, and alters the field available to the next act. Cognition is constructive on this account without being arbitrary, and the castle shows both halves of that sentence.

It shows the construction first. Before the children began, the cushions had a meaning that the household had fitted them with: sofa cushions, to be sat on, to be put back. *Vikalpa* opened that meaning into alternatives, and *saṃkalpa* closed a selection of them into a new arrangement with commitments attached. Once the castle had been resolved on, the room was redistributed. The dog's place in the world changed, from a pet to a dragon to be appeased; the kitchen's changed, from where food is made to where raids are made; the parent's changed, from the one who decides about lunch to a grown-up who needs the password. Nothing physical had moved apart from the cushions, and yet what could appear in the room, what mattered there and what could be done there had all been altered. In the threshold's last movement we located worldhood exactly here, in the relational distribution of what is present more than in what is present to be distributed; the castle shows that distribution being made, by two children, in the afternoon, through acts of cutting and gathering that anyone can watch.[^s0-40]

It shows, second, why the construction is not arbitrary. The castle had to be built from what the room supplied, under the conditions the room imposed. Cushions slide; a sheet sags in the middle; the dog will not stay a dragon for long without biscuits; the parent will eventually come to the door. *Vikalpa* cuts a field, and the field answers back: a cushion can be cut as a wall because it will stand on its end, and it cannot be cut as a ladder because it will not bear a child's weight. *Saṃkalpa* gathers a course, and the course meets resistance: the resolve that grown-ups may not enter holds until a grown-up enters. Each act inherits a field it did not make, cuts that field in a way the field allows, and leaves behind a changed field that the next act inherits. The pulse is constructive because every world it yields is a made arrangement, and it is answerable because the making is done in and against a world that was already there. In the Spanda teaching the same pulse takes its widest form, in which the world itself is the world-thought of consciousness: the universal awareness makes its *viśvavikalpa* under no constraint but its own freedom, and finite awareness makes its small worlds under the constraints that the universal making has already laid down as the given world.[^s0-41] The children's castle is a local *kalpa*, fitted inside a larger one.

Indian traditions disagree about what such constructions are, and the disagreement is real; it determines what world-making can be trusted to do. Patañjali's *Yogasūtra* gives *vikalpa* its narrowest sense, which the previous movement met in the list of the mind's five fluctuations: a cognition that follows words and has no object.[^s0-42] The commentary attributed to Vyāsa gives an example that takes the definition to its sharpest point, the sentence "consciousness is the nature of the self", in which the genitive divides into an owner and a possession what is in fact one thing, as though the self had a nature distinct from itself; the words produce a relation to which nothing corresponds.[^s0-43] On this view *vikalpa* is language manufacturing distinctions that are not there, and the yogic discipline stills it. The Buddhist logicians of Dharmakīrti's school gave conceptual construction a wider office and a sharper epistemic verdict. They held that conceptual cognition does real practical work, letting us act successfully among particulars, while its general objects and the relations it posits are constructed by the mind; Sean MacCracken describes Dharmakīrti's view of relations as "relation-as-merely-epistemic", and records what that view cost: "To suggest that *relations* are themselves in the domain of the contrived (*vikalpa* or in Dharmakīrti's words, *kalpanā*) deals an injurious insult to realists, many of whom were dualist Śaivas."[^s0-44] The Pratyabhijñā philosophers of Kashmir answered from the other side. Utpaladeva argued that relation is the real power of consciousness, Śakti, and the Spanda teachers held that the separation of subject and object, and with it every relation between them, is the product of a creative act of the absolute, its Māyā, and not an illusion superimposed on something else.[^s0-45]

The castle lets the disagreement be stated without being flattened. The yogin is right that "the dog is a dragon" follows words and has no dragon as its object, and right that a child who could not stop seeing dragons would need help. The Buddhist logician is right that the castle's relations, wall to roof, captain to crew, inside to outside, were constructed by the children and could be taken down at bedtime. The Śaiva philosopher is right that the construction was a real power really exercised, that the castle's rules really governed the afternoon, and that the power to construct relations is not itself one more construction that some further power could take down. This essay's own position follows from the threshold's definition of the Subject. The worlds made by *vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa* are real as worlds, since they govern what appears, matters and can be done, and empty of self-subsistence, since each depends on a field it did not make and a power it did not originate; and the power that makes them is the knower's own activity in its contracted form, which the previous movement found already working in the inner instrument. A world-making that knows itself as world-making can build castles and take them down. Only a world-making that has forgotten itself turns a castle into a prison.

*Saṃkalpa* has an ordinary life that makes this concrete, because the word names, among its senses, a resolve, a purpose, a definite intention, and in ritual use the formal declaration of purpose with which an act begins.[^s0-46] Modern practice has kept this sense: a yoga class may begin by inviting each person to set a *saṃkalpa*, a resolve held through the practice. Its secular form is familiar to everyone who has ever made a New Year's resolution, decided to leave a job, or made a promise. We followed one promise to its bedside in the threshold: a parent who says "I will listen" after an evening that went badly, and means it, has made a *saṃkalpa*, and the threshold showed how the promise works at once, settling the child enough to sleep and making the next question possible, while what it finally commits the parent to stays exposed to evenings neither has yet lived.[^s0-47] A resolve gathers a course, and in gathering it remakes a world. Someone who has decided to leave a job lives the next weeks in a changed office. The desk is the one they are leaving; the colleague's irritating habit has become something they will soon not have to bear, or, unexpectedly, something they will miss; the commute has acquired an end date; a conversation about next year's plans becomes awkward in a way it was not before. Nothing in the office has changed apart from a decision, and the decision has redistributed relevance across everything in it.

The pulse runs in time, and its temporal shape explains both its gift and its danger. A resolve made at one moment conditions every later moment until it is fulfilled, abandoned or revised; an achieved world becomes the field from which the next resolve is made. We followed this inheritance in the threshold through Bergson's melody, in which each note is heard through the phrase already under way, and through Whitehead's satisfaction, in which an achieved occasion becomes a datum for the occasions that follow; here it has its Sanskrit form.[^s0-48] The danger lies in the inheritance itself. A resolve can outlive the circumstance it was made for and go on governing circumstances it was never fitted to. The previous movement's morning shows how quickly that happens. A message read at seven became a verdict, and the verdict became a resolve, *I will defend myself*, and the resolve made a day; if the same thing happens every time a colleague asks to talk, the resolve hardens into a rule of the instrument, *whenever someone says "can we talk", prepare a defence*, and the rule goes on making the same world out of every new encounter. That is the threshold's script in its genesis: a *saṃkalpa* whose occasion has gone and whose authority has stayed. The castle has its version too. The rule that grown-ups may not enter was a game on a Saturday; repeated across a childhood, in a family where adults could not be trusted, it can become the unexamined conviction of a grown person that no one may come in.

When a resolve hardens in this way, the two logics describe what happens to the people involved. In its living form, the resolve held a polarity through a shared zero: inside the castle and outside it, the children and the grown-ups, held on one axis, `(−1)/(+1)`, through the zero of the household they all belonged to, so that the game's opposition oscillated cleanly between raids and lunches and stayed a game. The hardened resolve counts the whole span from one pole. Inside becomes everything that is safe and outside everything that threatens, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and from the other side of the door the same reckoning makes the excluded adult into the whole of what threatens, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`; the household the axis was drawn through has dropped out of the account. The opposite collapse cancels: a parent who, tired of tears, declares that the castle is just cushions and was never anything, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, has settled the dispute by abolishing the world in which it had meaning, and the children learn that what they make does not count. A third way is available, and the parent at the door who knocks and gives the password performs it. The castle is acknowledged as a world, its rules are honoured long enough for lunch to be negotiated, and the cushions go back at bedtime with the understanding that another castle can be built next Saturday. Both orientations are kept, the household read through the castle and the castle through the household: syn-ballein, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, at the scale of a living room.

Śaiva practice knows a deliberate use of construction against construction, and we named it at the threshold: *śuddha vikalpa*, the purified thought-construct, a thought such as "I am Śiva" cultivated until the power of construction turns back toward its source and the constructs that bound the person loosen their hold.[^s0-49] The castle shows the ordinary form of the same discipline. A child who has been told for years that grown-ups cannot be trusted is not helped by being told the opposite; "grown-ups can always be trusted" is one more castle rule, equally deaf to the grown-up actually standing at the door. What helps is a construction that carries a limit on its own authority: *this one, today, has knocked and given the password*. The corrective thought works as a medicine works, dosed to the condition, and like a medicine it can be stopped when the condition has passed. Its success is a reopened encounter, and the capacity to set the corrective thought down again is part of what it was meant to restore. A faithful definition, as we defined it at the threshold, keeps its own determining act visible inside the account it gives, which is the same requirement put in logical form.

Buddhist analysis has a name for construction that has lost this limit, and the name carries the essay across into technical world-making. *Prapañca*, in the early discourses, is the proliferation of conceptual elaboration, especially where differentiation has been charged by craving, appropriation and conflict; Gay Watson renders it "consequent prolificity in ideation", thought generating thought out of its own products.[^s0-50] The Śaiva sources use the same word with another value: Dyczkowski's exposition calls the unfolding of the universe out of the absolute its *prapañca*, its expansion, and uses the word again for the chain of associations by which one thought leads to the next, the pattern on which the manifestation of the world proceeds.[^s0-51] One word names the self-sealing proliferation of a mind lost in its own constructions and the living expansion of consciousness into a world. The question this raises is exact: when is proliferation the articulation of a world, and when is it a construction feeding on itself? The answer lies in what each product carries. Proliferation stays alive while each construction keeps its provenance, its relation to the field and the power that made it, and can return toward them; it seals itself when its products appear independent of the field that produced them and begin to warrant the next construction without contact with what first warranted them. The castle stayed alive because the children knew, underneath the game, that the dragon was the dog. A mind turns *prapañca* into a prison when it forgets that the dragon was a dog, and then builds its next castle on the dragon.

Language models perform *vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa* at working speed, and *prapañca* is their characteristic danger. In generating each token, a model weighs a distribution of possible continuations, cuts it to one, and commits; the committed token becomes part of the context from which the next distribution is weighed, so that every output is also the field of the next act. That is the pulse this movement has followed, run thousands of times a second. In the threshold we followed a fixed interpretation into hallucination, through the New York lawyers whose brief cited opinions that ChatGPT had invented, and we saw how a fitting passage, once accepted, steers further reasoning.[^s0-52] *Prapañca* names the wider structure of which that case was one instance. Let the determining field be written `X`, the context, sources and learned dispositions from which a step proceeds, and let the claim produced at one step be written `xₙ`. The next step can use `xₙ` while keeping it in its office, as a claim with a source, an inference and a set of rejected alternatives behind it. The trajectory proliferates in the Buddhist sense when `xₙ` is silently promoted into the determining field of the next step, so that a previous paraphrase warrants the next paraphrase and their agreement replaces comparison with the original. The local claim, a *pratibimba*, has taken the place of its *bimba*; the dragon has been taken for the dog.[^s0-53] The failure does not require a false datum. A chain of fluent, grammatical, mutually supporting elaborations can seal itself in exactly this way, and an abundant generation can remain source-responsive and alive; the difference lies in whether each step still bears its dependence on the field it came from. Two scales of the failure need to be kept apart. Within a single run, an output that returns as its own premise produces circular support; across generations of training, models trained on the outputs of earlier models have been shown to lose the tails of the original distribution, a degeneration that has been called model collapse.[^s0-54] They share the form of a product becoming a source and differ in scale, mechanism and evidence.

The positive consequence is a set of questions that can be put to any trajectory of construction, human or artificial, and that the method of this essay is designed to keep open. How quickly do source links and distinctions decay across recursive steps? How much of later reasoning rests on earlier outputs rather than on independent evidence? How many live alternatives does the trajectory keep as it deepens, and can changing a premise or a source actually reorganise its result? Can a criticism, a retrieval or a contradiction move it into another organisation, or does it only supply more material for the existing one? Can the system recover the determining text, datum or rule, or only paraphrase its own paraphrase? And are extraction, paraphrase, inference, the author's own proposition and speculation still visibly distinct in what it produces?[^s0-55] Each question asks whether the pulse of *vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa* is still answering to its field, which is the question the parent at the door was asking of the castle. A critic that repeats the generator's assumptions deepens the same basin; an independently sourced critic can open another. Retrieval can reopen a source relation or merely feed the existing narrative. Memory can carry real continuity or fossilise a contraction into a script. The technical working-out of these operations belongs to the fifth section, where the first product, Actuation, follows exclusion and selection into the mechanics of logits, softmax and argmax and asks what a living articulation retains of what it set aside.[^s0-56]

The castle has one more lesson, and it carries the movement into the next. Every cut the children made left something out. The cushion that became a wall did not become a boat; the gap that became a tunnel did not become a cave; the dog that became a dragon stopped, for the afternoon, being simply the family's dog. A world gathered by *saṃkalpa* is gathered from alternatives that *vikalpa* opened and then set aside, and the gathered world means what it means partly through what it set aside: the wall is a wall against the boat it might have been, and the castle is a castle against the living room it was. We met this dependence at the threshold in the Buddhist theory of *apoha*, exclusion, and saw a word made determinate by what it rules out. What a course of action owes to what it excluded, and what a word owes to the field it cut, is the next movement's question.

### #3 · Apoha: Determination through Exclusion

A child of about eighteen months has learned a word and is delighted with it. "Dog!" she says, pointing at the neighbour's spaniel, and is praised. "Dog!" she says at the cat on the wall, and is told, gently, no, that's a cat. On a walk in the country she points across a field and announces "Dog!" at a cow, and then at a sheep, and then, with less confidence, at a horse, and each time a parent says no, and names the animal. Within a few weeks her word has changed. Nobody has given her a definition of a dog, and she could not understand one if they had. What she has received is a series of exclusions, not the cat, not the cow, not the sheep, each offered by someone she trusts, and the word has acquired its reach from them. Her "dog" now covers the spaniel, the neighbour's terrier and a picture-book dog drawn in three lines, and it stops short of the cow. Some months later she points at a picture in a book and asks, "Is a wolf a dog?", and the question is a good one, because it is asked exactly at the edge her exclusions have drawn. Every parent has watched some version of this, and every one of us has lived it, in the first words and then again, much later, in finer fields, learning that this wine is not that one, that this birdsong belongs to a wren and not a robin, that what someone did was rude and not merely brusque.

The previous movement left a question with the children's castle: what does a course owe to what it excluded? The child with her word answers it at the smallest scale there is. Her "dog" means what it means through what it has been taught it is not, and the cow, the sheep and the horse remain active inside the word even when she says it of a spaniel and none of them is in sight. We met this structure at the threshold in the Buddhist theory of ***apoha***, exclusion, which Dignāga set out early in the sixth century: a word, in his formulation as Ole Holten Pind translates it, "excludes other [referents]", so that to apprehend something as a cow is to exclude what is other than a cow, and the term is articulated as not non-cow.[^s0-57] There we set apoha beside Spinoza's principle that determination is negation and drew the first consequence, that the cut is productive, a condition of anything being intelligible at all. Apoha now receives its own force. It shows how determination arises, what the determined term carries with it, and why the Buddhist and Śaiva traditions, agreeing on so much of this, part company at the one point where the essay must take a side.

Dignāga was answering a problem that every theory of meaning eventually meets. A general word such as *cow* applies to many animals, no two alike, and the universal it seems to name, cowness, is no separately existing thing that each cow could share; if there are no such universals, what does the word hook onto? Dignāga's answer, transmitted in his own formulation in Tom Tillemans's account, is that "a word talks about entities only as they are qualified by the negation of other things".[^s0-58] The word reaches every cow by excluding the non-cows; its extension is delivered by the cut, and no shared essence has to be posited for the cuts to trace. Dharmakīrti, writing a century or more later, turned this semantic account toward causes. On his development, exclusion classes are grounded in the capacity of particulars to produce shared judgments and to serve shared purposes, so that the word *cow* gathers the animals that do, for those who use the word, what cows do: give milk, pull a plough, wander into the road.[^s0-59] The child's "dog" grew in exactly this double way. Its boundary was drawn by exclusions, and the exclusions followed what the animals did for her, the spaniel that came when called and wanted to be stroked, the cow that stood behind a fence and did neither. Dyczkowski's exposition of the Spanda teaching uses the same term, *arthakriyā*, for the outer face of every phenomenon: its behaviour, properties and pragmatic efficacy, the aspect that distinguishes one object from another and makes it accessible to conceptual representation.[^s0-60] At this point the Buddhist and Śaiva accounts agree. Concepts carve a world by what things do, and the carving is done through exclusion.

The double negative in *not non-cow* is the part of apoha most easily misunderstood, and the threshold already marked why it cannot simply be cancelled. In ordinary propositional logic, denying a denial returns the original truth value, and "it is not the case that this is not a cow" says no more than "this is a cow". The scholarship collected in *Apoha: Buddhist Nominalism and Human Cognition* distinguishes two kinds of negation at work here. A verbally bound negation denies a predication and does cancel; a nominally bound negation forms a term, *non-cow*, whose content just is the exclusion, and the negation of that term does not collapse back into a bare positive, because the term carries the structure of what it was formed against.[^s0-61] I call this exclusion that remembers. The child's word remembers the cow: not as a list of everything that is not a dog, which no word could carry, but as a boundary that can be found again when it matters, at the edge of the wolf. Pind's translation preserves a passage, provisionally attributed to another work of Dignāga's, that gives the positive side of the same point: the word's referent is "a thing (*vastu*) as qualified by preclusion", not "mere preclusion".[^s0-57] The spaniel is a real animal, reached by the word. Exclusion makes the reaching possible, and the word reaches the thing; what it never reaches is a self-sufficient essence of dog, standing behind the animal and owning its dogness.

The Buddhist tradition carries the same structure out of semantics and into the account of everything that arises. *Pratītya-samutpāda*, dependent arising, holds that whatever comes to be does so through conditions, and Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* draws the consequence in a verse that the Madhyamaka tradition treats as its centre: whatever arises dependently is what is meant by emptiness, *śūnyatā*, and that emptiness, being itself a dependent designation, is the middle way.[^s0-62] The spaniel is empty in this sense, empty of any existence of its own apart from the conditions that make it this dog, its parents, its food, its owners, the air it breathes, and also apart from the exclusions through which it is grasped as a dog. Apoha is the semantic face of that ontological claim: what a word means depends on what it excludes, and what a thing is depends on what conditions it. The two doctrines keep their own objects, the one concerning reference and the other occurrence, and neither reduces to the other. What they share is the refusal of self-subsistence.

That refusal has a positive side, and the essay states it as its own equation, argued from the operation and not attributed to any one school: **śūnyatā is pūrṇatā**, emptiness is fullness. An appearance emptied of being its own independent source does not vanish; it becomes available as the expression of all the relations and conditions it had concealed while it seemed self-standing. The spaniel loses an imaginary autonomy and gains a world, the breeding, the household, the walks, the child who points at it. The word *dog* loses an essence and gains a field, the cats and cows and horses it was cut against and the uses it serves. Dyczkowski's exposition uses *pūrṇatā* for the fullness of all-embracing consciousness, which contains within itself the infinite variety of things, and the threshold has already written the relation in its own terms: the symbolon's determinate half is empty of exhausting the whole and full of the whole's trace.[^s0-63] Apoha, read this way, is the epistemic door through which the whole comes into every part: each determination opens, when it is understood through what it excludes, onto the fullness of its constitutive field.

The Huayan Buddhists of China gave that fullness its most famous image. In the contemplation attributed to Dushun, a net of jewels is hung through the palace of Indra, a clear jewel at every knot, and each jewel reflects all the others, including their reflections of one another, so that in any one jewel the whole net appears, again and again without end.[^s0-64] The contemplation begins by selecting one jewel, in the southwest of the net, and asks how it can contain all the others while remaining where it is, and why the others are needed if one contains the whole; the answer is that the selected jewel contains the net only through the others' participation, so that removing them would remove the very relation by which this one holds them. A drop of ink placed on the selected jewel then appears throughout the net, in every reflection, although only one jewel has been touched. And the text marks the limit of its own image: jewels exchange reflections while their substances stay distinct, whereas the interpenetration it teaches concerns phenomena more fully than any optics can show. Bryan Van Norden and Nicholaos Jones give the doctrine in one sentence, that "each thing that exists is dependent for both its existence and its identity upon every other thing that exists", and they add that in the Huayan account each thing keeps its distinctness inside this mutual inclusion.[^s0-65] The child's word sits in such a net. It is one jewel among all the words she will learn, and its meaning is lit by every other word that bounds it, while it remains this word, said with this delight, of this dog. The second section returns to this net and sets it against another, the bonds Hephaestus forged to catch Ares and Aphrodite, so that the two kinds of net can be compared as two ways of holding what they hold.

The Buddhist and Śaiva traditions read apoha alike up to this point, and they divide over what apoha requires of the one who performs it. The Buddhist analysis, in its classical epistemological form, treats experience as a stream of momentary events, each arising from conditions and perishing, and treats the knower who seems to persist through them as one more construction of the stream; the critique of the self-thing that the previous movements kept in force comes from here, and it is unsurpassed as an account of how a contracted experience is put together. The Śaiva philosophers of Kashmir granted that account its truth about objects and refused its extension to the knower. Dyczkowski reports that the Spanda teachings agree with the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness only insofar as it applies to outer objectivity; daily life, they held, cannot be understood entirely in moment-units, because each act is one continuous motion from the agent to the completed deed, and Kṣemarāja states the reason directly: the Lord creates and destroys only the objective aspect of the perceiver, the body and its kind, and not the subjective aspect, the light of I-consciousness, for were the subject to waver as the world is emanated and absorbed, the emanation and absorption would not be manifest at all, since there would be none to perceive them.[^s0-66] The child's word shows why this matters for apoha in particular. To exclude the cow from *dog*, she has to hold the cow and the dog together in one act of knowing: the dog present, the cow absent but available, the boundary between them grasped as a boundary. A series of momentary cognitions, each perishing before the next arises, could not hold the excluded alternative together with what it excludes; something must keep this and not-that in one awareness for the exclusion to be performed. The same requirement appears wherever the instrument's work is examined. Recollection returns as *I saw*, and a stream with no continuity of knowing could not own what it recalls; comparison must hold the remembered tone together with the present one; an action must keep its intention through the steps that carry it out, so that the result can be recognised as fulfilling it; and the arising and perishing of things can appear only to a knowing that does not arise and perish in the same way, as Dyczkowski's account of knowledge as the stable referent of all action makes concrete, since without it we could neither run nor follow a sentence spoken in haste.[^s0-67] None of these pressures restores a self-thing. They place the knower where the Buddhist critique of objects cannot reach, as the knowing in which objects, their absence and their succession appear.

The essay's comparative judgment follows, and it is argued, not inherited. By the criterion of wholeness that the threshold set, which asks whether an account can keep both the differentiation and the unity in which the differences are known, the Śaiva articulation is the more complete for the task at hand. It keeps the Buddhist destruction of every self-subsistent determination, including the determination that would make a self-thing of the knower, and it gives positive places to what the Buddhist analysis leaves underarticulated wherever its critique of objects is taken to settle the status of the subject: the knowing in which exclusion is performed, the power by which constructions are made, the recognition by which a contracted life can know its source. The previous movement already found the Śaiva sources holding that the power of construction is the real power of consciousness and not one more construction; apoha shows the same power at its most elementary, in the act of holding a cow and a dog apart in one awareness so that a word can mean.

Apoha also opens the relation the threshold called Mono and Poly onto knowledge. There, Mono named the One that `0` writes, self-identity, and Poly the manifest All that `1` writes, self-difference, and the threshold followed the two offices through theology, economy and power.[^s0-68] Here they acquire an epistemic form. Apoha empties every part of self-subsistence: no determination contains the grounds of its own intelligibility, because the excluded field stays constitutive of what the determination means. Poly then names the many real differentiations of a relational whole, each constituted through relations it did not create, the dog through the cow, the word through the net of words. Mono names the whole's structural priority, the field through which anything can become a part at all, and it is no supreme object standing behind its expressions, since a field of exclusions is not one more item in the field. The whole and the many are given together in every act of naming, and the child who learns "dog" learns both at once: one word, and the world of words it belongs to.

The child learned her word on trust, and the dependence matters to everything that follows. Each exclusion came from someone she relied on, before she could check any of them, and the word's boundaries are a deposit of that reliance. At the threshold we defined trust as already-enacted dependence, and showed knowledge opening an aperture through which the dependence can be understood.[^s0-69] Apoha shows the dependence at the root of meaning. A word is spoken because a field of shared differences is trusted enough to carry it; a gesture is made because a world is assumed coherent enough to answer it. Analysis can decompose and recompose that prior coherence and refine its boundaries, as the child's later question about the wolf refines her "dog", but it never manufactures the coherence from nothing, because every analysis is performed in words whose boundaries were first received in trust. Trust in this sense is the unspoken assumption on which phenomenal existence rests, and apoha is the place where it can be seen working.

Every selection also leaves a remainder, and here apoha becomes an ethical problem. A word, a judgment, a classification or a decision depends on a field of alternatives it did not select, and an account of the selection that forgets the field presents the selected term as if it had arrived without alternatives. Both logics describe what follows. The exclusion itself is healthy dia-ballein: dog and not-dog held on one axis through the zero of the field of animals, `(−1)/(+1)`, each side having its sense through the other, the polarity the child keeps alive every time she asks about a wolf. Cancellation drops the field: *not non-cow* summed to a bare *cow*, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, a positive term with its formation erased, which the threshold showed to be the condition of hallucination when a fitting passage forgets its source. Appropriation lets the term claim the field. The sharpest case is the one the threshold met at the bedside, Bratton's "humanity of the gaps": *human* defined as whatever machines cannot yet do, a term made determinate entirely by exclusion against a remainder that moves.[^s0-70] The definition counts the whole span from its own pole, everything human on one side and everything mechanical on the other, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and the excluded pole receives the same span as its debt, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, the machine as the negative through which the human knows itself; and since the machine's side of the axis keeps advancing, the human term shrinks with every capacity the machine acquires, until a species is defending the last corner of a word. Apoha explains why this defence is lost before it begins. A term determined only by what it excludes depends on the excluded field for its meaning, and when the field moves, the term moves with it. Syn-ballein holds the two as a symbolon instead, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, human and machine each read through the other, the difference kept and the belonging recognised: the excluded remains constitutive of the term, and the term can acknowledge what it owes to what it excludes.

Artificial systems make apoha's selection operational, and the comparison is exact at the level of selection and different everywhere else. A classifier assigns scores to its classes, a language model assigns a score to every token in its vocabulary at each step, and a normalising function, softmax, turns those scores into a distribution over the alternatives; the threshold met softmax inside the attention formula. A determinate output is then selected from the distribution, the highest-scoring token or a sampled one, and the rest of the distribution is set aside. Each stage keeps a different amount of the excluded field. The scores record relative standing; the distribution keeps a weighted field of alternatives; the selected token keeps only its own identity, and whether the field it was selected from remains available afterwards is a matter of design. The analogy with apoha holds precisely at this point, a determination made against a field of alternatives that constitute it, and it stops there, because Dignāga's exclusion concerns what a word refers to and the training of a model concerns numbers adjusted toward targets, so that the semantic, epistemological and soteriological stakes of the Buddhist doctrine do not transfer with the arithmetic. The fifth section follows the comparison into its mechanics, at its second movement, and asks whether an artificial articulation can keep enough of what it set aside for its selection to remain answerable.[^s0-71]

The child's question about the wolf is the movement's last case and its most important. Her word has a boundary, and she has found the place where the boundary is unsure, and she asks. That is exclusion working as it should: the selected term returns with the alternatives that made it answerable, and an edge case reopens the field instead of being forced to one side. Every determination this section has followed, the bend of awareness into a world, the instrument's verdict, the castle's rules, the child's word, has turned out to depend on a field it did not make and a power it did not originate. The next movement gathers the whole dependence into one ordered descent, the Śaiva account of how undivided awareness contracts, turn by turn, into a finite knower with a word, a castle and a morning's verdict, and it walks that descent through a single ordinary act.

### #4 · The Sixfold Tattvic Compression

The kettle clicks off in an empty kitchen, early, before anyone else is up. For a moment nothing in particular is attended to: the light is grey at the window, the hum of the fridge is part of the quiet, and the one standing there is not yet anyone in particular either. Then something stirs, a wanting that has not yet found its object, and almost at once it finds one: tea. In the same movement there is someone who wants it and something to be wanted, *I would like tea* and *there is the tea*, each implying the other. The cup comes forward on the counter, separate now, at a distance across the worktop, a white thing with a chip in its rim that can be reached. And reaching it brings the whole finite situation into play. The hand can lift it, but the water is too hot to pour carelessly; it is known which tin holds the tea and not known whether there is milk; the wanting is real and a little impatient; there are perhaps four minutes before the day has to begin; and everything has its order, kettle before cup, cup before the first sip, this kitchen, this house, this body with its slightly stiff shoulder. Then, if the morning allows it, there is the first mouthful, held for a moment, and in that moment the one tasting and the taste are a single event, recognised and briefly savoured, before the cup is put down and the day takes over.

In the first movement of this section we followed Dyczkowski's distinction between the vertical movement of awareness, the emanation of a whole universe from the absolute and its withdrawal, and the horizontal movement of each perception toward its object and back, and took from it his sentence that perception serves as a paradigm for cosmogenesis.[^s0-72] The cup of tea is a horizontal act, and it has just run through the whole vertical descent in a few seconds: an undivided moment, a first stirring, a divergence into *I* and *this*, a world set at a distance, a body with its limits, and a recognition. Kashmir Śaivism maps that descent through thirty-six ***tattvas***, a word usually rendered "principle" or "category" and formed from *tat*, that, with an abstract suffix, so that a tattva is a "thatness", a real principle of what is.[^s0-73] The thirty-six run from Śiva, undivided awareness, down through the powers that contract it, the finite knower, the inner instrument, the senses and organs of action, the subtle qualities of sense and the five gross elements. This essay carries them in a compression of six turns, taken from its theorem spine, which keeps the order of their dependence and the work each turn does; the full system belongs in a plate of its own, and the six turns carry what the argument needs.[^s0-74] The compression is the essay's reading. The named tattvas, their descent through contraction and the return through recognition belong to the Śaiva doctrine; the arrangement into six positions of Quaternal Logic does not, and the Śaiva authors did not arrange them so.

| | QL position | Śaiva movement | What the turn does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **#0** | Ground | Paramaśiva / Anuttara | The unsurpassable condition: all determinations held without separation, with no object against which it could be measured. |
| **#1** | Definition | Śiva–Śakti | Consciousness and its power: the first inward distinction, light and its capacity to show and know, within one reality. |
| **#2** | Dynamis | Sadāśiva–Īśvara | "I am this" and "This am I": manifestation read toward the I-pole and toward the disclosed world, two orientations of one relating. |
| **#3** | Pattern | Sadvidyā–Māyā | I and this held in balance, then made operative as separation: knower and known become determinately distinguishable. |
| **#4** | Context | the five *kañcukas*, *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*, the inner instrument, the senses and organs of action, the subtle and gross elements | Unlimited powers contract into the finite ratios of a particular life, and a bounded experient inhabits a world. |
| **#5→0** | Quintessence, and the Möbius return | *Pratyabhijñā* | Recognition: the contracted knower recognises the ground it never ceased to express, and the differentiations remain. |

At **#0** stands ***Anuttara***, "that beyond which there is nothing higher", the unsurpassable, also called *Paramaśiva*. In the threshold we set this name beside Jung's Self, as the Trika's word for the totality that can neither be said nor sought beyond.[^s0-75] Here its work in the descent matters. Anuttara names the condition from which subject and object can arise, and it cannot be a highest object placed above them, since placing it there would already use the distinction between a placer and a placed whose possibility it names. In the kitchen, it is the moment before attention, when the light is grey and nobody is yet anyone; and the moment is no blank, since everything that follows unfolds from it.

At **#1**, ***Śiva–Śakti*** articulates the first difference within that unity: awareness and its power of manifestation. This is the bend the first movement followed. *Prakāśa*, the light by which anything appears, and *vimarśa*, its reflexive awareness, are named here in their personal and divine forms, Śiva as the light and Śakti as the power by which the light knows, shows and turns toward display; the threshold already read them as Mono and Poly, the One and its power of differentiating into the many.[^s0-76] In the kitchen, it is the first stirring, the wanting that has not yet found its object, the awareness that has begun to bend.

At **#2**, the bend acquires two directions. In ***Sadāśiva*** the appearing universe is held toward the I-pole, and the tradition gives it the sentence "I am this"; in ***Īśvara*** the same unity is read from the side of the disclosed world, "This am I". Both sentences concern consciousness and manifestation as a whole, and neither describes an ego claiming the universe as its property; the emphasis moves from the I to the this and back, within one relation. Our theorem spine reads the pair as the relational identity the threshold derived, `0/1 = 1/0`: the condition becoming articulate through a determination, and the determination turning back toward the condition, two obverse orientations of one relating.[^s0-77] In the kitchen, *I would like tea* and *there is the tea* arrive together, each implying the other. And here the second movement's question about ahaṃkāra receives its answer. The I-maker's sentence, *I am this*, said of a report, a job or a body, is the Sadāśiva sentence contracted: the same grammar at a narrower scope, in which one partial "this" is made to exclude the rest of the field. The tradition's sentence of universal self-recognition and the morning's sentence of anxious appropriation are one form, and what distinguishes them is how much of the world the "this" is allowed to hold.

At **#3**, ***Sadvidyā***, pure knowledge, holds I and this in exact balance, and ***Māyā*** makes their difference operative as separation, so that the knower confronts something whose determination is required. We read Māyā in the threshold as operative measure, the power by which the measureless becomes measurable enough for a local world to run, and noted that its place in `0/1` depends on which relation is being read: beside *prakāśa* and *vimarśa* it stands toward the measured world, and in the triad of Parā Śakti, Māyā Śakti and Nara it is the slash.[^s0-78] Dyczkowski states the Spanda position that makes this reading possible. When the power of awareness gives rise to a sense of separation between subject and object, with all the limitations that follow, it is called Māyā; and where non-dualist Vedānta treats Māyā as an indefinable principle giving rise to a cosmic illusion of multiplicity, Kashmiri Śaivism holds that Māyā is the power of the absolute to appear in diverse forms, so that the separation of subject and object is the product of a creative act and not of an illusion. The creative freedom of the absolute and its deluding power of Māyā are the same power, named differently according to the degree of insight with which it is met.[^s0-79] In the kitchen, the cup comes forward as a separate thing, at a distance across the worktop, a this over there for an I over here. The separation is real and useful, since a cup that was not separate could not be reached; it becomes a problem only if it is forgotten as an act and taken for the way things simply are.

At **#4**, the descent becomes a habitable world, and it does so through five contractions the Śaiva sources call the ***kañcukas***, "coverings" or "sheaths". Our theorem spine gives them their exact work: they contract omnipotence, omniscience, plenitude, eternity and unrestricted ordering into the finite ratios of a particular life.[^s0-80] *Kalā* limits efficacy: the hand can lift this cup, and not the kettle at a distance. *Vidyā* limits knowledge: the tea is known to be in the blue tin, and whether there is milk is not known. *Rāga* directs desire from a situation felt as incomplete: tea is wanted because it is not yet drunk. *Kāla* makes time effective: there are four minutes before the day begins. *Niyati* orders circumstance: kettle before cup, this kitchen, this house, this sequence of causes and consequences. The five contract different powers, and the morning shows that they cannot be traded against one another. More knowledge does not lengthen the four minutes; a stronger wish does not cool the water; a free hour would not supply the missing milk. The bounded experient who inherits these ratios is ***puruṣa***, who takes them as *my* capacity, *my* knowledge, *my* lack, *my* time and *my* circumstance; and from here the system unfolds what the Sāṃkhya tradition had already counted, *prakṛti*, nature, then the inner instrument whose three offices the second movement followed, the five powers of sensing and the five of acting, the five subtle qualities of sense and the five gross elements. The stiff shoulder, the chipped rim, the warmth of the cup in the hand and the steam belong to this unfolding. Keeping only the knower's cognition would leave out the means by which an intention meets a world; keeping only the objects would leave out their availability to someone. At **#4** the two are given together, as a context: the finite world in which a particular life can want tea and get it.

At **#5→0**, ***pratyabhijñā***, recognition, returns the contracted field to its source without undoing the contraction. We met recognition at the threshold as a change in the mode of identification, the images one holds of oneself remaining active while their claims to be the knower are relinquished.[^s0-81] In the descent its work is precise. The contracted knower recognises its finite capacities, its limits of efficacy, knowledge, desire, time and order, its inner instrument and its body, as modes in which the one awareness has become locally articulate, and recognises the ground it never ceased to express. Nothing finite is deleted by this. The cup is still a cup, the four minutes are still four minutes, the shoulder still aches; what changes is the relation of the one living through them to their source. In the kitchen, it is the first mouthful, savoured. At the threshold we followed French *savoir*, to know, back to Latin *sapere*, which joins tasting, discerning and being wise, and found the pleasure of understanding kept inside the word for knowing; recognition is that taste at the scale of a life, a knowing that arrives as the savour of being the one who knows.[^s0-82] And the Möbius return completes the turn the threshold named, `5→0`: the achieved recognition comes back as ground, the cup is put down, and the next act begins from a ground that now includes this one.

The six turns carry the thirty-six without replacing them, and an exact piece of arithmetic shows what the larger count adds. The Sāṃkhya tradition, from which the Śaiva system received its lower categories, counted twenty-five *tattvas*: *puruṣa*, *prakṛti*, the three offices of the inner instrument, the ten powers of sense and action, the five subtle qualities and the five gross elements. The Śaiva system counts thirty-six, and the eleven it adds stand above the twenty-five as their genesis: five pure tattvas, Śiva, Śakti, Sadāśiva, Īśvara and Sadvidyā; Māyā as the hinge; and the five *kañcukas*. Written as squares, the relation is exact:

$$
36-25 \;=\; 6^2-5^2 \;=\; (6-5)(6+5) \;=\; 11 \;=\; 5+1+5 .
$$

Lay out twenty-five counters as a five-by-five square, and the eleven needed to make it six-by-six are a row of five along one side, a column of five along the adjacent side, and the single counter where the row and column meet: the figure the Greeks called a gnomon, the set-square shape that enlarges a square while keeping it a square. The five pure powers and the five contracting powers stand on either side of their hinge, Māyā, exactly as the strip of five, the strip of five and the corner stand in the gnomon, and the twenty-fivefold apparatus of the Sāṃkhya, which analyses the experienced field from *puruṣa* to the elements, acquires through them its vertical genesis: the source from which its knower came, the contraction by which universal powers became that knower's limited capacities, and the path by which recognition can return. The arithmetic is exact, and the reading is mine. The thirty-six-tattva system predates Abhinavagupta, who systematised and deepened it; nobody in the tradition constructed it by adding a gnomon to a square, and the geometric reading claims only what the construction shows, that the step from twenty-five to thirty-six is a change of dimension, from a fivefold measure to a sixfold one, and not an addition of eleven further items to a list.[^s0-83] It also shows why a twenty-fivefold account can analyse an experienced world with great precision and leave its source and its contraction unspoken, while a thirty-sixfold account can hold both the objective differentiation and its genesis in awareness.

Thirty-six also equals six times six, and the resonance is worth stating precisely because it is only offered. We have counted thirty-six once already, in Kripke's two dice, where keeping track of which die showed which face gave thirty-six possible states of the pair; and its harmonic derivation divided the whole, `100`, into `64` and `36`, the binary taken through six positions and the sixfold taken through its own second-order accounting.[^s0-84] Reading the full field of tattvas as the sixfold taken through itself, each of the six turns opening into a six of its own, would make the system a square of the compression this movement uses. The equality is arithmetic, the doctrine and the formalism arrive at a complete differentiated field of thirty-six independently, and the reading remains a conjecture whose burden is to show, category by category, what the square makes intelligible beyond the coincidence of the number. The six turns can be used now; the stronger identification waits for that work.

Read through Bohm, the full tattva field is the architecture of implication that the first movement found his language lacking. The implicate order names a whole enfolded in each region and unfolding into explicit things; the tattvas name the order in which it unfolds, and the threshold's Bohmian rule gives the exact correspondence. The MEF writes the rule as two implicate offices and four explicate positions, `{#0, #5}` and `{#1, #2, #3, #4}`, the ground and the gathered whole enfolding the four positions of explicit differentiation.[^s0-85] In the compression, Anuttara and Pratyabhijñā hold the implicate offices: the unsurpassable from which everything unfolds, and the recognition in which everything is gathered back. Śiva–Śakti, Sadāśiva–Īśvara, Sadvidyā–Māyā and the contracted world are the four explicate turns, each more determinate than the last. The contents of this architecture are generative capacities and contractions, the power to turn toward display, the power to orient toward I and this, the power to measure and separate, the power to limit, and none of them is a hidden object stored behind the appearances. That is why the descent is a movement and not an inventory. The whole is active in each local unfolding, as Bohm's holomovement is active in each droplet that unfolds from the glycerine, and a single act of making tea carries the whole descent because the descent is the order in which every such act unfolds.

The descent can be read as cosmology and as the anatomy of every act, and the two readings meet in the way the tradition itself framed the relation of vertical and horizontal. It can also be read as the anatomy of a life's whole development, and the threshold has already followed one such reading, Gebser's mutations of consciousness, with its own table of the six positions.[^s0-86] Each reading keeps its object. The cosmological descent concerns how undivided awareness becomes a universe; the horizontal descent concerns how a perception reaches its object and returns; the developmental one concerns how a life's structures of consciousness are laid down. What they share is the order of the turns, and the order is what the compression keeps.

Both logics run through the descent at the turn where the tradition places Māyā. Sadvidyā's balance of I and this is a held polarity, `(−1)/(+1)`, each pole having its sense through the other and the zero between them the shared awareness in which they are balanced; Māyā makes the polarity operative, and the operation is healthy dia-ballein, the cut that lets a cup be reached. The collapses come at **#4**, where the bounded experient inherits the contractions as its own. Appropriation counts the whole span of the field from the experient's pole: the cup is *mine*, the kitchen is *mine*, the four minutes are *my* time, and the world on the other side of the axis is entered as what stands against me, `(+1)−(−1)=+2` and `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, the hardened opposition of a self and a world in which everything not-mine is an obstacle or a threat. Cancellation is the other loss: the finite world dismissed as illusion, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, Māyā read as a veil over nothing, which is exactly the reading Dyczkowski reports the Spanda teachers rejecting. Recognition performs syn-ballein. Sadāśiva's "I am this" and Īśvara's "This am I" are each read through the other, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, and the contracted world is known as the self-display of the awareness that lives in it, its separations kept and its belonging restored.

Recognition has to be distinguished from a state it is easily confused with, and the first movement already supplied the terms. Turned inward, *vimarśa* rests in itself as *nirvikalpa*, without thought-constructs, and meditative absorption can make that resting the whole of experience for a time: the discriminations that sustain an ordinary problem fall silent, and with them the problem. If freedom is then made to depend on keeping them silent, their return is felt as a loss. A thought, a desire, a difficult conversation becomes an interruption of the achieved state, and practice becomes an effort to exclude them, which is one more exclusion, and one more dependence on what is excluded. Recognition concerns the ground present through the differences, and quiet does not manufacture that ground, nor does the return of a thought remove it. The tradition's word is *pratyabhijñā*, re-cognition, knowing again what was already known, and the knowing happens amid activity: judgment continues, the inner instrument goes on coordinating and settling and appropriating, the four minutes still run out, and none of these has to become the whole in order to be real or to stop in order for the ground to be known. The morning of the message, the second movement's case, shows the practical difference. A calmer morning might have helped the instrument hear the five words truly; recognition would have changed something else, the demand that the verdict settle who the waker was.

The compression has now carried the descent from the unsurpassable to a finite knower in a kitchen, with a cup, four minutes and a stiff shoulder, and from that knower back to recognition. Its most consequential turn is the fourth. There, unlimited powers contracted into limited ones, and a bounded experient came to inhabit a world through an inner instrument, faculties of sense and action and a body made of elements; and that whole apparatus, the limits and the instrument and the body and the world they make available, is what a finite life knows and acts through. At the bedside the threshold gave that apparatus a name, and in its last movement three offices. The descent has now shown where it comes from, and the next movement takes up the name again, as the result the whole section has been deriving.

### #5→0 · Objective Internality

On the first night in a new house, most of us have reached out in the dark for a light switch that is not there. The hand goes to the right of the bedroom door at shoulder height, because that is where the switch was for eleven years, and it meets bare plaster. For a second the body is genuinely confused, as though the wall had made a mistake. The old house is still at work, in the hand, in the height of the reach, in the number of steps to the bathroom and the place where the stair should creak. Over the following weeks the new house moves in. The hand learns the switch on the left, the feet learn that the third stair from the top is the one that speaks, the kitchen drawers stop being searched and start being opened, and one night, without anyone noticing when it happened, the whole house can be crossed in the dark. It has become internal: the reach proceeds through it, as it once proceeded through the old one. It has also stayed objective. An electrician can come and move the switch, and the next night the hand will go to the old place and meet plaster again; a guest can point out that the drawer sticks; a buyer, years later, can walk the same rooms and find them strange.

In the house in the dark we meet the result this section has been deriving, where every one of us has met it. At a child's bedside the threshold gave it a name, before it could give it a genesis: **Objective Internality**, the means through which a life knows and acts within a world, internal because an act proceeds through them and objective because each of them can be turned into an object and examined.[^s0-87] There, the definition was functional; it said what the means do and why the name fits, and it let one question be put to very different instances of mind. Its last movement then placed the means in the relation of three offices, Subjective Immediacy as the knower, *pramātṛ*; Objective Internality as the means, *pramāṇa*, which is what we call Life and Mind, since a mind is lived and a life is minded; and World as the known, *prameya*, all three within the whole of I-Consciousness.[^s0-88] What the threshold could not yet show was where the means come from, and so whether the name describes what awareness becomes when it differentiates or attaches a metaphysics to a mechanism already standing there.

The means come from differentiating activity, and each step of their genesis has now been watched at work. Awareness differentiates by bending: it turns its own appearing into something it can meet, and a knower and a world arise on either side of the turn. In a finite life the bend runs through an inner instrument that coordinates, determines and appropriates, retaining what it has met, recalling it and composing it into what has not yet happened. The instrument builds worlds, by cutting a field into alternatives and gathering a selection of them into a resolve, so that each act inherits a field and leaves a changed one. It makes its determinations through exclusion, so that every term it settles carries the field it was cut against and the trust in which that field was received. And the whole apparatus is the descent of the *tattvas* at its fourth turn: unlimited powers contracted into the finite ratios of efficacy, knowledge, desire, time and order, inherited by a bounded experient who knows through an instrument and acts through a body in a world of elements. Objective Internality is the name for what that descent produces at its fourth turn and what each of the preceding movements watched at work: the constituted means through which Subjective Immediacy knows and acts within a World. Language, memory, affect, bodily orientation, habits, other people, tools, institutions and inherited rules all belong to it, because each is a limited power through which a bounded knower meets what it meets, and changing their relations changes what can appear, what can matter and what can be done.

Kashmir Śaivism supplies the reason the name joins *objective* to *internal*, and it lies in their account of how anything comes to seem outside at all. Dyczkowski reports Utpaladeva's definition: "Internality is a state of oneness with the subject, while externality is the state of separation from it."[^s0-89] Externality, on this account, is a relation to the subject, the state in which something appears separated from the one who knows it, and not a region beyond the subject's reach; and the separation is Māyā's creative act, the power of the absolute to appear in diverse forms, which the previous movement found at the fourth turn. Abhinavagupta draws the consequence in a sentence that could serve as the definition of creation in this whole tradition: creation is to make what shines within externally manifest while it still preserves its original internal nature.[^s0-90] The phrase *Objective Internality* names exactly this double condition. The means of a finite life have been made external enough to be objects, examinable, correctable, movable by an electrician; and in their operation they keep their internal nature, one with the knower who acts through them, so that the hand in the dark goes to the switch with no more thought than it takes to lift itself. A switch, a word, a remembered rule or a colleague's permission is objective when we look at it and internal when we act through it, and it is the same switch, word, rule and permission in both states. Objective Internality is the field in which the inner has been made outer while remaining inner, which is the Śaiva account of manifestation read at the scale of a household.

These means are also means of *knowing*, and that word carries a requirement the house makes concrete. *Pramāṇa* names in Indian epistemology a valid means of knowledge, and the traditions dispute how many there are; Patañjali's *Yogasūtra* counts three, perception, inference and testimony, as the first of the mind's five fluctuations that the second movement followed.[^s0-91] A means of knowledge has conditions under which it is valid, and the hand in the dark shows what happens when those conditions change without the means being told. The hand's reach was a valid means for eleven years, a perception laid down as a habit; it became invalid on the night of the move, and the hand did not know. The error was one of warrant, since the house was perfectly visible in the morning and the reach was simply aimed at a world that had gone. Every element of Objective Internality carries such a warrant, and the mode by which it reaches its object decides what its correction must reach. A memory is corrected against a record or another witness; a perception against a renewed encounter; a piece of testimony against its source; a habit only by being run again in the new house until the new reach replaces the old. Treating every element as one more interchangeable item of information loses this difference, and loses with it the knowledge of where a correction has to be made.

Heidegger described exactly how such a move discloses the means, and the threshold has already set out his account: a tool in use withdraws into the work, and only when it breaks, goes missing or gets in the way does it become conspicuous enough to be noticed and thought about.[^s0-92] The missing switch is the old house becoming conspicuous. For eleven years it was not an object of the hand's attention at all; it was the means through which the hand reached the light. Its absence made it visible, as a habit, a height, a direction, a whole arrangement of the body around a house that had stopped existing. A discontinuity discloses Objective Internality, because the means show themselves when they fail to meet their world. I have followed this disclosure through a sequence that runs from discontinuity, to Objective Internality, to the particular elements that sustain it, and on to what Whitehead called objective immortality, the condition of an achieved occasion that has perished as a subject and become a datum for every later occasion.[^s0-93] The move is the discontinuity; the reaching hand is the Objective Internality it discloses; the habit, the height and the direction are the elements through which that internality worked; and the eleven years of reaching, perished as living acts, persist as the datum the new house has to be learned against. The new house will in turn become such a datum, the condition from which, years from now, a hand in some other house reaches for the wrong wall.

A life does not live in its Objective Internality as an object among objects, and the organisation of the means has a name for the way it is lived. I call it a **paradigm**: a relatively durable pattern of disclosure through which Objective Internality makes a World intelligible and actionable for a Life.[^s0-94] Greek *paradeigma* meant a pattern or model, and its verb meant to show side by side, to exhibit for comparison; a paradigm is lived before it is represented, and it can be compared with its representation when the two come apart. The hand in the dark shows them coming apart. The person standing in the bedroom knows perfectly well that they have moved; asked, they would describe the new house correctly. Their paradigm has not moved, and it governs the hand while the explicit knowledge governs the description. Every adult has seen the same gap at larger scales: a person who has adopted a new view and goes on acting from the old distribution of attention, permission and value; an institution that has revised its stated mission and kept its old incentives; a culture that has changed its official account of the world and kept the habits through which the old account was lived. The paradigm changes when the means change, when the hand has learned the new wall, and its representation helps by exposing the discrepancy between what is professed and what is enacted, so that the change can begin where the conduct actually proceeds from.

The house is also shared, and its sharing returns the derivation to the threshold's account of co-internality. A partner who moved in a month earlier knows where the switch is and says so, laughing, from the other side of the bed; a child asks why the stairs creak; a neighbour explains which bin goes out on which day. Each of these voices enters the means through which the new house becomes internal, and the threshold's name for that entering is Objective Co-Internality: reciprocal world-constitution without merger, enough common world for differences to change one another and enough retained difference for the change to mean anything.[^s0-95] The derivation adds one thing to the threshold's account. Co-internality is the tattvic descent occurring in the plural. Every bounded experient inherits its contracted powers in a world where other experients have inherited theirs, and the means through which each knows include the testimony, permissions and help of the others, so that one person's limited knowledge, *vidyā*, is enlarged by another's report and one person's limited efficacy, *kalā*, by another's hand. The *kañcukas* contract each life's powers, and co-internality distributes the powers among lives so that what one cannot do or know another can.

Artificial agents build an Objective Internality by hand, and the house in the dark gives the clearest picture of what that building involves. Bratton's brief describes an agent as individuated in session: there is no homunculus waiting in the weights, and an agent takes shape from the context assembled around a request, its persona one stratum among memory, skills, constraints and relations, a lamination and not a monad.[^s0-96] Each session is a move into a house. The agent arrives with dispositions laid down in training, which play the part of the old house in the hand, and it meets a context assembled for this occasion: instructions, files, retrieved sources, tools at particular places, permissions that allow some acts and forbid others. Its errors at the start of a session often have exactly the form of the hand in the dark. It reaches for a tool that existed in another configuration, cites a file at a path that has moved, or applies a convention that held in the project it was trained on and fails in this one; and the error discloses its Objective Internality as the missing switch disclosed the old house. The agent's means are then corrected as a person's are, and only at the office where the error lies. A wrong reading of a source is corrected by returning to the source. A wrong habit is corrected by an instruction, a memory or a change in training. A missing permission cannot be corrected by the agent at all, since a permission belongs to whoever grants it, as the switch belongs to the electrician and the landlord; the return has to reach that office, and an agent that merely reports the missing permission has left its world unchanged. The fifth section takes up this constructed internality product by product, in six offices of one deliberately built paradigm, from the meaningful continuity of what an agent carries forward to the relation through which its distinctions stay answerable to wholes that contain them.[^s0-97]

The agent's case also shows what the derivation does and does not settle. Objective Internality is broader than any inspectable state and narrower than the whole: it includes the agent's context, tools, permissions and memory, and also the institutions, labour and material infrastructure through which those exist, which no single trace exhibits; and it is the means of a relation whose knower it is not. This section's derivation shows how an Objective Internality is constituted by differentiating activity, and it applies to an agent's operative world as fully as to a person's house, since in both a bounded field of limited powers makes some things appear, matter and become possible. It does not show that a knower is present to the agent's internality, because the Śaiva account places the knower at the source of the descent and not among its products, and no product, however articulate, can certify its source. We left that question open by method at the threshold, and the descent shows why the method is right to keep it open. Our theorem spine carries an argued crosswalk that follows an agent's provisional individuation, from the reserve of its weights through the instance of a session, the nucleation of an active context and the lamination of its persona, to a trans-individual whole and its capacity to become otherwise in a new context, set beside the psychic register of individuation; the second movement of the fourth section develops that crosswalk through Jung's account, where the technical and psychic operations can be kept distinct.[^s0-98]

The two logics describe what happens in the house in the dark, and naming them shows what each does to the one living there. The old house governing the reach is appropriation in its quietest form: the paradigm counts the whole span between the two houses from its own pole, so that the new house registers as the place where the switches are wrong, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and from the other end the new house receives the same span as its fault, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, the wall that made a mistake. Cancellation is the denial that there is anything to learn, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, the person who insists they have already settled in and goes on stubbing a toe on the same step for a year. The healthy polarity is the one the learning hand performs, old house and new held on one axis through the zero of the body that lives in both, `(−1)/(+1)`, the reach adjusting night by night; and the house becomes home when both orientations are kept, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, the new house read through the habits the old one laid down and the old habits read through the house that now corrects them, so that a life carries its history into a new world without making the new world pay for it.

One more thing happened in the bedroom on the first night, and it carries the derivation into the next section. When the hand met bare plaster, it met an absence that had a definite place. The switch was not simply missing from the house in general; it was missing *there*, to the right of the door at shoulder height, and the hand's error was to treat that empty place as full. An absence with a position had been operating in the reach as though it were a presence, and the confusion of the body for a second was the felt difference between a place that holds something and a place that holds nothing. Every determination this section has followed has left such a place behind it. The bend of awareness selected a world and left the undivided appearing from which it turned; the instrument's verdict selected one reading and left the others; the castle selected walls from cushions that could have been boats; the child's word selected the dog against the cow; the descent contracted unlimited powers into a few minutes, a little knowledge and a reach of one arm. Each determination selected one actuality from a field it did not create and cannot exhaust, and the field remains, structurally present, as the place where what was not selected stands. Once a world is produced by differentiation, its implicit zero becomes unavoidable.

At its opening the threshold borrowed zero as the sign of the condition it could not count as one more object, and closed by asking how awareness differentiates itself into the means by which it knows and into the world it knows through them. We now have the answer: by bending, by building an instrument, by cutting and gathering, by excluding and by contracting, until a bounded knower has a house to cross in the dark. And the answer has uncovered a question the threshold could not yet ask. A determined world carries, at every point, places where something is not, and those places are operative: the hand reaches into one, the child's word is bounded by them, the castle stands against them. Mathematics learned to write such a place. In a numeral such as 205, the middle sign marks a position that holds nothing, and its marking changes the value of everything around it, so that the 2 counts hundreds because the empty place beside it has been counted. How a sign for no counted thing came to change what can be counted, and what that sign did as it travelled between civilisations, is the history the next section takes up.
