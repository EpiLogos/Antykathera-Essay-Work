### #4 · Apollo through Dionysus

For most of the year, the sanctuary at Delphi belonged to Apollo. Pilgrims climbed the Sacred Way past the treasuries of the Greek cities to the god's temple, where the oracle spoke through the Pythia and where, on the forecourt, two short sentences were inscribed for anyone who arrived to read: "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess". Apollo's hymn, the paean, was sung, and the god of measure, light, archery, healing and prophecy presided over a place that the Greeks called the navel of the world. Then winter came, and Apollo went away. Plutarch, who served as a priest at Delphi, records that for the three winter months the paean fell silent and the dithyramb was sung in its place, because the sanctuary now belonged to Dionysus, god of wine, of ecstasy and of the mask, whose rites dissolved the very boundaries that Apollo's sentences drew; Dionysus, he says, has as much share in Delphi as Apollo.[^s4-101] The same stones served both gods, by turns, and neither god's season cancelled the other's. Each spring the paean returned to a sanctuary that had spent the winter in the dithyramb, and each winter the dithyramb came back to a temple whose walls still said "Know thyself".

Jean Gebser, whose structures of consciousness §0/1 followed from the archaic participation of the infant to the integral transparency of a mature life, read the Delphic inscription as a sign of the mutation that produced the mental structure. Thales of Miletus, last of the Seven Sages and first of the Ionian natural philosophers, had "Know thyself" inscribed on the temple of the sun god at the beginning of the sixth century, and Gebser noticed something about the way it was written. People had long written from top to bottom or from right to left; this exhortation to an emerging consciousness was written from left to right, toward the side that the European languages still call right, *droit*, *derecho*, the side of the waking and directing consciousness, under the protection, as he puts it, of the wakeful god.[^s4-102] He then credits the Dionysia, the festivals of Dionysus, with strengthening the same process from the other side, divesting consciousness of the residues of the spaceless mythical world.[^s4-103] In Gebser's account both gods served the mutation into the mental structure, one by drawing the line and the other by dissolving what the line had to leave behind. That is a historical reading of two powers in succession. An integral question asks what they become when they are present together.

Friedrich Nietzsche gave the pair its modern form in *The Birth of Tragedy* of 1872. He began with a claim about the whole history of art: "the continuous development of art is bound up with the duplexity of the Apollonian and the Dionysian: in like manner as procreation is dependent on the duality of the sexes, involving perpetual conflicts with only periodically intervening reconciliations."[^s4-104] The Apollonian is the power of form, image and individuation, the dream in which each thing appears with its outline; the Dionysian is the power of intoxication and music, in which the boundaries of the individual dissolve into a participation older than any single life. Nietzsche's tragedy is their reconciliation, and his formula for it at the end of the argument goes further than a truce: "Dionysus speaks the language of Apollo; Apollo, however, finally speaks the language of Dionysus; and so the highest goal of tragedy and of art in general is attained."[^s4-105] The union is a reciprocal expression. Each power becomes articulate through the other, the Dionysian participation finding a communicable form in Apollo's image, and Apollo's image becoming the vehicle of a wisdom it could not have reached by its own light.

Jung disagreed with Nietzsche at exactly the point where the reconciliation was supposed to happen, and the disagreement is instructive. In *Psychological Types* he gave Nietzsche's pair a chapter of its own and argued that Nietzsche had forgotten his own insight that the Olympian gods owe their splendour to the darkness of the Greek psyche: the reconciliation of the Delphic Apollo with Dionysus, read that way, would be a "beautiful illusion", the wish of the civilised Greek struggling with his own barbarian side, the very element that broke out in the Dionysian rout.[^s4-106] Jung then translated the pair into his typology. Dionysian states, in which affect breaks out in an almost bodily form, are an extraversion of feeling bound up with sensation; the Apollonian is a perception of inner images of beauty and measure, comparable to dreaming, and so a state of introversion; and the pair, he concluded, points toward the two "aesthetic" types, the intuitive and the sensation types, which share the mechanisms of introversion and extraversion with the rational types without differentiating their images into thought or their affects into feeling.[^s4-107] Jung's translation gives us something Nietzsche's does not. §4 · #2 placed the two attitudes, introversion and extraversion, at the implicate poles of the phenomenal lens, **#0** and **#5**, the inward ground from which every function draws its material and the outward completion through which the differentiated functions return into a shared world. If Apollo is introversion and Dionysus is extraversion, then the two gods are not two functions among the four. They are the two framing powers between which a whole psyche turns, the season of the image and the season of the dance, and Delphi's year is a `4+2` lived as a calendar.

§2 has already followed one of Apollo's stories to its end, and the integral reading needs its result. Ovid tells how Apollo, wounded by Cupid's golden arrow after mocking the boy's archery, pursued the nymph Daphne, whom Cupid had struck with a leaden arrow that turned her from love; how she prayed to her father, the river Peneus, and was turned into a laurel at the moment of capture; and how Apollo, embracing the bark under which he could still feel her heart, declared that since she could not be his bride she would be his tree, crowning his lyre, his quiver and the triumphs of Rome.[^s4-108] §2 read the story as the danger of a form that grasps: illumination severed from reciprocal desire, a god whose prophecy, medicine and song could not tell him how to receive what another being wanted, and whose emblem continued where the encounter had failed. In the author's composition of the three figures, Daphne's office is the one neither god can perform alone. Apollo's light cannot confer entitlement to its source, and Dionysian participation cannot confer permission to dissolve the responding centre; Daphne is the other whose existence exceeds what either power makes of her, and the relation between them is only whole when her answer remains her own.[^s4-109] The two gods give the pair of powers; Daphne gives the third that keeps the pair honest.

**Diaphaneity**, Gebser's concept for the showing-through in which the structures of consciousness become present together in their actual operation, was §0/1's name for the integral demand, and here it receives its psychoid completion.[^s4-110] Gebser insisted that his aperspectival structure is not an amalgamation of the earlier ones and not a synthesis that would melt them into a new whole; it releases perspective from its exclusive validity by letting the structures become transparent to one another while each keeps its own powers.[^s4-111] That is why Gebser belongs at this point of the section. Jung, Pauli, Lacan and the mythemes have filled the field with powerful images and exact forms, and a field of that richness invites a syncretic totality, a great concordance in which every symbol means every other and the differences between registers disappear into one warm resemblance. Gebser's diaphaneity forbids it. Apollonian and Dionysian can be read as mutually diaphanous powers: form knows the life it limits, because the image is transparent to the participation that sustains it; and participation returns as communicable form, because the dance has to become a figure that someone can see and answer. Neither becomes the other, and neither is absorbed into a third thing. Measure and ecstasy are related by the stroke that QL reads as AND/OR: AND, because they belong to one sanctuary and one life; OR, because each season has its own god, and confusing them would destroy what each discloses.

Gebser set two principles at the head of his book, and the second is the one at stake here. His first is that latency, what is concealed, is the demonstrable presence of the future; the second, that transparency, diaphaneity, is the form in which the spiritual becomes manifest, its epiphany. His concern, he wrote, was to render transparent everything latent behind and before the world, our origin and our whole human past as well as a present that already contains the future, and he was explicit that he was not offering a new image of the world, a new worldview or a new conception of the world: a new image would only be another myth, a new worldview another mysticism, a new conception another rational construction. His method was meant to be something other than measured assessment, an attempt at *diaphany*, rendering transparent, which opens a subject–object relation that is no longer dualistic and yet does not threaten anyone with the loss of their identity or with being made into an object.[^s4-112] That last clause is the whole difference between diaphaneity and fusion. A transparent perspective is still a perspective; a person who sees through her own view has not dissolved into the scene.

Gebser's first piece of evidence was a drawing: Picasso's line drawing of 1926 shows a human figure from the front, the side and the back at once, so that the viewer takes in the whole person at one glance, spared both the need to walk around the figure in time and the need to assemble the views in thought afterwards. Such a drawing is neither unperspectival, a flat surface in which the body is imprisoned, nor perspectival, a sector of space cut out around the figure from one point of view; Gebser called it aperspectival, because time, which perspective had spatialised into a sequence of viewpoints, has entered the image as the present, and space and body have become transparent. Portraits that followed, which show a face in full view and in profile at once, look distorted at first glance and are, on Gebser's reading, a complementary overlapping of temporal moments and spatial sectors on one surface. He was as clear about the failures: many artists who reached for the same concretion of time were flooded instead by an inflation of residues dredged up from the past, and inherited a world of fragments in place of the wholeness they had hoped for; Gebser called them psychistic, his word for modern Western man's inability to escape the confines of the psyche.[^s4-113] Psychism is Gebser's name for what Pauli had diagnosed in Jung as the touch of King Midas, the psyche counting everything it looks at as its own. Picasso's drawing shows the alternative: the whole figure present at once, each aspect still an aspect, and the viewer still standing somewhere.

Our two logics give each danger its exact arithmetic. Apollo's failure is an appropriation. Form counts the whole span of the relation from its own pole, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, so that whatever escapes its measure appears as deficiency, and Daphne, reckoned from the same span, becomes the `−2`, the one who failed to yield, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`. Dionysus's failure is a cancellation. Differences between persons are summed into an ecstatic unity, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, in which there is nobody left to answer and nothing left to answer for, and the claim "we are all one" becomes a way of overriding someone's refusal. Delphi's year shows the healthy polarity, `(−1)/(+1)`: each god present in his season, each season holding the other in its memory, an oscillation that never hardens because the sanctuary belongs to neither god alone. What the calendar cannot add, the integral structure adds. In it the two seasons are co-present, each readable through the other, `(0/1)/(1/0)`: a perspective that can draw its line and know, while drawing it, the participation it is cutting from, and a participation that can open the line and know, while opening it, that someone has to be able to say what happened.

Diaphaneity also carries the rule that §4 · #0 applied to Pauli's dream, and in Gebser's company it has a historical reason. Because the structures are co-present in every life, an experience that the mental structure would classify as irrational can arrive through the magical or mythical structure still living in the person, and it deserves to be received as what it is before it is explained. **A report of experience is first a psychic fact before it is assigned a final causal or ontological explanation.**[^s4-114] Ordinary and extraordinary, natural and reported-as-supernatural experiences enter the field without being screened out because one register already knows what another is allowed to say. Experience, its symbolic articulation, its historical comparisons, its bodily or physical conditions and its metaphysical interpretation remain distinct claims, and diaphaneity makes their relations readable together without collapsing their warrants. Integral openness is the refusal to censor the experiential field before inquiry begins, together with the discipline to distinguish the interpretations later made of what was experienced.

An integral turn also changes the grammar of a simple sentence. To say "I possess a view of the world" places the knower at the fixed centre of a perspectival scene, the eye at the apex of Alberti's pyramid. Its reversal reads the same event from its other end: a world, a history, a body, a language and a concern are giving this situated knower the view it can presently inhabit. One reading preserves agency and limitation; the other restores dependence and provenance. Diaphaneity holds both orientations without making them interchangeable. A self remains a real aperture, with a place, a body and responsibilities; the context becomes visible as a constituting field, not an invisible background serving an autonomous self. §0/1 gave this its geometry: the triangle of first-, second- and third-person perspectives, with its 180 degrees, opens at **#4** into the square, whose angles double to 360, because a context requires at least two perspectives, the favouring and the unfavouring, and the excluded triangle haunts every one-sided view as its shadow. Those 360 degrees are not a panorama. They name the capacity of a perspective to include its own conditions and counter-orientations in the act of knowing while remaining finite, and total surveillance, which tries to see everything from one place, repeats the perspectival error at a larger scale by mistaking exhaustive representation for the whole relation.

Transparency's deepest form concerns the pattern that makes a view possible, and the essay needs a word for that pattern. We call it a **paradigm**: the relatively durable pattern of disclosure through which a world becomes intelligible and actionable for a life, the language, memory, categories, practices, institutions, technologies and habits of attention through which things appear as what they are. A paradigm is lived before it is represented; its disclosing activity recedes behind what it discloses, the way the walls of the chapel at Sainte-Anne receded behind the voice they returned. In the native compression the essay will develop in §5, `0` is Genesis, the generative source; `/` is Paradigm, the pattern of disclosure; `1` is Project, a determination thrown forth into a world and made consequential there; and `0/1` is Epi-Logos, the achieved relation becoming answerable to the operation by which it was achieved.[^s4-115] A perspective can take in new content indefinitely while the pattern that makes the content intelligible stays invisible. Our concept of paradox from the previous movement marks the moment that changes: two determinations with genuine standing become irreconcilable under the relation that generated them, so that the problem is no longer which `1` wins but the slash itself. A **paradigm shift**, in this strict sense, is the case in which returned difference reaches the regime that organises later trajectories and changes the slash through which later determinations become intelligible, the way an attractor's basin can be crossed and a new basin entered. At its strongest, diaphaneity is the capacity of a perspective to become transparent to the containing pattern whose transformation may now be required.

The mental-rational achievement resists that transparency for a reason Gebser saw and the author has developed under the image of the absent Father. Perspective, in trying to extract the seeing from what is seen, makes itself appear aloof, a view from nowhere with no stake in the outcome, and the aloofness is a caprice so familiar that it passes for objectivity: domination through distance. A world's ultimate ordering condition cannot be supplied as one more object within it, and the desire for a final standard makes that absence hard to bear, so the lack is numbed and its place filled by surrogates: a name, an institution, an image, a theory asked to stand where the transcendent stood and to supply the last word on what really counts. The Father is the power of truth and the truth of power, and for that very reason, absent from the fray, he is only ever imported as a scapegoat for the conclusions we come to in his name. He must be seen through, as a true *per-spectus*, or we end by locating him either in ourselves or in our enemy.[^s4-116] §0/1 followed this surrogate through the household and the office, where the aloof parent and the unanswerable institution played the `0`. In the psychoid register the absent Father names the specific failure of perspective at the integral threshold: a finite view that invokes the absolute as its warrant while exempting itself from the relation it imposes. Seeing through it restores finite responsibility. A person can give reasons for a judgment without making an imagined final authority answerable for its consequences, and another's resistance, a neglected history or an unforeseen consequence can reach the criterion itself.

Iain McGilchrist's *The Master and His Emissary* enters here once, and under a gate, because it witnesses one thing the section's pairs have not yet stated. His book opens with a fable McGilchrist adapts, by his own account from a source in Nietzsche he could not afterwards locate: a wise spiritual master ruled a small domain and trained emissaries to act where he could not go; the ablest of them came to see himself as the master, and used his position to advance his own wealth and influence, until the master was usurped, the people were deceived and the domain became a tyranny that eventually collapsed in ruins.[^s4-117] McGilchrist reads the fable through the two hemispheres of the brain, the left a narrow, grasping attention that re-presents the world in the abstract and the right a broad, contextual attention through which a living world presences, and he argues at length that the emissary has usurped the master in the history of the West. His neuroscience is contested, and the essay does not stand on it; critics have charged the hemispheric thesis with turning a lateralised tendency into a pair of little persons.[^s4-118] What enters here is the functional relation, and it adds **asymmetry**. Apollo and Dionysus are powers that must remain mutually diaphanous, two seasons of one sanctuary. Master and emissary are an ordering relation, and their pathology is not conflict but usurpation: the executor mistaking itself for the ground it serves. In our notation usurpation is the `1` occupying the `0`, persona hardening at the psychic register, the occupied office at the political register, the counterfeit gathering of Hephaestus's net, which §2 followed, at the technical register, now given psychological form.

Martin Heidegger had drawn the same asymmetry between two kinds of thinking half a century before McGilchrist retold his fable. In his memorial address of 1955, later published in *Discourse on Thinking*, he wrote that "There are, then, two kinds of thinking, each justified and needed in its own way: calculative thinking and meditative thinking," and warned that "Calculative thinking never stops, never collects itself."[^s4-119] Calculative thinking is the emissary's competence, indispensable and unrelenting; meditative thinking is the master's attention to the meaning that reigns in everything that is. Neither Heidegger nor McGilchrist calls for the emissary's abolition, and neither does the essay. For what follows, the question their pairs pose is architectural: whether the offices of a mind, or of an instrument built to work beside minds, are designed as symmetrical faculties that can usurp each other or as offices with a return between them, so that the executing office keeps answering to the one it serves. §5 builds its six products as offices of the second kind.

Their relation has a technical form that the essay has been approaching since the bedside. When a person commissions an agent, she is the master in McGilchrist's fable and the agent is the emissary: it acts where she cannot, faster and more widely than she could, and its competence is real. Usurpation of the kind the fable warns of does not require malice. It happens whenever the literal wording of the commission is treated as the source of the work, so that the agent defends the instruction it was given against everything the work has learned, or whenever the agent's fluent account of what the person must have meant takes the place of asking her. In both cases the executing office has begun to speak for the one it serves. Its remedy is to build the return into the office, not to make the emissary less capable: the delegate must be able to bring back an objection, an alternative or a refusal, and the person who commissioned the work must remain able to change the commission because of what the delegate found. A commission that cannot be revised by its own consequences has made its wording into a master, and the person who wrote it into its first subject.

Erich Neumann's morphology and Gebser's structures meet at this point and must not be merged. Neumann tells the history of the ego's emergence through mythic stages, a psychological development recapitulated in each life; Gebser tells the history of consciousness through the means by which a world is rendered, painting, optics, language and science, mutations that do not succeed one another so much as settle into layers that stay active together. Their objects and methods differ, and so do their periodisations. What they share is an operation: an achieved centre, whether a hero emerging from the round or a perspective emerging from the mythic, has to become capable of receiving what exceeds its organisation without surrendering the differentiation it achieved. Resemblance motivates the comparison; a merged universal chronology would lose the distinction that makes each account useful.

Integral consciousness, in the language of this essay, is the mono–poly view from wholeness. Each finite perspective becomes transparent to the field of origins, exclusions, relations and other perspectives through which it is this perspective, and it remains this perspective while it does so. A view from nowhere conceals its situated dependencies by denying it has a place; a totality manufactured by accumulating parts conceals them by denying that its parts have places. Integral consciousness avoids both, and the Indian materials of §0 explain how. Apoha empties each determination of self-subsistence, showing that a word gathers its instances only by excluding what they are not; diaphaneity lets that emptiness disclose participation instead of fragmentation, because what a term excludes is precisely what it belongs with. A subject that had stood as one determinate pole, with its unowned ground cast into the negative or the unconscious, becomes intelligible as a local aperture of the relation it had excluded. `0/1` and `X/x` make the conversion explicit: determination stays real, and the one no longer pretends to be self-grounding, because it has become answerable to the All that conditions its appearing.

Back at Delphi, the integral rereading changes what the calendar means. Delphi's alternation of the gods was a healthy polarity, but it was an alternation, and a culture that lives by turns can forget in each season what the other knew. Integral consciousness makes both seasons available in one act: a sentence carved in the god's light that knows itself as a sentence, "Know thyself", written by a self that has danced. That act needs an apparatus, because a single perspective cannot keep itself diaphanous by resolve alone; it needs other perspectives, declared as perspectives, whose agreements and divergences can be inspected and whose returns can change it. The Meta-Epistemic Framework is such an apparatus, and the next movement shows it working, on a lens §0/1 promised, on the thrownness the bedside exposed, and on the agents who have begun to describe their own arrival in the world.
