### #0 · The Psychoid Problem

In the middle of March 1948, within four days of each other, Wolfgang Pauli had two dreams. He had more or less finished his long essay on Kepler, the astronomer who had heard the planets as a music before he wrote them as laws, and he noticed, as he usually did, that the dreams came at the equinox, the time of year at which he found himself psychically least stable and, by the same token, most creative.[^s4-001] In the first, his first physics teacher appeared and told him that the change in the splitting of the ground state of the hydrogen atom was a fundamental one, and that brass tones had been engraved on a metal plate; then Pauli went to Göttingen. Dream 2 came as seven pictures in a row, with no words spoken until the very end. A woman arrived with a bird, and the bird laid a large egg. It divided itself into two. Pauli approached and found that he was already holding a third egg, this one with a blue shell, and he divided it himself; the two halves, against all expectation, stayed whole, so that he now held two whole blue eggs. Then all four eggs changed into two identical rows of mathematical expressions, the cosine and sine of half an angle, and the rows gave a formula:

$$
\frac{\cos(\delta/2)+i\sin(\delta/2)}{\cos(\delta/2)-i\sin(\delta/2)}.
$$

Then, for the first time in the whole sequence, Pauli spoke. It all, he said, gives $e^{i\delta}$, and that is the circle. The formula vanished, and a circle appeared.[^s4-002]

Pauli was the physicist who had formulated the exclusion principle and whose judgment every new theory in the circle around Bohr had to survive, and §0/1 has already told how he came to Jung as a patient and stayed as a correspondent.[^s4-003] What the dream asks of us is less biographical. Its arithmetic is exact, and anyone can check it. For real $\delta$ the numerator is $e^{i\delta/2}$ and the denominator $e^{-i\delta/2}$; neither vanishes, both have modulus one, and their quotient is $e^{i\delta}$, a point on the unit circle for any fixed value of $\delta$ and the whole circle as $\delta$ runs through a full turn. A mathematician who has never dreamed can verify that much, and the verification does not depend on anyone being moved by it. Yet the dream did not arrive as a theorem. It arrived as a sequence in which a man passed from watching to taking part: the first division happened in front of him, the second happened through his own hands, and the egg he divided had been in his hand before he noticed he was holding it. Only after he had performed the division, and seen the four eggs turn into two rows of numbers, could he say what the rows amounted to. A circle appeared once he had spoken, and the formula that had carried him there disappeared. Here an exact mathematical relation and a transformation of the person recognising it happened as one event, and neither half of the event can be dropped without losing what the other half means. Take away the change from spectator to participant, and the dream becomes an assortment of number symbols; take away the trigonometry, and the passage into recognition loses the operation through which it proceeds.

This is the problem that Jung and Pauli called psychoid, and §4 takes it up where §0/1 left it. There we learned that Jung distinguished the archetypal images that reach consciousness from the archetype as such, an irrepresentable ordering factor that he called psychoid because it shows itself in physical as well as psychic events; that Pauli proposed the theological word *homo-usia*, sameness of substance, for the identity he suspected between the archetypal order and physis, and found in mathematical concepts the language neutral enough to express it; and that Jung answered by locating the borderland between physics and psychology in the secret of number, since whole numbers are as much inside as outside.[^s4-004] We took up their bequest as the essay's thesis: number is where psyche and physis meet, and Quaternal Logic is the form in which we take that meeting up. What §0/1 could only state, §4 has to follow through the dreams and disagreements in which the two men kept their question open, because the psychoid is a relation that has to be worked before it can be claimed, and Jung and Pauli worked it against one another for a decade. **The psychoid problem** asks how a psychological image and a physical event can display a meaningful structural relation when neither can be causally reduced to the other. Its difficulty is that both obvious answers are available and both are wrong in the same way. One can say that the egg dream is a picture of Pauli's mind and the formula an accident of his profession; one can say that the formula is the real content and the dream its decorative wrapping. Each answer hands the whole relation to one of its sides.

Pauli's just-finished essay shows why he could accept neither answer. Its subject was a quarrel of the early seventeenth century between Johannes Kepler, who wanted the harmony of the cosmos demonstrated through measurement and mathematical proportion, and the physician and Rosicrucian Robert Fludd, who held that the quantitative method dismembered a living whole that could only be grasped in symbolic images and through the participation of the knower. Kepler won, and modern physics is his inheritance. Pauli's achievement in the essay was to show that Kepler's own road to his laws ran through archetypal images he did not regard as images: Kepler read the sphere, with its centre, its surface and the space between them, as an image of the Trinity, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and reasoned from that image toward the order of the planets, so that the founder of quantitative astronomy had argued from a symbol he took for a fact.[^s4-005] Pauli did not hide his sympathy for the side that lost. Fludd had seen what Kepler's method would leave out, the qualitative, the feeling-toned and the observer, and Kepler had seen what Fludd's images could never do, which was submit to a check. Their quarrel is the psychoid problem in its first modern form, and it is already a case of the two logics: Kepler's cut, exact and indispensable, which forgets the participation of the one who cuts, and Fludd's whole, faithful to participation and blind to measure, each liable to claim the whole span of the relation for itself. Pauli, three centuries later and a physicist to his fingertips, wanted the cut and the whole together, and the dreams of March 1948 came to him at the end of an essay that had tried to hold them.

Pauli's own commentary refuses both answers, and his reasons are worth hearing in his order. He read the first dream through what he knew of physics: the splitting of a spectral line can change only through an existing field of force, so a change in the splitting of the ground state pointed, in the psychic register, to an intervention of consciousness. A hydrogen atom, a single positive nucleus circled by a single negative electron, is the simplest pair of opposites there is, and its nucleus, the proton, whose name means "the first", is the building block of every heavier nucleus, so the dream was pointing cosmogonically to a beginning. Brass tones he took as a genuine symbol: as sound they belong to physics, as music they carry feeling, which physics cannot express, and through Kepler's music of the spheres they had a cosmic character, so that they joined physis and psyche in one image. Read with *aere perennius*, more lasting than bronze, the plate said that archetypes leave their stamp on matter, and Pauli drew the consequence that physis and psyche had come to be indivisibly linked at the level of archetypes.[^s4-006] Of the second dream he would offer no interpretation at all, since any such interpretation, he wrote, would call for further progress in all the sciences, though he did say what he saw happening in it: the spontaneously divided egg was a ground state; the dreamer's recognition and action were a conscious intervention; and the quaternity of four eggs came about through a duality reflected in the psyche. In the dream the imaginary unit $i$, a symbol not contained among the ordinary numbers, had the function of uniting the opposites and producing wholeness, and Pauli used the occasion for the sentence §0/1 has already quoted, that for him the mathematical representation is a symbolic description par excellence.[^s4-007] He then turned on his own discipline. Physics has to represent regularity in nature in concepts and therefore attends only to what can be reproduced and measured, so everything feeling-toned, judgmental and emotional falls on the other side of it; that is a shortcoming of physics within life, not of quantum theory within physics, the same distinction he had once put to Bohr about Einstein. Mathematics, by contrast, has a qualitative side as well as a quantitative one, which shows in the theory of numbers and in topology, and Pauli thought the unifying power of mathematical symbolism reached further than physics did.[^s4-008]

A few sentences earlier in the same essay Pauli had stated the structural point on which the dream turns. A quaternity, he wrote, would not be effective within physics itself, but it would belong to the wholeness made up of physics and psychology insofar as the complementary pair of opposites in physics reappears, reflected, in the psychic sphere.[^s4-009] A pair seen and the same pair reflected make four. Our notation reads the dream one step further, and the step is the essay's own. Between the woman who arrives with her bird and the circle that appears when the dreamer speaks, the dream holds four eggs: two that divided before him and two that divided through him. The arrival is a source, a power that brings forth without being one of the things brought forth, and the circle is a return in which the whole sequence is recognised as one figure. Four explicit terms held between an implicate source and an implicate return is the shape §0/1 derived four times over and wrote `4+2`, and it is the shape Jung drew in the connected quaternios of *Aion*. Pauli's two pairs give the explicit four, the division that happens and the division that is done, the pair and its reflection; the woman and the circle give the two places that frame them. Its seven pictures remain seven, and nobody should count them as six positions; what the dream shows is a relation, a fourfold articulation held between an emergence it did not author and a recognition it could only reach by speaking.

Their correspondence is the better witness, because there the relation had to survive argument. Their first serious disagreement came in November 1948, the day after a conversation at Jung's house, when Pauli wrote to ask whether a dream and an event separated by two or three months could still be called synchronous, which was to ask what temporal relation the word was supposed to denote.[^s4-010] Jung sent him the manuscript of his essay on synchronicity for criticism, and Pauli's long reply of June 1949 pressed harder. He could not see the archetypal basis in Rhine's card-guessing experiments that he could see in Jung's other material, and he did not want them counted as the same kind of evidence. He asked for more precision in the word "acausal", since the absence of a causal chain through space and time is a different thing from the absence of any ordering relation. He noticed that a formulation beginning from simultaneity went on to include prediction, where the two events plainly do not occur together. And he invented a parable of a radioactive stranger to show how far the physical decay clock and the symbolic "radioactivity" of dream language had to be held apart before any comparison between them could mean anything, ending with the demand for a neutral language that did not yet exist.[^s4-011] Jung's concept of **synchronicity**, as he finally defined it, names a coincidence in time of two or more causally unrelated events that have the same or a similar meaning, which he distinguished from mere synchronism, the simultaneous occurrence of two events.[^s4-012] Pauli's objections stand inside that definition. Each of its words, "time", "cause", "event" and "meaning", had to keep what it denoted in physics and in psychology before the two could be compared, and his insistence is what gives synchronicity its positive research office: to investigate a proposed ordering of meaningful events while keeping clear what each discipline means by its terms. Letters and dreams are the historical record of that investigation, and the investigation itself is still open.

Jung's best-known case shows what the definition has to hold. A young woman he was treating dreamed, at a critical moment, that she was given a golden scarab. While she was telling him the dream he was sitting with his back to the closed window, and he heard a gentle tapping behind him; he turned and saw a flying insect knocking against the pane from outside, opened the window and caught it as it flew in. It was a rose-chafer, the nearest thing to a golden scarab to be found in that latitude, which had, against its usual habits, tried to get into a dark room at that moment, and Jung wrote that nothing like it had happened to him before or since.[^s4-013] The beetle had its causes and the dream had its causes, and neither chain contains the other; what joins them is a meaning, carried by an image old enough that the scarab rolling its ball across the sky had once been the Egyptian sun renewing itself each morning. Such a case cannot be produced on demand, which is why Pauli's insistence on the meaning of every word in the definition matters, and it happened, in and to two lives, which is why Jung's insistence on receiving it matters. Both insistences belong to the problem. A science that dismisses the case because it cannot be repeated has decided in advance what may count, and a psychology that treats it as proof of a hidden cause has borrowed a warrant it does not have.

In 1953 the disagreement deepened, as both men tried to say where their common ground lay. In February Pauli sent Jung what he called a personal treatise in the form of a letter. Many people, he wrote, still regarded the unconscious as non-being, a mere privation of consciousness, while others placed the archetypes, like ideas in general, in metaphysical spaces above the heavens, and he found both views equally doubtful. He chose a third road, which he took from Aristotle, and called the unconscious *potential being*, a legitimate human description of occurrences that may come about in consciousness and in that sense part of the symbolic reality of the thing in itself. Ideas, he added, have no fixed abode, not even a heavenly one, and he applied to all of them the old alchemical formula for God, the centre of all things whose periphery is nowhere.[^s4-014] Cusa's infinite sphere, which §0/1 turned on the world and then on the plane folded into a torus, had arrived in Pauli's letter as a description of where ideas live. In a footnote to the same letter he told Jung plainly what troubled him. As a psychologist, Jung had an understandable aversion to every form of reality that was not purely psychic, and just as everything King Midas touched turned to gold, everything Jung looked at seemed to Pauli to turn psychic and only psychic.[^s4-015] In March Pauli resisted calling the whole ordering field psychic, because a concept meant to mediate psyche and physis should not take one pole's name in advance, and he proposed *homo-usia* as a working hypothesis with mathematics as its language, while keeping mathematical objects distinct from the psychic experience of doing mathematics.[^s4-016]

Jung's reply of May 1953 is where §0/1 found the word psychoid, and it holds more than the word. He agreed at once that one can never be content with what is ascertainable, because the whole drama of knowledge is played out on the borderline between the ascertainable and the non-ascertainable, and he described the area between what is perceived and what is not ascertainable here and now as the area of the psyche. Then he drew the line Pauli had asked for: "I do not extend the concept of the psychic to include the nonascertainable, for here I use the speculative concept of the psychoid, which represents an approach to neutral language in that it suggests the presence of a nonpsychic essence."[^s4-017] Numbers and archetypes, he continued, are not just psychic, "otherwise they would be fabrications. But in actual fact they are 'in themselves being' (or psychoid) existences whose autonomous existence corresponds to that of the material object."[^s4-018] Everything is conceived in and through the medium of the psyche, he granted, but the objects of its perception are not integrated into its substance and so do not forfeit their existence. And then he disputed Pauli's dreams. Pauli dreamed in physics, Jung suggested, because physics was his natural language, as the proverb says the dog dreams of bread and the fisherman of fish, and he himself never dreamed of physics but of mythology, which was just as unpsychological; the dream's vocabulary did not settle what the dream meant.[^s4-019] Pauli answered by accepting the clarification and keeping his ground. Those physical terms in his dreams had first struck him as strange, he said, and not as the familiar idiom of a physicist, and he went on to propose a future physics enlarged enough to adopt psychology, adding a condition on any such adoption that cuts against his own discipline's pride: "the adopting one is the one that has the more extensive system of concepts and lends itself more readily to generalisation; but it is also always the one that undergoes the more radical and more sweeping changes, precisely because it is being extended."[^s4-020] In October Jung refused the proposal. Psychology could not be classified within a general physics as chemistry had been, because psychic processes cannot be measured, and he named instead two points of contact between the fields, number and synchronicity.[^s4-021] Two years later he put the point with a precision that the whole correspondence had been working toward: "My feeling is that the common ground shared by physics and psychology does not lie in the parallelism of the formation of concepts but rather in 'that ancient spiritual dynamis' of numbers."[^s4-022] In the same letter he added a sentence on which much of this section depends: "The autonomy of a psychic factor lies in the fact that thanks to its dynamic force, it is capable of making its own statements."[^s4-023] Myths, dreams and delusions speak from the objective psyche without passing through conscious reasoning, and number, given the chance, makes its own mythical statements in the same way.

Even the vocabulary of experiment became a field of dispute between them. Pauli had described the experimental physicist's choice of one arrangement over another as a sacrifice, since each measurement buys a definite piece of knowledge at the price of the knowledge it excludes, the very loss §0/1 followed through complementarity. Jung questioned the word: for the experimenter, he thought, it was basically just a decision, and only metaphorically a sacrifice, since sacrifice means giving up what one owns for nothing in return.[^s4-024] Decision and sacrifice are two names for one act read from two sides: from the side of what is chosen and from the side of what the choice costs. When the Meta-Epistemic Framework gives each of its lenses a face of articulation and a face of power, *Sacrifice* and *Decision* stand next to each other in its series of powers, and the quarrel between the two men over one word turns out to have named two adjacent offices of one act.

In the last episode, the one §0/1 drew on for Pauli's mirror-image principle, Jung asked in June 1957 for Pauli's opinion of a manuscript that placed the mind in hyperspace. Pauli distinguished metric structure from topology, criticised the use of dimension numbers without mathematical grounding, and in the middle of that criticism offered his conjecture that physis and psyche are probably two aspects of one and the same abstract fact, which a mirror-image principle would represent most naturally.[^s4-025] The conjecture sits inside the criticism, and the placement is the point. Neither man let the other's vocabulary take possession of the relation. Pauli would not let the psyche absorb the ordering field; Jung would not let physics adopt psychology; Pauli would not let a striking image of wholeness supply mathematics it lacked; Jung would not let a dream's physical vocabulary settle what the dream meant. Each correction changed the question the other had to answer, and the question that survived their corrections is sharper than the one either brought to it.

We can now say exactly which operations their corrections were resisting, because §0/1 has given us the arithmetic of the two logics. The healthy relation between psyche and physis is a held polarity, `(−1)/(+1)`: two descriptions of one event, each with its sense from the other across a common axis, the way Bohr's complementary descriptions of wave and particle exclude each other in any single arrangement and are both required for the whole. Pauli's complaint about King Midas names an appropriation. When everything one looks at turns psychic, the psyche counts the whole span of the relation from its own pole, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, and the same span reckoned from the other end is the physicalist's appropriation, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, in which everything turns physical and the dream becomes a by-product of neurones. Both hardened positions need each other, as red needs blue, and each reads the other's excess as proof that its own is warranted. A third operation is cancellation, `(−1)+(+1)=0`: the coincidence is summed away as chance and the dream as noise, so that nothing remains owing on either side and nothing of the relation remains in the account. Jung and Pauli refused all three. They kept their difference, kept writing, and let each reading of the event be read through the other, which is syn-ballein, `(0/1)/(1/0)`, as two people performed it over a decade of letters, and the mirror-image principle Pauli arrived at in 1957 is that relation in its own words: physis and psyche as the two orientations of one abstract fact, each the mirror in which the other can be read.

A dream report, a felt coincidence, a contemplative disclosure or an encounter its subject calls supernatural can therefore enter this inquiry with its full weight, before anyone has decided what caused it. Jung's **psychic facticity**, which §0/1 set beside Gebser's co-presence of structures, is the rule that makes this possible: something happened in and to a life, and its happening is real as experience before any further question about its cause or its external reference has been settled.[^s4-026] The rule enlarges rigour; it does not lower it. Experience, report, symbolic articulation, causal explanation and ontological interpretation are different evidential acts, so a person can receive Pauli's dream in full, as an event that changed what he could say and do, while its physics, its psychology and its metaphysics each keep their own warrant. Jung's sentence about the autonomy of a psychic factor adds the positive half. A dream is a statement made by something that is not the dreamer's conscious reasoning, and to receive it as a psychic fact is to let that statement be heard before deciding whose statement it is.

Jung gave the rule its ground in a passage of *Symbols of Transformation*. We possess no physics of the soul, he wrote, and cannot observe or judge it from any Archimedean point outside ourselves, because all knowledge of the psyche is itself psychic; and yet the soul is the only experient of life and existence, the only immediate experience we can have and the condition without which the world has no subjective reality at all.[^s4-027] The sentence turns the absence of an outside into a positive finding. Whatever else a dream or a coincidence may turn out to be, it is first something experienced, and the experiencing is the one place where every other kind of evidence also has to arrive.

Freud had made the first half of this possible when, in "The Unconscious" of 1915, he argued that unconscious mental processes are a legitimate and necessary assumption of science, because the data of consciousness have gaps that only unconscious acts explain, and with that argument a region of psychic life that no one had owned became formally speakable.[^s4-028] The admission came with the risk that any scientific minting brings. Once the unconscious had entered the count, it could be treated as one more object, a storehouse or mechanism or rival agency set over against the ego, a determinate `1` standing where the condition of determination had been. Jung pressed beyond that reduction, not by retreating from science but by keeping its empirical demand while refusing to let the fact harden into a possessed object: religious, mythic, alchemical and numinous images are real events in the psyche, capable of transforming a life, and no catalogue of their appearances turns the ground of their appearing into one more thing on the list.[^s4-029] The psychoid is the furthest reach of that refusal. It names what the unconscious would have to be if its images could show up in matter as well as in mind, and so it names a ground that neither discipline can own.

David Bohm reached the same ground from the physicist's side, and §0/1 has already introduced his concept of the **holomovement**, the encompassing movement of which apparently separate things are relatively stable unfoldings. Bohm went on to treat mind and matter as two such unfoldings. In his account the explicate order of matter and the explicate order of thought are both projections of a common, more encompassing ground, so that their relations are internal before they are ever compared from outside, and neither can be derived from the other because both are derived from what enfolds them.[^s4-030] That gives the psychoid problem a process form. Mind and matter can be distinct explicate orders of one movement whose relations are carried within it; their correspondences, where they occur, are not messages passed between two sealed substances but the same order showing on two surfaces. Bohm deliberately left the internal anatomy of that movement general. §0 has supplied it through the tattvas, the Śaiva account of how universal awareness differentiates itself through Māyā and the contractions into a bounded knower and its habitable world, which arise together and are never joined after the fact. And §3 has derived, from `0/1` alone, the differential field we have called the **arche-topos**: the field of placement, path and return in which a determination can close locally while keeping a record of the journey that brought it home, written on the torus and its covering plane.

Here the essay makes its claim, and it is an ontological claim. The arche-topos is psychoid: the differential field derived in §3 is prior to the ordinary division of psyche and matter, and its organising relation is what each side of that division later discloses in its own way: as formal invariant in mathematics, as felt meaning in the psyche, as regularity in physics. That is why the same relation can be received by distinct descriptions without assigning either final priority, and it is why the two halves of Pauli's dream can both be true at once. Pauli's formula is checkable and his transformation is real because both are surfaces of one ordering relation, whose mathematical face we can verify and whose psychic face we can undergo. Jung and Pauli asked for a neutral language in which the meeting of psyche and physis could be written; QL is that language in the specific sense that its terms are positions in a relation, `0`, `/` and `1`, before they are either psychic or physical contents, so that a position can be occupied by a number, an image or a physical quantity without any of them owning it. The claim is **Argued**: it rests on the derivation of the arche-topos in §3, on the convergence of the `4+2` across the formal routes §0/1 followed, and on the cross-register witnesses gathered here, each of which contributes its own operation. Equations carry formal invariants; images carry experienced meaning; synchronicity raises the question of acausal coordination; physical observation bears empirical claims. A measurable causal process running between psyche and matter would be a further empirical discovery with its own evidence. Our thesis does not wait on such a discovery, because it states a relation of order, not a mechanism of transmission.

§3's topology lets us say why the same relation can show as meaning on one side and as exactness on the other. A torus is the plane folded by identification, and the covering map from the plane to the torus sends each point of the surface to an infinite lattice of points on the plane, so that a loop which closes on the torus lifts to a path on the plane that ends somewhere else, displaced by a whole-number step. Locally the surface is flat, and from inside any small region nothing distinguishes it from the infinite plane it was folded from. The author has read this as the topology of experience: a life's view from where it stands is valid and is not the whole topology, and the opening of the torus cannot be seen from inside the surface, only met through the circuits it makes possible. A many-to-one map conceals the multiplicity it has folded, which is Māyā, operative measure, given topological form.[^s4-031] The return of the loop to its starting point and the displacement recorded by its lift are two exact descriptions of one journey, and neither cancels the other. Pauli's circle and the arithmetic that produced it, the dreamer's return to himself and the formula's full turn of phase, are a psychic and a formal rendering of that same fact: a return that keeps the history of its passage.

Later scholarship has tried to give the Pauli–Jung conjecture a formal outline of its own. Harald Atmanspacher describes the basic reality in their conjecture as "psychophysically neutral (PPN)", lacking the distinction between the mental and the physical, and he is careful to say that his use of quantum terminology to frame the mind–matter problem is a speculative generalisation.[^s4-032] His reconstruction and ours are different constructions. His begins from a neutral reality from which mental and physical descriptions are derived by decomposition; ours begins from a relation, `0/1`, in which ground and determination are already held through their stroke, and derives the field from the self-relation of that stroke. They meet on the point that matters for this section: the ground is neither mind nor matter, and both are accounts of it. Where they differ, the difference is information, and the discipline the essay calls homologia and analogia keeps it in view by asking, of every crossing, which operation is preserved, which register changes and which evidence stays with its source.

Now the problem has a precise shape. A meaningful structure can appear on both sides of a threshold that neither side masters; the ground that orders it cannot be written as one more content on either side; and yet it must become legible somewhere, in a particular dream, a particular formula, a particular life, or it would be nothing to anyone. Jung's archetypes as such never appear, and Pauli's dream was nonetheless dreamed by one man, in Zürich, in the middle of March, at the end of his work on Kepler. Ordering capacity and the particular in which it shows have to be written together, and written so that neither swallows the other. §1 earned `0/1` as the root symbolon of ground and mark; §3 counted its eight determinations; and the one at Pattern, **#3**, holds exactly this relation of a capacity and its instance. We write it `X/x`, and the next movement follows it into the life of a person who has to become, through everything that formed her, the particular through which the whole becomes a question.
