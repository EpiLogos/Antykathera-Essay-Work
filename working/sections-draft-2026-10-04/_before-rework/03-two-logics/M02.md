### #1 · Dia-ballein: Polarity and Its Collapse

Twelve people sit around a table in a room with no windows, and after three days of evidence they have to turn an evening none of them witnessed into one of two words. The foreman has a form with two boxes. Whatever happened in the stairwell on the night in question, whoever pushed or fell or was pushed, whatever the defendant felt and intended and has regretted since, the room must produce *guilty* or *not guilty*, and the law has made sure there is no third box. Some jurors find this unbearable at first. One of them knows that the truth of that evening is more complicated than either word, that the defendant was frightened as well as angry and that the man who fell had been threatening him for months; another keeps asking whether they can say something about the provocation; a third wants to write a note to the judge explaining what they really think. Nothing on the form allows it. And yet, as the hours go on, most of the room comes to see that the form is protecting something. It does not ask them to say what happened. It asks them whether the charge has been proved. *Not guilty* does not mean *innocent*; it means that the case brought against this person has not been made beyond reasonable doubt, and the doubt, which the law has placed on the defendant's side of the axis from the start, is allowed to stay unresolved. The verdict cuts, cleanly and finally, and in cutting it says exactly as much as it can say and no more.

That form is dia-ballein at its most exact and most humane, and it repays attention, because the logic of the jury room is the logic most of our institutions run on. Two values, opposed, exhaustive: every case must fall into one box or the other, and none may fall into both. The presumption of innocence is the zero of the axis, the point from which the case is measured and to which the defendant returns if the measurement fails, and its office is to keep the polarity held. Guilt has to be established *from* that zero, in one direction, against resistance; it does not sit opposite innocence as an equal possibility weighed at the outset. Placing the burden of proof gives the axis a handedness. This is why the verdict can be decisive without claiming to be complete. It decides one question, whether the state has made its case, and it leaves the whole of that evening in the stairwell, the fear and the anger and the months of threats, in the hands of the people who lived it, where no form can reach.

Change how the verdict is read and the same two words perform the two collapses we wrote in §0/1. Read *not guilty* as *nothing happened*, and the verdict has been made into a cancellation: the charge and its refutation are added together and come out at zero, and the evening is closed like a settled account, with the man at the bottom of the stairs and the defendant's fear both summed out of existence. Read *guilty* as *this is what he is*, and the verdict has been made into an appropriation: the whole span of a life is measured from the charge, the defendant becomes his worst evening, and the public's satisfaction at the conviction, `(+1)−(−1)=+2`, is the convicted man's whole standing in the world, `(−1)−(+1)=−2`, counted from the other end.[^s2-13] Neither misreading is made by the jury. Both are made afterwards, by a newspaper, a neighbour, an employer reading a criminal record years later, and both are made possible by the same thing that makes the verdict useful, its refusal of a third box. Both the exactness of the cut and the danger of its collapse come from one source.

Classical logic is the jury room raised to a law of thought. Aristotle set out its two governing principles in the fourth book of the *Metaphysics*: the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject in the same respect, and between the two parts of a contradiction there can be nothing intermediate, so that of any one subject one must either affirm or deny any one predicate.[^s2-14] The first is the principle of non-contradiction and the second the principle of excluded middle, and together they give a proposition exactly two values, true and false, opposed and exhaustive, the two boxes on the foreman's form. Written on our axis, truth and falsity are `+1` and `−1`, negation is the operation that turns one into the other, and the zero between them is the undecided proposition, the question before it has been answered. A great deal of human thought has been made exact by this logic. Every proof by contradiction, every classification that sorts cases without overlap, every switch in a circuit that is either on or off, every legal verdict and medical test and audit relies on it, and the computational world we now inhabit is built from it at the level of the transistor. Dia-ballein in this form is not a philosophical opinion. It is the operating condition of measurement, and nothing in this essay asks anyone to give it up.

What we ask is that its zero be kept. Held polarity in classical logic does keep it, in the same way the presumption of innocence does: the undecided proposition is where every inquiry starts and where it returns when the evidence fails, and a careful reasoner knows that most of the propositions she meets are sitting there, at zero, waiting. Trouble begins when the logic of two exhaustive values is taken to describe the world as well as our verdicts about it, when the boxes on the form are mistaken for the shape of the evening. Then every situation must already be one thing or the other, every person must already be on one side or the other, and the zero, the undecided, the not-yet-known, the question, appears as a defect in the world, a gap to be closed by deciding, instead of as the condition in which every deciding takes place. Aristotle himself guarded the principles carefully: *at the same time*, *in the same respect*. Each qualification is a recognition that a polarity is held within a field, and that the field has dimensions, time and respect among them, along which opposed predicates can both be true of one subject without contradiction. Our friend at the barrier in §0/1, who both was and was not the person we knew, could be described without contradiction once we said in what respect.[^s2-15] Classical logic, used well, is full of such qualifications. Used badly, it drops them, and the axis collapses into its arithmetic.

Whoever drops them has a name, and §0/1 promised that this section would develop the formula that names him: the devil is the literalist of the minus sign, reading zero as cancellation where Logos reads it as return.[^s2-16] The formula can now be unfolded, because the minus sign does more than one job and the literalist is the one who reads every job as the same. In arithmetic the sign `−` marks a direction: `−1` is a position on the line, one step to the left of the origin, as real a place as `+1` and differing from it only in orientation. That sign also names an operation: subtraction, taking one quantity away from another. And in logic the sign of negation, which classical notation often writes as a bar or a hook and which our axis writes as the passage from `+1` to `−1`, turns an assertion into its denial. A literalist reads all three as the second, so that every minus means *take away*. On that reading the sum `(−1)+(+1)=0` says that the two units have taken each other away and nothing is left: zero is a hole where two things were, the remainder after an annihilation. Read the minus as a direction, and the same sum says something else entirely. One step to the left and one step to the right return to the point from which both steps were measured, and the zero they arrive at is the origin, the one place on the line that both directions share and from which each has its sense. Nothing has been annihilated. Two journeys have come home.

Here the formula's two halves meet. *Cancellation* is the literalist's zero, the place where the account comes out even because everything on one side has been taken away by something on the other. *Return* is the Logos's zero, the place from which the account began and through which its terms remain related, so that coming out even means coming back to the shared condition, with both journeys still remembered. The diabolical, in the Greek sense §0/1 recovered, does to persons what the literalist does to numbers.[^s2-17] The *diabolos* is the slanderer, the one who throws something across between two people so that they appear to each other under an accusation, and the operation of slander is exactly a literal reading of a sign. A neighbour stands somewhere relative to me: to my left on some question, below me in some ledger, opposed to me in some quarrel. That is a direction, a relation, a position on an axis we share. Slander reads the sign as a substance: the neighbour *is* the minus; his difference from me is what he is made of, and the axis that gave his position its sense has dropped out of the account. Once that reading takes hold, the only resolution it can imagine is the literalist's: take the minus away, and what remains is zero, the quarrel settled by the other's removal.

Apoha, the Buddhist theory of exclusion that §0/1 met in Dignāga, shows what the literalist misses in the plainest act of naming.[^s2-18] To apprehend something as a cow is to exclude what is other than a cow, so *cow* is articulated as *not non-cow*, and we noticed then that cancelling the two written negatives would return us to the word *cow* and leave the word's work unexplained. Our literalist of the minus sign cancels them. Double negation, in classical logic, is the identity: not-not-p is p, two minuses make a plus, and nothing is lost on the way. In apoha something is kept on the way, the field of what the word excludes, the not-cows against which this animal is recognised as one of those, and keeping it is what lets differing particulars be understood together. The literalist's arithmetic is correct and its philosophy is wrong. `(−1)(−1)=+1` is true; the claim that the passage through the two negations has left no trace in the meaning is false, and a whole theory of language was built to show why. So it is with every institution that classifies. A category made by exclusion carries its excluded field with it, and an account that forgets the excluded field has not become simpler. It has become a literalist.

Dia-ballein's knowledge, when it collapses, denies its own provisionality, and the denial has a direction. We said in the previous movement that knowledge is a performance of trust wearing the mask of certainty, and that the uncertainty a performance cannot admit has to go somewhere.[^s2-19] In the collapsed logic it goes across the axis. The asserted `+1` feels complete because a `−1` has been found to carry what it could not hold, and the uncertainty that belongs to the shared field, to the limits of the evidence, to the trust that was extended before anything was proved, reappears as a property of the excluded term: the enemy is the reason things are unclear, the scapegoat is the reason things went wrong, the alien intelligence is the reason the future cannot be predicted. Projection is the psychological name for this movement, and it works because it relieves. A diffuse unease that belongs to everyone becomes something located over there, which can be watched, denounced, fought or expelled, and the relief is real even when the location is false. An epistemic cut has become a social act. Nobody had to decide to scapegoat anyone; it was enough to take a mask for a face.

*Agentworld* describes this movement with precision in its account of how human beings meet non-human interlocutors. "Reactive alienation," Bratton writes, "converts strangeness into threat and responds with reflexive rejection: the other must be named, registered, bounded, and held at a distance from what counts as properly ours."[^s2-20] Its counterpart, productive alienation, "treats the non-human interlocutor as a mirror whose strangeness forces the human to see itself at an angle it could not otherwise occupy." Read through the two logics, Bratton's two modes are the held polarity and its collapse. Productive alienation keeps the axis: the machine stands at a real distance from us, its strangeness is a direction we could not otherwise take, and the difference becomes a way of seeing ourselves from somewhere else, which is the self-knowledge the bedside mirror promised in §0/1. Reactive alienation literalises the sign: the machine's difference becomes its substance, its strangeness becomes its threat, and the only resolution imaginable is the one the brief records elsewhere, the fantasy that it must be stopped before it stops us. Bratton adds that "both modes are live at once, and different people oscillate between them, moment to moment," and the oscillation itself is instructive. A held polarity oscillates cleanly, each position passing into relation with the other through the stroke, as Spencer-Brown's re-entering distinction alternated in time; a collapsed one swings between `+2` and `−2`, between the conviction that the machine will save us and the conviction that it will destroy us, and never passes through the zero where the question of what it is, and what we are in relation to it, could be asked.[^s2-21]

There is also a computational name for dia-ballein, and it is the most honest name the logic has, because it states the logic's method and its condition in one phrase: divide and conquer. Faced with a problem too large to solve directly, a divide-and-conquer algorithm splits it into smaller subproblems of the same kind, solves each of those, splitting again if necessary until the pieces are trivial, and then combines the solutions into a solution of the whole. Merge sort is the standard textbook example. To put a long list in order, split it in half, sort each half by the same method, and then merge the two sorted halves by walking down both together and always taking the smaller front element. Textbooks name the three steps plainly: divide, conquer, combine.[^s2-22] The power of the method lies in the first two, which make an intractable task tractable by cutting it into pieces that can be handled independently. Its success lies entirely in the third. A merge sort that divided and sorted but never merged would leave a heap of small ordered lists and no ordered list at all, and every step of the dividing would have been wasted, because the pieces were only ever pieces of one problem.

Politics drops the third step and keeps the first two, which is why §0/1 called divide and conquer the oldest and most enduring trick of any state or power.[^s2-23] The method is genuinely powerful in politics as in computation: a population divided into groups that can be addressed, managed and set against one another separately is far easier to govern than one that acts together. But the political version does not merely forget the combine. It keeps it. The combining step, the capacity to bring the divided parts back into one action, is retained by whoever did the dividing, while everyone else remains divided. Workers appear as individual performances, people seeking help as cases, communities as counted preferences, and the channels through which these separated participants could act together run vertically, through the centre that divided them, instead of horizontally, among themselves. That is the arrangement George Carlin's "big club" described and the Dialog directory documented in §0/1: syn-ballein reserved for the members, dia-ballein for everyone outside.[^s2-24] An algorithm that hid its merge step from its own subproblems and then presented the merged result as something only it could have produced would be a strange algorithm, but it would be a very effective form of rule.

§0/1 added a condition to the trick that deserves to be stated as a proposition. Divide and conquer works only while both parties consent to be divided; whichever logic is at play, it takes two to tango.[^s2-25] The consent in question is not a signature on a contract. It is the acceptance, by each of the divided parties, that the axis drawn between them is the whole of their relation. Each side agrees to reckon the span from its own end: each counts the distance between them as the other's departure, the other's fault, the other's debt, so that one side's `+2` is met by the other's `−2`, and the two together, with perfect symmetry, confirm the line on which they stand. Red against blue was this agreement reckoned from both poles at once. Then the divided parties do the divider's work for him, since each needs the other as the negative through which it knows itself, each treats dissent within its own ranks as contamination by the enemy, and each reads the other's hardening as proof that its own must continue. That is what makes the trick durable. Division is maintained from below, by the divided, out of a loyalty to their side that is also, without their noticing, a loyalty to the axis.

Withdrawing that consent does not mean ceasing to differ. A boundary defended against an assault and the assault on it are not equivalent, and some disagreements are between a truth and a falsehood, which no appeal to shared ground should be allowed to blur. Withdrawing consent means refusing to let the axis be the whole field. It means asking, of any opposition one is enlisted into, what field was divided so that these two terms now appear as mutually exclusive wholes, who drew the line and who holds the combining step, and what the two sides share that neither side's account of the other can see. Here dia-ballein is answered by its own held form. A polarity that remembers its zero can still oppose, contest and refuse; what it cannot do is consent to be divided, because it knows that both its poles have their sense through a field neither owns.

Martin Heidegger gave the arrested runtime its epochal name in a short address of 1955. "There are, then, two kinds of thinking," he said, "each justified and needed in its own way: calculative thinking and meditative thinking." Calculative thinking plans, computes, reckons with given circumstances and counts on definite results; and Heidegger characterised it by what it does not do: "Calculative thinking never stops, never collects itself."[^s2-26] *Never collects itself*: in our terms, the calculative is the thinking that divides and conquers without the step that would gather its results back into the field from which they were taken, a runtime racing from result to result without returning. Heidegger's fear was that "calculative thinking may someday come to be accepted and practiced as the only way of thinking," and in this section's vocabulary that is the capture of a plurality of ways of knowing by one of them, one logic occupying the zero of the whole field.[^s2-27] We receive his distinction and decline the quietism that sometimes accompanied it. "Each justified and needed in its own way" is Heidegger's own sentence, and it should govern how his distinction is used. Calculation reveals; the measurement of a river's flow discloses the river in a definite way, and the computational inheritance of the twentieth century has disclosed the planet to itself on a scale no earlier culture could reach. The pathology lies where this movement has located it, in the cut that forgets the common operation and field by which its poles were produced, and in the runtime that arrests before return. A calculative thinking that collected itself would still calculate. It would also know what it was calculating for, and from what.

Dia-ballein has a crowd as well as a calculation, and the crowd is where the collapsed axis acquires its force. Gustave Le Bon, writing in 1895 from political commitments this essay does not share, made one observation that has outlasted the rest of his book: a crowd in the psychological sense need not be gathered in one place. "An entire nation," he wrote, "may become a crowd" under certain influences, and the individuals who compose it come to "feel, think, and act in a manner quite different" from the way each would in isolation, because the crowd is "a combination followed by the creation of new characteristics" and not an average of its members.[^s2-28] Le Bon's racial hierarchies and sweeping generalisations are no part of what we take from him. What we take is the formal point that a collective can be a formation, something produced across many people that acts on each of them, and that it can be produced at a distance, through media and repeated influence, without anyone standing in a square.

Jung, writing after the Second World War, followed the cost of that formation to the individual. "The bigger the crowd," he wrote, "the more negligible the individual becomes," and he traced the sequence by which the individual's negligibility becomes political: responsibility "is collectivized as much as possible," and the State "in particular is turned into a quasi-animate personality," a being that wills, knows, protects, punishes and demands sacrifice, behind which, Jung insisted, stand the particular people who know how to operate it.[^s2-29] The State becomes a person; the persons become numbers. Here the collapsed axis shows its political mechanism. A field of relation among many people, with all its internal differences, is made to appear as a single subject facing another single subject, *us* against *them*, and each of those apparent subjects is administered by people whose own position in the field has dropped out of view.

Mattias Desmet's account of what he calls mass formation proposes the affective preparation for this transfer: social isolation, lack of meaning, and free-floating anxiety, frustration and aggression, affect with no object adequate to it.[^s2-30] A narrative supplies the object. It concentrates diffuse distress on a target and promises restored coherence through obedience, sacrifice or expulsion, and the resulting unity is felt as relief, because an isolated person has found company in a common enemy. The relief is real; the unity is a counterfeit, because what binds the crowd together is not a recovered relation among its members but a shared fixation on a term outside them. Vicky Iakovou's criticism of the use of the concept of totalitarianism keeps the account honest: mass psychology cannot stand in for the analysis of institutions, parties, offices, property, coercion and the material organisation of authority, and susceptibility to a story does not explain who has the power to enforce it.[^s2-31] We keep both accounts in view because each corrects the other. A full circuit runs through both registers. A population is atomised; its unbound affect is offered a target; the axis between *us* and *them* is intensified until it seems to exhaust political reality; and an institution, a party, a state or an empire presents itself as the sole mediator, protector or saviour of the field it has helped to sever. Division happens below and a counterfeit unity is installed above, and the people who could have combined among themselves are combined only through the apparatus that divided them.

None of this makes the axis false. There are real differences of interest between employers and workers, between creditors and debtors, between those who own housing and those who rent it, between a colonising power and the people it governs, and an account that dissolved them into a vague common humanity would be the other collapse, cancellation, `(−1)+(+1)=0`, with the injustice summed away. Empire and subject, institution and citizen, rich and poor are unequal positions within one social field, and their inequality is a fact to be measured, not a misunderstanding to be talked away. What the collapsed logic does is make each of these positions appear self-sufficient, as though the rich were rich by nature and the poor poor by nature, as though institutions existed apart from the citizens whose labour and consent sustain them, so that the relation that reproduces the inequality disappears behind the inequality itself. Dia-ballein held measures the inequality exactly and keeps the relation that produces it in view. Dia-ballein collapsed measures the same inequality and reads it as a property of the people on each side.

So the literalist of the minus sign is not a monster at the edge of our institutions. He is a habit inside them, and inside us, the habit of reading a position as a substance and a zero as an ending. Every classification can do it and every verdict can be read that way. The jurors at the table are not literalists; they know that *not guilty* is not *innocent* and that the evening in the stairwell exceeds their two boxes. The newspaper that reports their verdict may be, and the employer who reads the record years later very likely is. Between the jury room and the employer's desk, the same cut has passed from a held polarity into a collapsed one, and nothing in the cut itself has changed. What changed is whether the axis it was made on, the field it came from, and the question it actually answered still travelled with it.

That is the condition under which dia-ballein stays itself, and it is also the hinge to the other logic. A cut that travels with its axis is already half way to a fitting, because it carries, in its own edge, the shape of what it was cut from. That edge is where the next movement goes. Myth has given the cut a body too, a god who separates, contests, refuses and defends, and who will meet his counterpart in a bed made by a craftsman; but before Ares can be read, the logic that throws together has to be stated in its own terms, as the retained seam of the symbolon and the trust it was made to carry.
