### #4 · Zero Keeps One Foot Outside Mathematics

Sooner or later in a mathematics lesson somebody types one divided by zero into a calculator to see what will happen, and the class leans in. The screen says "Error", or "E", or on a newer machine "Math ERROR", and the teacher explains that you cannot divide by zero. Someone asks why not, and the honest answer takes longer than the lesson has left. The calculator has not failed. It has reported, in the only word its display has for the purpose, that the question it was asked has no answer under the rules it was built to follow, and the class has met, without anyone saying so, the place where a system of calculation has to declare what its rules are.

The rules can be stated exactly. Take an ordinary field, the kind of number system the class uses every day, in which there are addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by anything other than zero, and in which zero and one are different numbers. Dividing one by a number $b$ means finding the number $q$ with $b\cdot q=1$, so dividing one by zero would mean finding a $q$ with

$$
0\cdot q=1.
$$

The difficulty is not that such a $q$ is hard to find. The laws of the system rule it out, whatever $q$ is. By the distributive law,

$$
0\cdot q=(0+0)\cdot q=0\cdot q+0\cdot q,
$$

and subtracting $0\cdot q$ from both sides leaves $0\cdot q=0$, for every $q$ in the system. To ask that $0\cdot q$ also equal one is to ask that zero equal one, which the system was built to deny, and adding some new element $q$ while keeping the same laws would not help, because the argument applies to the new element too. Zero divided by zero fails differently. Every $q$ satisfies $0\cdot q=0$, so the question has too many answers and the rule of division cannot pick one. A nonzero number divided by zero has no answer, and zero divided by zero has no single answer, and the word "undefined", which covers both, hides the two different reasons. Brahmagupta's rule that zero divided by zero is zero, which we met in the verses of 628, picked one answer out of the many the question admits, and later arithmetic declined to make that choice.

At **#1** we promised a derivation of Kaplan's remark that dividing by zero would make all numbers the same. Suppose we insisted on giving zero an inverse anyway, keeping all the other laws of the system and adding a number $z$ with $0\cdot z=1$. Then for any number $a$ at all,

$$
a=a\cdot1=a\cdot(0\cdot z)=(a\cdot0)\cdot z=0\cdot z=1,
$$

so every number equals one; and since $0$ is a number, $0=1$ as well. The whole system collapses into a single element, which is at once zero and one and every other number. Mathematicians know this object as the zero ring, the trivial ring with one element, and it is what one obtains, for instance, by the construction of fractions when zero is allowed among the denominators.[^58] Kaplan's remark that if one really could divide by zero all numbers would be the same is therefore exact: dividing by zero without changing any other law identifies everything with everything.

The zero ring is the precise image of a collapse that has run through this essay from its opening pages. In the zero ring, the One and the All coincide without remainder: there is one element, and it is every number, so that unity and multiplicity have been identified by abolishing the difference between them. That is fusion, the loss of the seam by merger, which the symbolon was made to resist, since a token whose halves had melted into one piece could no longer prove anything by their fit. The ordinary field takes the other course. It refuses division by zero, and in refusing keeps every number distinct from every other; the price is that the determinate one can never return to the zero by the operation of division, since `1/0` has no value. This refusal is dia-ballein doing its proper work. It cuts, it keeps the difference between zero and one exact, and it states the cut as a law, so that every calculation can proceed knowing where the edge is. The essay's notation asks for neither collapse. `0/1 = 1/0`, in the relational register the threshold established, is not an equation between two numbers and does not assign `1/0` a value in a field; it says that one relation can be read from either end, with the difference between the defined and the undefined retained as the seam through which the two readings meet. Ordinary arithmetic keeps the seam by refusing to cross it, and the zero ring loses the seam by crossing it without remainder. QL keeps the seam and crosses it as a relation, and the question to ask of any proposal, mathematical or otherwise, is the one the symbolon taught us: where did the seam go? Into a refusal that preserves difference by forbidding return, into a merger that permits return by abolishing difference, or into a relation that holds both?

Between those two extremes mathematics has built a series of systems that give division by zero a meaning by changing something else, and each shows the frame becoming visible at zero. The real projective line adds a single point at infinity to the numbers, so that a nonzero number divided by zero can be assigned that point, at the price that the point at infinity cannot itself be added to infinity or multiplied by zero in a way that keeps the familiar laws.[^59] The complex numbers, completed in the same way, become a sphere, the Riemann sphere, on which the plane of complex numbers wraps around until all its far directions meet at a single point at the top, so that zero and infinity become the two poles of one globe, and the map that sends every number to its reciprocal turns the sphere upside down, exchanging zero and infinity, and is defined everywhere.[^60] What has become total there is a map of points; division as an operation still fails when both of its parts vanish. Further systems go further. In the algebraic structures called wheels, division by zero is always defined, and the price is that some familiar laws, such as $x-x=0$, hold only in a weakened form.[^61] In the structures called meadows, every number has an inverse and zero is its own inverse, so that $0^{-1}=0$, and the price is that multiplying a number by its inverse gives one only when the number is not zero.[^62] Each system keeps some of the old laws and gives up others, and none of them abolishes the difficulty; each relocates it, and states where it has gone.

The point at infinity has a history that returns us to the painter's room. Perspective, as the threshold followed it through Gebser, organises a picture around a vanishing point, the empty place on the horizon toward which parallel lines in the scene converge; railway tracks that never meet on the ground meet there on the canvas. In 1639 the architect and engineer Girard Desargues took the painters' practice into geometry and proposed that parallel lines should be treated as meeting at a point at infinity, one such point for each direction, so that any two lines in a plane meet somewhere and the theorems of geometry no longer need exceptions for parallels.[^63] Projective geometry grew from that proposal, and the projective line on which a nonzero number divided by zero has a value is its descendant. The vanishing point of the painters became the mathematicians' point at infinity, and the point at infinity became the place where `1/0` could be put. Rotman's three meta-signs, zero, the vanishing point and imaginary money, meet here in a single construction: the empty place on the horizon, which organises a picture while holding nothing, is the place where the quotient by zero lands once the space has been enlarged to receive it. The threshold found the knower made into a place at the near end of Alberti's pyramid. Projective geometry found the far end, the vanishing point, and gave it the value of the forbidden quotient.

This is the precise sense in which zero keeps one foot outside mathematics. Zero is wholly mathematical: it is written, counted, operated on, and indispensable to every positional notation and every formal construction of number. Yet the operation that returns a determinate quantity to zero, division, cannot be completed without choosing or changing the space in which completion is sought, and so the attempt exposes the commitments of the whole system. A calculation that meets zero in its denominator forces the system to say what it is: a field, which refuses; a projective line, which adds a point; a wheel or a meadow, which changes its laws. "Outside" here means frame-disclosing. Nothing supernatural is meant; the edge is mathematical through and through, and at it every determinate account of number shows what it had presupposed.

Our own machines have had to make this choice explicitly, because a computer that divides by zero cannot ask a teacher what to do. The standard for floating-point arithmetic that governs almost every processor now built, IEEE 754, gives division by zero a definite law.[^64] It provides two zeros, a positive and a negative, which compare as equal but remember the direction from which a calculation arrived at zero: one divided by positive zero is positive infinity, one divided by negative zero is negative infinity, and the processor raises a flag to record that a division by zero occurred. Zero divided by zero, which has no single answer, gives a special value called NaN, "not a number", and NaN has a property no number has: it is not equal to itself, so that a program which asks whether NaN equals NaN is told that it does not. William Kahan, the principal architect of the standard, defended the signed zero in a paper whose subtitle is "Much Ado About Nothing's Sign Bit", and the phrase is exact, since the whole design turns on letting nothing carry a sign.[^65]

Read through the threshold's notation, the signed zero is a remarkable invention. The healthy polarity of dia-ballein, `(−1)/(+1)`, holds two signed quantities on one axis through a zero that connects them and gives each its direction. In ordinary arithmetic that zero has no sign of its own; it is the point where the axis turns. IEEE 754 writes two zeros, one for each side, and so lets the axis remember, at the very point of its turning, from which pole a quantity approached. The memory matters exactly when the quantity tries to return through zero: divide by the zero and the result runs off to the infinity on the side from which one came. The machine has written the chirality of the approach into nothing itself, so that the return through zero keeps the orientation of the journey, much as a loop on the torus, which the threshold followed, comes home to its starting point while its winding records which way it went round. NaN gives the other limit a sign. Frege's zero, we saw in the previous movement, was the number of the concept "not identical with itself", the count of what fails to equal itself, of which there is nothing; the machine's NaN is the one value that does fail to equal itself, the result of asking zero to divide zero. Frege derived the first number from the impossibility of self-difference, and the engineers marked the impossibility of the empty quotient with a value that actually differs from itself. The echo carries no proof and implies no line of influence; it shows two careful constructions, a century apart, both reaching for self-non-identity at the edge where nothing meets nothing.

Anyone who has trained a neural network knows what happens when that edge is reached by accident. The loss, the number by which training measures how wrong the model's predictions are, is plotted as it falls over hours or days; then a single division by a quantity that has shrunk to zero, or a logarithm of zero somewhere in the arithmetic, produces a NaN, and because any calculation involving NaN yields NaN, the corruption spreads through the model's parameters within a few steps, and the run is lost. Engineers protect against it by keeping a small number between every denominator and zero. The optimiser used to train most large models, Adam, divides each update by the square root of a running average and adds to that square root a constant written ε, set by default to $10^{-8}$, so that the denominator can approach zero but never reach it; normalisation layers inside the models add a similar ε, by default on the order of $10^{-5}$, before taking a square root.[^66] These constants are design decisions at the edge the calculator displayed as "Error": each declares a law for what happens near zero, choosing to keep a distance from the zero instead of reaching it, and every large model in use today carries thousands of them in its arithmetic. The machines that now reconstruct language from number depend, at countless points, on a deliberate refusal to let a quantity return all the way to nothing.

Calculus met the same edge three centuries earlier, and its history shows the foot outside mathematics being brought in by a change of frame. Newton and Leibniz, the same Leibniz who would read the binary digits as an image of creation, both computed the rate of change of a quantity as the ratio of two increments that are allowed to shrink until they vanish, so that a derivative looks like a quotient of two quantities that have become zero, nothing divided by nothing. The method worked, and nobody could say what the vanishing increments were. In 1734 the philosopher and bishop George Berkeley published *The Analyst*, whose subtitle addresses it to "an infidel mathematician" and asks whether the objects, principles and inferences of the modern analysis are more distinctly conceived or more evidently deduced than the mysteries of religious faith. The increments, he observed, are "neither finite Quantities nor Quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the Ghosts of departed Quantities?"[^67] 

The phrase has outlived the argument, but the argument was precise. A quantity that is divided by and then set equal to nothing has been treated as something and as nothing in one calculation, and the method's success did not make the treatment coherent. Berkeley was asking the mathematicians to admit that their certainty rested on a trust they could not certify, the same trust Russell would later want mathematics to replace and the threshold traced through Gödel's theorems. The answer took more than a century. Augustin-Louis Cauchy in the 1820s and Karl Weierstrass after him rebuilt the calculus on limits, so that the derivative is no longer a ratio of vanished quantities but the value a ratio approaches as its increment shrinks, and the division by zero is never performed; the definition they made standard asks that for every small positive quantity, traditionally written ε, a corresponding closeness can be found, and the engineers of the optimiser, following a convention that descends from that definition, use the same letter for the small constant they keep in their denominator.[^68] Kṛṣṇa's argument about the khahara, which we met beside Bhāskara's algebra, had reasoned in the same direction centuries earlier: the smaller the divisor, the greater the quotient, and no specified quotient is the greatest, so the endless quantity is approached through a process and is never reached as a value. In 1966 Abraham Robinson showed that infinitesimals, nonzero quantities smaller than every positive real number, could be given a consistent meaning in an enlarged number system, where the increment is never zero, the division is legitimate, and the standard part of the result recovers the derivative.[^69] For a real $x$ and a nonzero infinitesimal $\varepsilon$,

$$
\frac{(x+\varepsilon)^{2}-x^{2}}{\varepsilon}=2x+\varepsilon,
$$

and taking the standard part gives $2x$, the derivative of $x^{2}$. Substituting zero for the increment before dividing would give $0/0$. Robinson's construction, as Kaplan puts it in effect, turned the ghosts into well-founded fictions: zero, which had begun as a punctuator in a Babylonian column, became through calculus the limit that the most powerful method of modern science approaches without ever reaching.[^70]

Calculus has a further lesson that §3 takes up in its derivation of the eight determinations. Differentiating a quantity yields its rate of change and loses the constant added to it, so that integrating the derivative gives back not one quantity but a family, differing by an arbitrary constant that the derivative cannot recover.[^71] The local rate is exact, and it does not contain the condition from which its exactness was taken. That is the same structure in another material: a determination can be exact without containing the whole of what it presupposes, and the presupposed condition reappears as what the determination cannot reconstruct. The theorem spine writes this at the fifth determination as `∞/dx`, the unbounded field entering local change; here it is enough to see it as one more case in which the return toward zero discloses the frame.

The threshold named the gap that recurs at every such edge. The immutable gap, we said after following Gödel, is the non-coincidence between what a theory can express and what it can establish, which relocates with every enlargement of the theory and returns through it, so that it was never one withheld fact awaiting discovery. Division by zero is the mathematical form of that gap at the level of number, and the history we have just followed shows it relocating exactly as Gödel's did. The field refuses `1/0`; the projective line adds a point and then cannot divide infinity by infinity; the wheel defines every division and loses part of $x-x=0$; Robinson's infinitesimals make the derivative a true quotient and must then take a standard part to return to the real numbers. Each enlargement of the frame makes the old impossibility possible and produces a new one, because the operation that returns a determinate quantity to its zero always asks the system to account for the condition of its own computation, and that condition cannot be one more of its numbers. The local field-theoretic result is a proof, and its warrant is mathematical. The identification of this frame-disclosing edge with the immutable gap is the essay's own argued claim, and the boundary between the two is the one the threshold set for Gödel: the mathematics does not prove our notation; it gives the return of the determinate one upon its ground an exact form in one register, and shows that form recurring through every attempt to remove it.

The same structure has a psychic history, which §4 follows. Psychoanalysis did for the unconscious what arithmetic did for zero: it admitted into a science a term for what consciousness does not contain, made it operative, gave it rules of interpretation, and then found, at the edge where consciousness tries to account for the condition of its own activity, that the term could not be mastered as one more content. Freud's admission and Jung's widening of it have their own objects, methods and evidence, and the parallel with zero is a parallel of operations, not of things: a determinate system admits a sign for what it cannot contain, gains great power by operating with it, and at its edge is made to disclose its own frame. The essay holds the two histories apart until §4 can bring them together through the psychoid, where Jung and Pauli located the meeting of psyche and physis in number.

The calculator's "Error" looked like a dead end, the point past which there is nothing to be said. Followed through the field, the zero ring, the projective line, the sphere, the wheel and the meadow, the floating-point standard, the training run and the calculus, the edge turns out to be among the most informative places in mathematics: the place where a system shows what it assumes, what it protects and what it has chosen to give up. Zero, which entered arithmetic as an empty place and then as a number with rules, ends its mathematical history as the term at which every number system is made to declare itself. A sign that can do that has earned the right to stand for the condition the threshold borrowed it to name, and the loan can now be returned.
