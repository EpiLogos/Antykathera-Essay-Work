### #1 · Śūnya Becomes Operational

In 628, at Bhillamāla in western India, the astronomer Brahmagupta finished a long treatise in Sanskrit verse, the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, the "correctly established doctrine of Brahmā", whose business was the motions of the planets and the computation of eclipses and whose eighteenth chapter, on algebra, contains a small run of verses that changed what a number could be.[^19] His pupils would not have read those verses so much as learned them, line by line, until the rules could be recited in the middle of a calculation the way a craftsman reaches for a tool without looking. They are rules for three kinds of quantity. Brahmagupta called positive quantities *dhana*, fortunes or assets, and negative quantities *ṛṇa*, debts, and beside these two he set a third, *śūnya*, the empty, and stated what happens whenever any of the three meets another.[^20] Fortune and debt are words from the household and the market, the vocabulary of what one has and what one owes, and the rules read in part like the bookkeeping of a careful merchant: a debt taken from nothing becomes a fortune, a fortune taken from nothing becomes a debt, and a fortune added to an equal debt leaves nothing at all.

Henry Thomas Colebrooke's English translation of 1817 renders the rules for addition and multiplication as follows: "The sum of cipher and negative is negative; of affirmative and nought is positive; of two ciphers is cipher. [...] The product of cipher and negative, or of cipher and affirmative, is nought; of two ciphers, is cipher."[^21] Colebrooke's "cipher" and "nought" both translate *śūnya*, and his "affirmative" and "negative" render the words for fortune and debt. The verses can be followed with numbers on a page. Add nothing to seven and seven remains; add nothing to a debt of seven and the debt remains; multiply seven by nothing, or a debt of seven by nothing, and nothing is left. With these two rules zero stopped being a mark for a vacant position and became something arithmetic must pass through, a quantity with definite effects on the quantities it meets. That is what we mean by saying that *śūnya* became operational. The sign gained, in Brahmagupta's verses, the permissions of a number.

The two rules already show zero acting in two quite different ways, and the difference will carry much of this section. In addition zero is an identity: it leaves the other quantity exactly as it was, so that seven plus nothing is still recognisably seven. In multiplication zero absorbs: it sends every quantity to the same result, so that six times nothing and seventeen times nothing are both nothing,

$$
6\cdot0=0=17\cdot0,
$$

and the result keeps no trace of which number went in. Six and seventeen remain different numbers; the product has simply stopped recording the difference. We met a smaller version of this at the threshold, with Kripke's dice. An account that kept only the total of a throw lost the difference between die A showing one and die B showing two and the reverse, though the two throws were different states of the pair, and we said then that the account had changed what it retains: the total answers one question exactly and leaves another unanswered. Multiplication by zero is the extreme case. It retains nothing of its input, and a reader who sees only the product cannot recover what was multiplied. Kaplan makes the consequence vivid: since $6\cdot0=17\cdot0$, anyone who tried to cancel the common zero, as one cancels a common factor of three, would conclude that six equals seventeen.[^22] The prohibition on cancelling zero is the first wall the arithmetic of zero runs into, and at **#4** we shall find the same wall standing in every number system that has tried to remove it.

The vocabulary of fortune and debt lets us see something else in Brahmagupta's verses, which bears directly on the logics the threshold distinguished. The sum of a positive and a negative, the first verse says in Colebrooke's English, is "their difference; or, if they be equal, nought."[^23] A fortune added to an equal debt leaves nothing, and that rule is exactly the first collapsed operation of dia-ballein, written as law in the seventh century:

$$
(-1)+(+1)=0.
$$

A debt and an equal fortune cancel, and the zero they produce is the zero of a settled account, in which nothing is owing on either side and nothing of what passed between the parties remains in the balance. Brahmagupta's subtraction rules give the second collapsed operation, appropriation, in both its chiralities. "When affirmative is to be subtracted from negative, and negative from affirmative," the verse says, "they must be thrown together":[^24] a debt subtracted from a fortune yields their combined magnitude as a fortune, and a fortune subtracted from a debt yields it as a debt,

$$
(+1)-(-1)=+2,\qquad(-1)-(+1)=-2,
$$

so that the whole span between the two quantities is counted to one side, and the side that receives it is fixed by the pole from which the reckoning begins. Colebrooke's phrase "thrown together" has an irony the translator could not have intended. The Greek for throwing together is *syn-ballein*, the verb behind the symbolon whose halves keep their difference so that their fit can be recognised; what the rule throws together is two magnitudes fused into one sum, in which the two signed quantities are no longer distinguishable at all. The same English words name the fit that keeps a relation and the sum that dissolves it, and the difference between them is the difference between the two logics. I do not suggest that Brahmagupta was writing a treatise on the two logics; he was giving correct rules for signed quantities, and they remain correct. What his vocabulary shows is that the arithmetic of signed quantity was, at its origin, the arithmetic of the ledger, of what is had and what is owed, and that the zero which arithmetic first learned to compute with was the zero of cancellation, the zero at which a fortune and a debt annihilate each other. The polar axis itself, `(−1)/(+1)`, with zero as the point that relates the two signs and gives each its direction, is present too, since a fortune is a fortune only relative to the zero from which a debt runs the other way. Brahmagupta's verses therefore hold, side by side, the healthy polarity through zero and its two collapsed operations, at the very moment zero entered calculation. Whether a culture reads the zero of its accounts as the axis that relates its terms or as the nothing to which their collision reduces them becomes, from here on, one of the questions this history carries.

The ledger stayed with the numerals when they travelled. Fibonacci's manual, as we saw, taught Indian reckoning to merchants, and in 1494 the Franciscan mathematician Luca Pacioli included in his *Summa de arithmetica* the first printed account of double-entry bookkeeping, the Venetian method in which every transaction is entered twice, once as a debit and once as a credit, so that the books balance when the two columns sum to the same total.[^25] The balance of a double-entry ledger is Brahmagupta's rule at the scale of a whole enterprise: every fortune is matched by a debt, and when the books are closed the difference between them is nought. The threshold followed what this arithmetic can conceal. A balanced account answers exactly how much remains owing and says nothing of what passed between the parties, and the chartered companies whose books were kept this way could count the whole span of every exchange from their own side. The zero at the foot of a balanced ledger is the zero of cancellation, and the modern economy has been built on its reliability. It is a real achievement, and it is the reading of zero that the rest of this history has to complicate, since the same sign, followed to its edge, turns out to hold something no ledger can balance.

Division is where the verses run into trouble, and Brahmagupta faced the trouble without flinching. Division asks for a return: twelve divided by three is four because four multiplied by three gives back twelve, so a quotient answers to the multiplication that would undo it. Replace the three by zero and the question becomes what can be multiplied by zero to give back twelve, and multiplication by zero has already made that question unanswerable, because every candidate multiplied by zero gives zero. Brahmagupta stated two rules here. "Cipher, divided by cipher, is nought," runs Colebrooke's translation, and "positive, or negative, divided by cipher, is a fraction with that for denominator", a form the commentators called *tac-cheda*, "having that for its denominator".[^26] Later mathematics would reject the rule for zero over zero, since every number multiplied by zero gives zero and so no single number answers the question it poses, and would refuse to evaluate the other. What matters for our history is that Brahmagupta did not refuse the question. He gave the empty divisor a rule, and in giving it he committed arithmetic to saying something about the one operation that zero makes impossible to reverse.

Five centuries later Bhāskara II, the most celebrated of the medieval Indian mathematicians, took the problem up in two books. His *Līlāvatī*, a book of arithmetic written in verse and addressed throughout to a reader whom it keeps questioning, gives the rules for zero in two couplets, and one of them answers the difficulty about multiplication that we have just met. The product of zero is nought, Bhāskara says in Colebrooke's English, "but it must be retained as a multiple of cipher, if any further operation impend. Cipher having become a multiplier, should nought afterwards become a divisor, the definite quantity must be understood to be unchanged."[^27] Multiplication by zero destroys the record of what was multiplied, so Bhāskara keeps the record: a number multiplied by zero is written as that number with a zero beside it, held in suspense, and if a later step divides by zero, the two zeros cancel and the number returns. The example that follows puts the reader to the test. What number, multiplied by zero, added to half itself, multiplied by three and divided by zero, gives sixty-three? The answer is fourteen, since fourteen and its half make twenty-one and three times twenty-one is sixty-three, once the zero that multiplied and the zero that divided have been allowed to cancel; and the edition records that the method was in frequent use in astronomical calculation.[^28] Ordinary arithmetic cannot do this, because it computes the product at once and then has nothing left to divide. Bhāskara's arithmetic postpones the computation, keeps the zero as a marked operation and not as a finished value, and so lets a quantity pass through zero and come back. It is a small, practical instance of what this essay calls retention: the relation is kept through the moment at which an ordinary account would have closed it.

His *Bījagaṇita*, the "seed-computation" or algebra, which the Sanskrit tradition dates to 1150, returns to the zero denominator itself.[^29] His rule keeps the expression instead of evaluating it: in Colebrooke's English, "a quantity, divided by cipher, becomes a fraction the denominator of which is cipher", and he gives the fraction a name, *khahara*, the quantity whose divisor is *kha*, the empty space.[^30] Then he says something that has no counterpart in any modern textbook. Of this quantity with zero for its divisor, he writes, "there is no alteration, though many be inserted or extracted; as no change takes place in the infinite and immutable God, at the period of the destruction or creation of worlds, though numerous orders of beings are absorbed or put forth."[^31]

The image asks the reader to perform a definite act of thought. Ordinarily adding a quantity to another makes the second larger and taking one away makes it smaller; that is what it means for both to be quantities on one scale. Bhāskara's expression with zero in its denominator does not change when finite quantities are added to it or taken from it, and he finds the model for that invariance in God, who is neither enlarged by the creation of worlds nor diminished by their destruction, though whole orders of beings come forth from God and return. In modern terms the khahara behaves like an infinite quantity, which a finite addition cannot change. In Bhāskara's terms it behaves like the divine, of which creation is not a part added to a whole. Both readings are in the passage, and neither is incidental to the other. The mathematical rule makes a claim about an expression's behaviour under addition and subtraction; the theological comparison tells the reader how to conceive that behaviour, by setting the changing many against a unity they cannot measure. The image does not prove the arithmetic, and the arithmetic does not establish a doctrine of God; what the passage shows is a mathematician, at the moment the mathematics of zero reached its edge, reaching for the language of the source in which worlds arise and are reabsorbed.

The threshold arrived at the same relation from the other side. Whitehead's formula for the creative advance, we said there, is that the many become one and are increased by one, and what grows is the many: each new occasion is one more, while the One that `0` writes is neither added to nor diminished, the uncarved block staying one while every carving joins the many. Bhāskara's God, unaltered through the putting forth and absorbing of worlds, is the same relation read in the language of twelfth-century Indian cosmology, and his khahara is the place where that relation touched arithmetic. The older Indian scriptures had stated the relation in an arithmetic of their own. A verse of the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*, recited still as an invocation, says: *pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idam pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate / pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate*, "That is full; this is full; from the full the full comes forth; when the full is taken from the full, the full alone remains."[^32] The verse is not a theorem about infinite quantities, and Bhāskara does not cite it. It shows, though, that the culture in which the zero denominator received its theological image had long possessed a way of speaking of a whole from which wholes come forth without its diminution, a whole whose arithmetic is not the arithmetic of parts. In Bhāskara the language of that whole and the language of a calculation met in a single rule.

Bhāskara's later commentators argued about what kind of quantity the khahara is, and their notes, which Colebrooke translated alongside the text, show them thinking toward the infinite with great care. The illustration in the *Bījagaṇita* itself calls the fraction "an infinite quantity", *ananta-rāśi*, the endless quantity. One commentator, Gaṇeśa, calls it indefinite, unlimited, or infinite, "since it cannot be determined how great it is"; another, Ranganātha, reasons that the smaller the divisor the greater the quotient, so that zero, "being in the utmost degree small, gives a quotient infinitely great"; and Kṛṣṇa adds the decisive step: "if it can be specified, that the amount of the quotient is so much, it has not been raised to the utmost: for a quantity greater than that can be assigned."[^33] The last sentence is an argument that the infinite cannot be any specified quantity, because whatever quantity is specified, a greater can be named. It reasons from the shrinking divisor toward a quotient that grows without bound, which is the reasoning European calculus would later make rigorous as a limit, and it concludes that the endless quantity is beyond every determination that could be made of it. Kṛṣṇa's infinite stands to every specified quotient as the Subject stood, at the threshold, to every specified description: whatever is determined falls short of it, and falls short precisely because it is determined.

Modern readers have not always known what to do with the meeting. Kaplan, after showing correctly that cancelling a common zero would make all numbers equal, goes on to explain the Indian treatment of the zero denominator by a cultural disposition, as though the mathematicians had been led astray by their metaphysics.[^34] The explanation does not hold up. The mathematician Amartya Kumar Dutta has reconstructed the Indian rules for zero-divided expressions as a coherent regime with its own restrictions: such expressions are retained and carried through further operations, while the familiar moves of cancellation and cross-multiplication are not permitted for them, and their evaluation can be postponed until the computation has removed the zero from the denominator.[^35] Bhāskara's suspended zero-multiple in the *Līlāvatī*, cancelled by a later division, is a case of exactly that postponement. On this reading the Indian mathematicians were not making a modern mistake; they were working under laws different from those of a modern field, and the laws can be stated. What Kaplan's cancellation argument shows is that a zero denominator cannot be treated as an ordinary quotient without collapsing the distinctions between all numbers. What Dutta's reconstruction shows is that a zero denominator can be kept, as a written object under special rules, without that collapse. The two findings do not contradict each other. Together they make the demand this section presses at **#4**: judge an operation under the laws it actually claims, and state where those laws differ from the ones being used to judge it.

Kaplan's page holds a sentence that I read, the first time, as more than a warning. If one really could divide by zero, it says in effect, all numbers would be the same. The mathematics is exact, and **#4** derives it. But the remark also states, without intending to, the wager of the whole essay in negative form. A register in which all numbers were the same would be a register in which multiplicity had collapsed into oneness; arithmetic forbids division by zero precisely to prevent that collapse, and so arithmetic is not the register in which the many can be seen as one without ceasing to be many. Bhāskara's image points to a register in which they can: the divine whole, unchanged as worlds come forth and return, holds the many as its own manifestation without being reduced to their sum. Number alone could not hold both, and the history of zero is in part the history of mathematics discovering, at its own edge, that it needs a further symbolic register to say what its prohibitions protect. That register is what `0/1` will write.

The chronology locates this meeting more exactly. The *Bījagaṇita* is dated to 1150. Abhinavagupta, the philosopher who gave Kashmir Śaivism its fullest systematic form, is conventionally placed between about 975 and 1025, so that roughly a century and a quarter separates his death from Bhāskara's algebra.[^36] Abhinavagupta's tradition gave the most developed account in Indian philosophy of a consciousness that is the source of all manifestation and is not changed by manifesting. In it, as the threshold set out through Dyczkowski's exposition, *prakāśa* is the light of consciousness, the plain fact of appearing, and *vimarśa* its reflexive awareness of itself, so that whatever appears and the power through which it appears belong to one self-articulating whole; and Śiva and Śakti, the Subject in its speakable aspect and its power to differentiate and take on identities, stand as Mono and Poly, one source and its many manifestations. §0 has followed that tradition's anatomy of how a single awareness contracts into the finite knower and its world. Bhāskara was an astronomer working in a different discipline, in another region, and there is no evidence that he knew Abhinavagupta's works or that his algebra drew on them, and we claim no such line of influence. The year 1150 is not the date of zero's invention either, since Brahmagupta's rules are five centuries older.

What the dates establish is a civilisational braid. Within one intellectual world, over little more than a century, a philosophical school gave exact form to the relation between an unchanging source and its many manifestations, and a mathematical tradition, meeting the zero denominator, described its behaviour in the image of the same relation. The two practices had different objects and different methods; the Śaiva work is metaphysical, phenomenological and soteriological, Bhāskara's is computational and astronomical. Yet both had made the relation between finite determination and an exceeding source thinkable in exact and non-identical ways, and their co-presence in one civilisation is the historical fact on which our return of zero rests. Jung gave a name to conjunctions of this kind, meaningful in their correspondence and connected by no cause that can be traced, and the name was synchronicity.[^37] The term is apt here in its strict sense: the Śaiva doctrine did not cause the algebra, and the algebra did not cause the doctrine, and their meeting in one century and one culture is meaningful because each discloses, in its own register, the relation the other states. Gebser's co-presence of structures gives the same fact its methodological form. Different modes of knowing can remain operative together without one becoming the hidden explanation of the other, and their difference is precisely what lets them illuminate each other.

That is why this section reads the zero's history as having moved from metaphysics into number and, later, from number back into ontology. In the Indian setting the sign was born inside a language where the empty was the condition of appearing, and it acquired its first arithmetic rules in a culture that could describe its strangest behaviour in the image of the unchanging source. When the sign travelled west it carried the rules and left the image. The rules made the sign universal; the image stayed home. The return of zero, as this essay pursues it, brings back what stayed home, now with everything the rules have since made exact, and it can do so honestly only because both the rules and the image were present, separately and together, in the history itself.

The rules also teach something about trust. Brahmagupta's pupils learned the verses before they could have proved them, and they used them to compute eclipses whose timing could then be checked against the sky. The rules were received on the authority of a teacher and a text and relied on in practice, and only gradually, as the calculations kept succeeding, did the reliance become knowledge of why they worked. We called that prior reliance *fides* at the threshold, the trust already enacted before any arbitration, and we said that knowledge opens an aperture through which the dependence can be understood without dissolving it. The arithmetic of zero is a clear case. Nobody who first used the rule "fortune plus equal debt is nothing" had derived it from axioms; the axioms came more than a thousand years later, and they were chosen so that the rules would follow. The trust came first and the warrant was built to fit it, and the warrant, once built, also revealed where the trust had gone wrong, in the rule that zero divided by zero is zero. Examination changed what the tradition was prepared to entrust, without making the original reliance a mistake.

The verses established something a calculating machine depends on. A rule stated precisely enough can be followed by someone who does not understand what the quantities mean, and the following will still be correct. A pupil who has memorised "the product of cipher and affirmative is nought" can multiply by zero correctly without any view about the nature of emptiness, and in that independence of correct procedure from understanding lies the condition of all mechanical computation. The algorithms that carry al-Khwārizmī's name, and whose arithmetic a language model performs billions of times for every word it produces, are rules of this kind: they deliver correct results to an executor who need not know what the results are about. Brahmagupta's verses are among the first written rules that let zero be handled that way, and they also show the cost. The rule for zero divided by zero was followed as faithfully as the others, and it was wrong, and nothing in the procedure could detect that it was wrong. The error had to be found by someone asking what the rule meant.

By the twelfth century, then, zero had been placed, named, given arithmetic rules and pressed to the edge where division fails, and at that edge an Indian mathematician had set it beside the image of the unchanging source of worlds. The next step in its history is formal, and it happened in Europe at the end of the nineteenth century and the beginning of the twentieth, when logicians asked whether number itself could be built from nothing, and found that the answer was yes, provided one counted the nothing.
