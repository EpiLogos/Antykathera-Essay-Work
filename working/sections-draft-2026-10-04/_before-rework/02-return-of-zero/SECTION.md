## §1 — The Return of Zero

<a id="M13"></a>

### #0 · A Sign Migrates Between Worlds

A child at the kitchen table is asked to write the number one hundred and five, and she writes 1005. She has written exactly what she heard: a hundred, which she already knows looks like 100, and then a five. The parent at her elbow sees the mistake at once and finds it unexpectedly hard to explain. The hundred, the explanation goes, already has its places inside it, and the five has to go into the last of them, so the middle zero is no longer the hundred's zero at all; it stands for the tens, and there are no tens. Then why write anything there? Because without it the 1 slides into the tens' place, 105 becomes 15, and a hundred has shrunk to a ten without anyone touching it. She goes back to the page with a new respect for the small circle, and perhaps with a suspicion that the adults have been keeping something from her. Most of us made some version of that mistake once, and the research on how children learn to transcode spoken numbers into digits finds it among the commonest errors of the early school years.[^1] Some of us have since sat at the elbow of the one making it.

What the child meets is a sign for no counted thing that changes what is counted. The zero in 105 adds nothing to the sum, since there are no tens to add, and yet the hundred is worth a hundred only because that zero keeps it in the third place from the right. Take the zero out and every mark to its left changes value, while nothing has been added or removed. The circle counts nothing and positions everything, and a child who has understood this has understood the decisive event this section follows: absence ceased to be a blank outside the count and became a position within it. The writing of numbers that we learn as children rests on that event, and so does the arithmetic every calculating machine performs.

By the following year the girl will not see the zero at all. She will read 105 at a glance, as adults do, and the work the small circle performs will have passed out of her attention and into the way she reads. Zero has become so effective that its intervention is almost invisible. The threshold gave a name to what has happened to her. Objective Internality, we said there, is the means through which a life knows and acts within a world, and a received rule becomes part of it when it turns into a condition of receiving again: in a human life a remembered rebuke can shape how an innocent question is heard, and here a rule learned at a kitchen table shapes how every later number is seen. Place value has moved from the page into the reader. It is objective, since she could still state it if asked, and internal, since she now reads through it without looking at it. Much of the history that follows is the history of that movement in reverse, the slow conversion of what readers of numbers carried silently in themselves into marks that could be written down and sent to readers who did not share their training, and the child's year of learning reruns in miniature what took the species two and a half thousand years.

Our first section closed on a promise. We had followed counting into the layer beneath naming, the *this one*, *another one*, *this one again* by which two parents keep hold of an unnamed child, and we had written `0` for the pole that cannot enter its own count as one more item, the knowing to which every counted thing appears. We said then that our work meant to echo zero's own advent, making the uncounted condition explicit within the count. A promise of that kind is a loan. The threshold borrowed zero as a sign for the Subject before it had shown where the sign came from or what it could carry, and the account cannot close until the sign's history has been told and the loan repaid. §0 has meanwhile followed awareness as it differentiates into the means through which it knows and the world it knows through them, and every determination it traced was selected from a field it did not make and could not exhaust. A determination of that kind always leaves a zero behind it, the field from which it was cut and the act that cut it. Before §2 can distinguish the two ways two determinate terms can be related, we have to recover that zero in the place where human beings first learned to write it down, the history of number.

That history has to be told with some care, because what we now call zero gathered several distinct inventions into one sign, and each was made for a different problem in a different practice. A blank space can keep a place in a column. A separator can mark an empty position inside a written number. A digit can stand among the other digits as one more figure in a positional system. A number can enter arithmetic and obey rules of addition and multiplication. An empty set can serve as the first object of a formal construction, and an origin can fix the point from which coordinates are measured. Each of these is a different office, a different permission granted to a mark by the practice that receives it, and the circle the child writes in 105 now performs several of them at once. They arrived separately, across roughly two and a half thousand years and at least four civilisations, and the sign became powerful precisely because it migrated, gathering offices as it crossed from one practice to another. If we call all of them "zero" from the start, we lose the history that made the sign what it is; if we keep them apart, we can watch absence acquire its powers one by one.

The story begins in Mesopotamia, where scribes wrote numbers in base sixty with a wedge-shaped stylus on wet clay. Their system was positional, as ours is: the same sign could stand for one, for sixty, or for three thousand six hundred, according to where it stood in the number. Old Babylonian scribes of the early second millennium BCE had no sign for an empty place. Where a position was vacant they might leave a little more space, or nothing at all, and a reader had to infer the value from the context of the calculation, which usually made it clear. A modern transcription shows the difficulty. Write the places as a sequence, and $(1,0,1)$ means

$$
1\times60^{2}+0\times60+1=3601,
$$

while $(1,1)$ means $1\times60+1=61$; if the scribe leaves no mark for the empty middle place, the two numbers can look identical on the tablet, and only the problem being solved tells the reader which was meant. (The example is an illustration of the positional difficulty, not a transcription of a particular tablet.) In the last centuries BCE, scribes in the Seleucid period wrote a separator, two small slanting wedges, where a place inside a number was empty.[^2] It was a real achievement and a limited one. The separator stood only between other signs, never at the end of a number, so the scale of the whole number, whether a figure meant 1 or 60 or 1/60, still depended on context; and nobody added it, multiplied by it, or treated it as a quantity in its own right. It held a place open, and that was all it did.

Read through what the first section taught us, the scribe's difficulty is the difficulty of context itself. A mark whose value depends on position needs a reader who knows what the position is, and for a thousand years Babylonian mathematics left that knowledge in the reader: the context of the calculation did the work a written zero would later do. The separator moved a little of that context onto the tablet. In the terms of the threshold's sixfold, a fraction of what had been held at **#4**, the whole situation in which a mark is read, was written down as a mark, so that a reader who did not share the scribe's situation could still recover the value. Much of what follows in this history repeats that move: something a practitioner had carried tacitly, in memory and circumstance, becomes a sign that travels without its practitioner.

Not every number system needs a zero, and seeing why makes the history of zero a history of position. Roman numerals add their signs together: CCLXX is a hundred and a hundred and fifty and ten and ten, and each sign means the same wherever it stands, apart from the convention that a smaller sign written before a larger is subtracted from it. A system of that kind has no places, so it has no empty places, and it has nothing for a zero to do; a Roman merchant who wanted to write two hundred and seventy wrote what was there and left out what was not, and nothing he left out could change the value of what he wrote. The absence of zero in such a system is no failure of imagination. It follows from the design, in which the value of a mark is fixed by its shape and not by its position. Only when value depends on position does an absence acquire consequences, since in a positional system what is not there decides what everything else is worth. That is why zero belongs to the history of place-value notation and to no other: it is the sign a positional system needs in order to say that a place exists and holds nothing.

Greek mathematics achieved its geometry, its theory of proportion and its proofs of the infinitude of primes without positional notation and without a zero. Greek numerals were written with letters, each letter with a fixed value, so the problem of an empty place never arose in ordinary arithmetic. Hellenistic astronomers, who inherited Babylonian sexagesimal fractions for their tables of angles and times, met the problem again in that setting. In Ptolemy's *Almagest* of the second century CE and in the astronomical papyri around it, a small circle, often written with a bar above it, marks an entry in a table where there are no degrees, or no minutes, in a measurement that still has other parts.[^3] The circle is closer to punctuation than to a number: it says that a measure of the specified kind is absent, so that a reader can tell "no minutes" from a missing or damaged entry. Several origins have been proposed for the sign, and none is settled. The question "did the Greeks have zero?" turns out to be badly posed. They had a mark for an absent measure inside a table, which is one of zero's later offices, and they did not have a number zero that could enter calculation, which is another; asking for one verdict about both erases exactly the difference this history needs.

The decisive transformation took place in India, and it took place in language before it took place in arithmetic. By the time Āryabhaṭa composed his *Āryabhaṭīya* in 499 CE, Indian astronomers were working with a decimal place-value system, in which each place is worth ten times the place to its right, and Āryabhaṭa describes the places explicitly.[^4] Indian mathematicians wrote their works in verse, to be memorised and recited, and to fit numbers into metre they used words in place of digits: a word for the moon, of which there is one, could stand for 1; a word for the eyes or the hands for 2; and so on through a large conventional vocabulary. The words chosen for zero are telling. They are words for the sky, for space, for the open and the empty: *kha*, the hollow or aperture; *ākāśa*, the space in which things appear; *ambara*, the sky; and *śūnya*, the empty.[^5] A word for space could name the absence of a quantity because absence was already intelligible as the openness within which something might be placed. A later commentator on Bhāskara's algebra, Kṛṣṇa, glossed the terms exactly, and Colebrooke translated his gloss in a note: *śūnya*, *kha* and the other words are "synonyma of vacuum or etherial space", meaning "nought or cipher; a blank or the privation of specific quantity".[^6] The phrase is precise. Zero is the privation of *specific* quantity, the absence of this or that amount, and a privation of that kind leaves the space in which amounts appear exactly where it was. Space does not grow larger when an object enters it, nor smaller when the object leaves, and a vocabulary that could think of emptiness in that way was ready to give a column with nothing in it a name of its own.

*Śūnya* carries the deepest of these word-worlds. The adjective means empty or void, and its abstract noun, *śūnyatā*, emptiness, had already become, centuries before Āryabhaṭa, the central term of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka philosophy. For Nāgārjuna, writing around the second century CE, emptiness is not nothingness: whatever arises in dependence on conditions is empty of any self-standing nature, and that emptiness is itself dependent designation, the middle way between asserting things as substances and denying them altogether.[^7] To call a thing empty in this sense is to say that it is constituted by relations it does not contain, so emptiness turns out to be the condition under which anything at all can appear and function. This is the same lesson the threshold drew from apoha, where a word's meaning lives by what it excludes, and from the tetralemma, whose fourth corner, NEITHER, refused both verdicts and exposed the act that had produced them. The word that would become the name of the mathematical zero was already, in the philosophical literature of the same civilisation, the name of the relational emptiness in which determinations arise. No text tells us that a mathematician chose *śūnya* because of Nāgārjuna, and we make no such claim; what the history shows is that the sign entered calculation carrying a word that its culture had already used to think about the ground of all determination.

The written dot and circle followed, and the surviving inscriptions give the history some dates it can hold. A stone inscription from Sambor on the Mekong, in present-day Cambodia, dated in the Śaka era to the year 605, which is 683 CE, writes its date with a dot for the zero, and it is the earliest securely dated zero in the Indian family of numerals.[^8] At Gwalior, in a small temple inscription of 876 CE, the number 270 is written with a hollow circle in the place that we still use.[^9] The Bakhshali manuscript, a birch-bark mathematical text found near Peshawar in 1881, uses a dot for zero throughout; radiocarbon dating announced by the Bodleian Library in 2017 assigned some of its leaves to as early as the third or fourth century, and five specialists in Indian mathematics replied that the earliest-dated leaf cannot date a manuscript copied later, and that its zeros already work as numbers in calculation, not only as place-holders.[^10] We do not need the earliest date. What the dot and the circle show is that by the eighth and ninth centuries a sign for the empty place was in ordinary written use across a wide area, as one digit among ten, and that it travelled with the other nine.

By then the sign had acquired an office the Babylonian separator never had. Brahmagupta, writing in 628, gave rules for how zero behaves when it meets positive and negative quantities in addition, subtraction and multiplication, so that zero became a quantity arithmetic must reckon with and no longer only a mark for a vacant place.[^11] The next movement follows those rules, and the strange theological image Bhāskara set beside one of them five centuries later, at the point where they meet the zero denominator. The order of events matters here. In India the history ran from a philosophical and cosmological vocabulary of emptiness, through a positional notation, into explicit arithmetic rules, and the sign arrived in the count still carrying the name of the openness in which counting happens. This is what I mean by saying that zero came into mathematics from metaphysics. The claim is not that a philosophical doctrine caused the arithmetic; it is that the mathematical sign was born inside a language where the empty was already a positive term, the condition of appearance, and that the sign's later history in the West would strip that term away and then, at the limits of formal reason, be forced to recover it.

From India the numerals moved west through the scholars of Baghdad. Around 825 CE, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī wrote a treatise explaining the Indian method of reckoning with nine figures and a zero, and that treatise survives only in a Latin translation of the twelfth century, which opens *Dixit Algorizmi*, "Algorizmi says".[^12] The scribes who copied it turned the author's name into a word for the method itself, and from *algorismus*, the art of reckoning with Indian figures, comes our *algorithm*. Every procedure a computer now executes, and the whole vocabulary in which we describe what artificial intelligence does, carries in its name the memory of a ninth-century scholar who wrote to teach the use of zero. Arabic writers called the empty place *ṣifr*, the empty, rendering into Arabic the meaning of the Sanskrit *śūnya*. In Latin the word became *cifra*, and later *zephirum*, and from these two routes English received both *cipher* and *zero*: *zephirum* was shortened in Italian to *zefiro* and *zero*, while *cipher* went on to mean a numeral in general, a person of no account, and a secret code.[^13] The doubled sense of *cipher* records how the sign looked from two sides. A sign that counts nothing and yet decides the value of everything around it looked, to those who did not know its rules, like a secret; to those who did, it was the key that opened every calculation.

Leonardo of Pisa, known later as Fibonacci, learned Indian reckoning as a boy in the customs house at Bugia in North Africa, where his father was posted for the merchants of Pisa, and in 1202 he published the *Liber Abaci*, the book that did most to bring the method into Latin Europe. Its first chapter sets the nine Indian figures before the reader, written in descending order, 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1, and then adds the tenth: *cum hoc signo 0, quod arabice zephirum appellatur*, "with this sign 0, which in Arabic is called *zephirum*", any number whatever can be written.[^14] The sentence keeps the nine figures and the zero apart. The nine are *figure*, figures; the zero is *signum*, a sign, and it is introduced as the thing that lets the nine do their work, since without it no figure could be held in its place. What follows is a manual for merchants: how to write numbers, how to add and multiply them, how to convert weights and currencies, how to share profits among partners. The sign entered European life through the counting house, in the service of exchange, and the long competition between calculation on counting boards with movable counters and calculation written out with pen and ink on paper was settled over the following centuries by commercial practice more than by philosophy. In 1503 Gregor Reisch's encyclopaedia for students, the *Margarita philosophica*, printed a woodcut of that competition: Arithmetic stands as a woman between two calculators, one working a counting board with counters, the other writing Indian figures with a pen, and the figure with the pen is shown as the one at ease while the man at the board looks on, as the image is usually read, in some dismay.[^15] On the counting board the empty place had always been visible as a line with no counters on it, and a calculator who used one never needed a sign for nothing, since the board itself held the places. Writing the calculation out required the sign, because paper keeps no places of its own. The zero was the price of leaving the board behind, and what the pen-reckoner gained by paying it was a calculation that could be checked afterwards, copied into a ledger and carried away, since every step stayed on the page.

A familiar story adds that medieval Christians feared zero as a diabolical sign and that authorities banned the new numerals for that reason. The historian Philipp Nothaft has examined that story and argues that the evidence for a demonised zero and for general bans is thin, and that the narrative was largely constructed by modern writers.[^16] Particular restrictions on figures in account books, where a 0 could be altered into a 6 or a 9, belong to the history of fraud prevention in specific places, and they do not show a culture afraid of nothing. The correction matters to an essay about the diabolical, because the myth of the satanic cipher is itself an act of *dia-ballein* in the oldest sense of the word, throwing an accusation across a distance: a modern account sets itself up as enlightened by charging an earlier culture with superstition, and the charge has travelled more readily than the evidence. A reception story about a sign needs a source as much as the sign does, and here the received story turns out to be the less trustworthy of the two.

The sign's arrival in Europe coincided with a transformation that the threshold has already described from the side of painting. In the same centuries in which European merchants learned to write with zero, painters learned to construct a picture around the vanishing point, the empty place on the horizon toward which every line of a perspectival scene converges, and the place that organises the whole count of the scene while holding nothing. Brian Rotman, in *Signifying Nothing*, argued that zero, the vanishing point and the imaginary money of early modern finance arrived together as three expressions of one semiotic change: each is a sign about other signs, a meta-sign that organises a whole system of signs from a position the system cannot occupy, and each brought with it a new kind of subject, the one who can stand outside the system and see it whole.[^17] The account brings our history to the same point our reading of Gebser reached through Alberti's pyramid, where the eye at the apex is the knower made into a place. Rotman's meta-subject and our Subject are different things, and the difference has to be kept. For Rotman the subject who reads zero is produced by the signifying practice, a position the system of signs creates for its user; for our argument the Subject is the condition under which any such position can appear at all, and no description of how a position is produced exhausts the knowing in which the description is understood. The two accounts meet at the same sign and divide over what the sign implies, and our notation, which writes the Subject as `0` and its produced positions as determinations in the `1`, can hold Rotman's insight without accepting his conclusion.

Migration bought the sign an extraordinary freedom. A Muslim astronomer in Baghdad, a Christian merchant in Pisa and a Hindu temple official in Gwalior did not share a metaphysics, and they did not need to share one in order to agree that 270 plus 30 is 300. A calculation could pass between people who disagreed about God, about the nature of space and about the meaning of emptiness, and they could check one another's work by following the steps. This independence of the sign from any compulsory metaphysics is one of the greatest achievements in the history of human reason, and it is the condition of everything modern science and computation have done with number. Something was also given up in the passage. The word *śūnya*, which in its home language named the emptiness in which all things arise, travelled as *ṣifr* and *cifra* and *zero*, words that meant only the empty place; the philosophical life of the term did not accompany the figure into the counting houses of Pisa, because the figure did not need it to work. I call that loss the first advent's sacrifice: mathematical universality was purchased by leaving behind the symbolic world in which the sign had been meaningful. The sacrifice was necessary, and it was real. Neither half of that sentence can be dropped. A history that only mourned the loss would miss what exactness made possible, and a history that only celebrated the gain could not explain why, many centuries later, the exact sign returns at the limits of formal reason asking to be read again.

Our own time is watching the sign migrate once more. A large language model does not receive numbers as a child does, digit by digit in their places; it receives text cut into tokens, fragments of characters chosen because they recur often in the data on which the model was trained, and a number such as 1005 may arrive as one token, or two, or four, depending on how the tokeniser happens to divide it. Place value, the thing the child at the kitchen table had to learn, is therefore not given to the model in its input at all. It has to be reconstructed from patterns in the training text, and researchers have shown that frontier models do arithmetic measurably better or worse depending on whether the digits of a number are grouped from the left or from the right, that is, depending on whether the token boundaries respect the places.[^18] The receiving practice decides, again, what the sign is allowed to do. A model that writes 1005 for one hundred and five has made the child's mistake for a different reason: the child wrote what she heard, while the model wrote what its tokens let it see. Each new receiving practice, the Babylonian tablet, the Greek table, the Sanskrit verse, the Latin manual and the tokeniser, grants the sign some offices and withholds others, and to understand what a mark can do we have to ask each time what its receiver permits.

Seen whole, the migration gives the threshold's question a historical body. We asked there how thought can mark the condition it cannot count as one more object, and history answers that human beings learned, slowly and in several places, to put absence in a position: first as a gap the reader filled from context, then as a separator, a circle in a table, a word for the sky, a dot in a row of digits and a figure in a merchant's manual. At each stage the sign took over work that a practitioner had previously done in silence, so that what had been carried tacitly in the reader's situation became a mark that could travel without the reader. Read this way, zero is a sign whose whole history is the writing-down of context, the gradual conversion of what a calculation presupposes into something the calculation can contain, and the history will end, at the formal limit, by showing that the conversion can never be completed, because the act that writes the context down always stands in a further context of its own. The invented system makes this history legible as its own prehistory; nobody who wrote a dot for zero in a Sanskrit manuscript was anticipating `0/1`, and none of them needed to be for the sign they handed on to carry the relation we are recovering.

The decisive turn, though, has not yet been told. A place that holds nothing open is still only a place. Zero becomes the sign this essay needs when arithmetic can pass through it, when a calculation can add zero, multiply by zero and, at last, try to divide by zero, and when someone has to say, in verse and with full seriousness, what happens then.

### #1 · Śūnya Becomes Operational

In 628, at Bhillamāla in western India, the astronomer Brahmagupta finished a long treatise in Sanskrit verse, the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, the "correctly established doctrine of Brahmā", whose business was the motions of the planets and the computation of eclipses and whose eighteenth chapter, on algebra, contains a small run of verses that changed what a number could be.[^19] His pupils would not have read those verses so much as learned them, line by line, until the rules could be recited in the middle of a calculation the way a craftsman reaches for a tool without looking. They are rules for three kinds of quantity. Brahmagupta called positive quantities *dhana*, fortunes or assets, and negative quantities *ṛṇa*, debts, and beside these two he set a third, *śūnya*, the empty, and stated what happens whenever any of the three meets another.[^20] Fortune and debt are words from the household and the market, the vocabulary of what one has and what one owes, and the rules read in part like the bookkeeping of a careful merchant: a debt taken from nothing becomes a fortune, a fortune taken from nothing becomes a debt, and a fortune added to an equal debt leaves nothing at all.

Henry Thomas Colebrooke's English translation of 1817 renders the rules for addition and multiplication as follows: "The sum of cipher and negative is negative; of affirmative and nought is positive; of two ciphers is cipher. [...] The product of cipher and negative, or of cipher and affirmative, is nought; of two ciphers, is cipher."[^21] Colebrooke's "cipher" and "nought" both translate *śūnya*, and his "affirmative" and "negative" render the words for fortune and debt. The verses can be followed with numbers on a page. Add nothing to seven and seven remains; add nothing to a debt of seven and the debt remains; multiply seven by nothing, or a debt of seven by nothing, and nothing is left. With these two rules zero stopped being a mark for a vacant position and became something arithmetic must pass through, a quantity with definite effects on the quantities it meets. That is what we mean by saying that *śūnya* became operational. The sign gained, in Brahmagupta's verses, the permissions of a number.

The two rules already show zero acting in two quite different ways, and the difference will carry much of this section. In addition zero is an identity: it leaves the other quantity exactly as it was, so that seven plus nothing is still recognisably seven. In multiplication zero absorbs: it sends every quantity to the same result, so that six times nothing and seventeen times nothing are both nothing,

$$
6\cdot0=0=17\cdot0,
$$

and the result keeps no trace of which number went in. Six and seventeen remain different numbers; the product has simply stopped recording the difference. We met a smaller version of this at the threshold, with Kripke's dice. An account that kept only the total of a throw lost the difference between die A showing one and die B showing two and the reverse, though the two throws were different states of the pair, and we said then that the account had changed what it retains: the total answers one question exactly and leaves another unanswered. Multiplication by zero is the extreme case. It retains nothing of its input, and a reader who sees only the product cannot recover what was multiplied. Kaplan makes the consequence vivid: since $6\cdot0=17\cdot0$, anyone who tried to cancel the common zero, as one cancels a common factor of three, would conclude that six equals seventeen.[^22] The prohibition on cancelling zero is the first wall the arithmetic of zero runs into, and at **#4** we shall find the same wall standing in every number system that has tried to remove it.

The vocabulary of fortune and debt lets us see something else in Brahmagupta's verses, which bears directly on the logics the threshold distinguished. The sum of a positive and a negative, the first verse says in Colebrooke's English, is "their difference; or, if they be equal, nought."[^23] A fortune added to an equal debt leaves nothing, and that rule is exactly the first collapsed operation of dia-ballein, written as law in the seventh century:

$$
(-1)+(+1)=0.
$$

A debt and an equal fortune cancel, and the zero they produce is the zero of a settled account, in which nothing is owing on either side and nothing of what passed between the parties remains in the balance. Brahmagupta's subtraction rules give the second collapsed operation, appropriation, in both its chiralities. "When affirmative is to be subtracted from negative, and negative from affirmative," the verse says, "they must be thrown together":[^24] a debt subtracted from a fortune yields their combined magnitude as a fortune, and a fortune subtracted from a debt yields it as a debt,

$$
(+1)-(-1)=+2,\qquad(-1)-(+1)=-2,
$$

so that the whole span between the two quantities is counted to one side, and the side that receives it is fixed by the pole from which the reckoning begins. Colebrooke's phrase "thrown together" has an irony the translator could not have intended. The Greek for throwing together is *syn-ballein*, the verb behind the symbolon whose halves keep their difference so that their fit can be recognised; what the rule throws together is two magnitudes fused into one sum, in which the two signed quantities are no longer distinguishable at all. The same English words name the fit that keeps a relation and the sum that dissolves it, and the difference between them is the difference between the two logics. I do not suggest that Brahmagupta was writing a treatise on the two logics; he was giving correct rules for signed quantities, and they remain correct. What his vocabulary shows is that the arithmetic of signed quantity was, at its origin, the arithmetic of the ledger, of what is had and what is owed, and that the zero which arithmetic first learned to compute with was the zero of cancellation, the zero at which a fortune and a debt annihilate each other. The polar axis itself, `(−1)/(+1)`, with zero as the point that relates the two signs and gives each its direction, is present too, since a fortune is a fortune only relative to the zero from which a debt runs the other way. Brahmagupta's verses therefore hold, side by side, the healthy polarity through zero and its two collapsed operations, at the very moment zero entered calculation. Whether a culture reads the zero of its accounts as the axis that relates its terms or as the nothing to which their collision reduces them becomes, from here on, one of the questions this history carries.

The ledger stayed with the numerals when they travelled. Fibonacci's manual, as we saw, taught Indian reckoning to merchants, and in 1494 the Franciscan mathematician Luca Pacioli included in his *Summa de arithmetica* the first printed account of double-entry bookkeeping, the Venetian method in which every transaction is entered twice, once as a debit and once as a credit, so that the books balance when the two columns sum to the same total.[^25] The balance of a double-entry ledger is Brahmagupta's rule at the scale of a whole enterprise: every fortune is matched by a debt, and when the books are closed the difference between them is nought. The threshold followed what this arithmetic can conceal. A balanced account answers exactly how much remains owing and says nothing of what passed between the parties, and the chartered companies whose books were kept this way could count the whole span of every exchange from their own side. The zero at the foot of a balanced ledger is the zero of cancellation, and the modern economy has been built on its reliability. It is a real achievement, and it is the reading of zero that the rest of this history has to complicate, since the same sign, followed to its edge, turns out to hold something no ledger can balance.

Division is where the verses run into trouble, and Brahmagupta faced the trouble without flinching. Division asks for a return: twelve divided by three is four because four multiplied by three gives back twelve, so a quotient answers to the multiplication that would undo it. Replace the three by zero and the question becomes what can be multiplied by zero to give back twelve, and multiplication by zero has already made that question unanswerable, because every candidate multiplied by zero gives zero. Brahmagupta stated two rules here. "Cipher, divided by cipher, is nought," runs Colebrooke's translation, and "positive, or negative, divided by cipher, is a fraction with that for denominator", a form the commentators called *tac-cheda*, "having that for its denominator".[^26] Later mathematics would reject the rule for zero over zero, since every number multiplied by zero gives zero and so no single number answers the question it poses, and would refuse to evaluate the other. What matters for our history is that Brahmagupta did not refuse the question. He gave the empty divisor a rule, and in giving it he committed arithmetic to saying something about the one operation that zero makes impossible to reverse.

Five centuries later Bhāskara II, the most celebrated of the medieval Indian mathematicians, took the problem up in two books. His *Līlāvatī*, a book of arithmetic written in verse and addressed throughout to a reader whom it keeps questioning, gives the rules for zero in two couplets, and one of them answers the difficulty about multiplication that we have just met. The product of zero is nought, Bhāskara says in Colebrooke's English, "but it must be retained as a multiple of cipher, if any further operation impend. Cipher having become a multiplier, should nought afterwards become a divisor, the definite quantity must be understood to be unchanged."[^27] Multiplication by zero destroys the record of what was multiplied, so Bhāskara keeps the record: a number multiplied by zero is written as that number with a zero beside it, held in suspense, and if a later step divides by zero, the two zeros cancel and the number returns. The example that follows puts the reader to the test. What number, multiplied by zero, added to half itself, multiplied by three and divided by zero, gives sixty-three? The answer is fourteen, since fourteen and its half make twenty-one and three times twenty-one is sixty-three, once the zero that multiplied and the zero that divided have been allowed to cancel; and the edition records that the method was in frequent use in astronomical calculation.[^28] Ordinary arithmetic cannot do this, because it computes the product at once and then has nothing left to divide. Bhāskara's arithmetic postpones the computation, keeps the zero as a marked operation and not as a finished value, and so lets a quantity pass through zero and come back. It is a small, practical instance of what this essay calls retention: the relation is kept through the moment at which an ordinary account would have closed it.

His *Bījagaṇita*, the "seed-computation" or algebra, which the Sanskrit tradition dates to 1150, returns to the zero denominator itself.[^29] His rule keeps the expression instead of evaluating it: in Colebrooke's English, "a quantity, divided by cipher, becomes a fraction the denominator of which is cipher", and he gives the fraction a name, *khahara*, the quantity whose divisor is *kha*, the empty space.[^30] Then he says something that has no counterpart in any modern textbook. Of this quantity with zero for its divisor, he writes, "there is no alteration, though many be inserted or extracted; as no change takes place in the infinite and immutable God, at the period of the destruction or creation of worlds, though numerous orders of beings are absorbed or put forth."[^31]

The image asks the reader to perform a definite act of thought. Ordinarily adding a quantity to another makes the second larger and taking one away makes it smaller; that is what it means for both to be quantities on one scale. Bhāskara's expression with zero in its denominator does not change when finite quantities are added to it or taken from it, and he finds the model for that invariance in God, who is neither enlarged by the creation of worlds nor diminished by their destruction, though whole orders of beings come forth from God and return. In modern terms the khahara behaves like an infinite quantity, which a finite addition cannot change. In Bhāskara's terms it behaves like the divine, of which creation is not a part added to a whole. Both readings are in the passage, and neither is incidental to the other. The mathematical rule makes a claim about an expression's behaviour under addition and subtraction; the theological comparison tells the reader how to conceive that behaviour, by setting the changing many against a unity they cannot measure. The image does not prove the arithmetic, and the arithmetic does not establish a doctrine of God; what the passage shows is a mathematician, at the moment the mathematics of zero reached its edge, reaching for the language of the source in which worlds arise and are reabsorbed.

The threshold arrived at the same relation from the other side. Whitehead's formula for the creative advance, we said there, is that the many become one and are increased by one, and what grows is the many: each new occasion is one more, while the One that `0` writes is neither added to nor diminished, the uncarved block staying one while every carving joins the many. Bhāskara's God, unaltered through the putting forth and absorbing of worlds, is the same relation read in the language of twelfth-century Indian cosmology, and his khahara is the place where that relation touched arithmetic. The older Indian scriptures had stated the relation in an arithmetic of their own. A verse of the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*, recited still as an invocation, says: *pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idam pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate / pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate*, "That is full; this is full; from the full the full comes forth; when the full is taken from the full, the full alone remains."[^32] The verse is not a theorem about infinite quantities, and Bhāskara does not cite it. It shows, though, that the culture in which the zero denominator received its theological image had long possessed a way of speaking of a whole from which wholes come forth without its diminution, a whole whose arithmetic is not the arithmetic of parts. In Bhāskara the language of that whole and the language of a calculation met in a single rule.

Bhāskara's later commentators argued about what kind of quantity the khahara is, and their notes, which Colebrooke translated alongside the text, show them thinking toward the infinite with great care. The illustration in the *Bījagaṇita* itself calls the fraction "an infinite quantity", *ananta-rāśi*, the endless quantity. One commentator, Gaṇeśa, calls it indefinite, unlimited, or infinite, "since it cannot be determined how great it is"; another, Ranganātha, reasons that the smaller the divisor the greater the quotient, so that zero, "being in the utmost degree small, gives a quotient infinitely great"; and Kṛṣṇa adds the decisive step: "if it can be specified, that the amount of the quotient is so much, it has not been raised to the utmost: for a quantity greater than that can be assigned."[^33] The last sentence is an argument that the infinite cannot be any specified quantity, because whatever quantity is specified, a greater can be named. It reasons from the shrinking divisor toward a quotient that grows without bound, which is the reasoning European calculus would later make rigorous as a limit, and it concludes that the endless quantity is beyond every determination that could be made of it. Kṛṣṇa's infinite stands to every specified quotient as the Subject stood, at the threshold, to every specified description: whatever is determined falls short of it, and falls short precisely because it is determined.

Modern readers have not always known what to do with the meeting. Kaplan, after showing correctly that cancelling a common zero would make all numbers equal, goes on to explain the Indian treatment of the zero denominator by a cultural disposition, as though the mathematicians had been led astray by their metaphysics.[^34] The explanation does not hold up. The mathematician Amartya Kumar Dutta has reconstructed the Indian rules for zero-divided expressions as a coherent regime with its own restrictions: such expressions are retained and carried through further operations, while the familiar moves of cancellation and cross-multiplication are not permitted for them, and their evaluation can be postponed until the computation has removed the zero from the denominator.[^35] Bhāskara's suspended zero-multiple in the *Līlāvatī*, cancelled by a later division, is a case of exactly that postponement. On this reading the Indian mathematicians were not making a modern mistake; they were working under laws different from those of a modern field, and the laws can be stated. What Kaplan's cancellation argument shows is that a zero denominator cannot be treated as an ordinary quotient without collapsing the distinctions between all numbers. What Dutta's reconstruction shows is that a zero denominator can be kept, as a written object under special rules, without that collapse. The two findings do not contradict each other. Together they make the demand this section presses at **#4**: judge an operation under the laws it actually claims, and state where those laws differ from the ones being used to judge it.

Kaplan's page holds a sentence that I read, the first time, as more than a warning. If one really could divide by zero, it says in effect, all numbers would be the same. The mathematics is exact, and **#4** derives it. But the remark also states, without intending to, the wager of the whole essay in negative form. A register in which all numbers were the same would be a register in which multiplicity had collapsed into oneness; arithmetic forbids division by zero precisely to prevent that collapse, and so arithmetic is not the register in which the many can be seen as one without ceasing to be many. Bhāskara's image points to a register in which they can: the divine whole, unchanged as worlds come forth and return, holds the many as its own manifestation without being reduced to their sum. Number alone could not hold both, and the history of zero is in part the history of mathematics discovering, at its own edge, that it needs a further symbolic register to say what its prohibitions protect. That register is what `0/1` will write.

The chronology locates this meeting more exactly. The *Bījagaṇita* is dated to 1150. Abhinavagupta, the philosopher who gave Kashmir Śaivism its fullest systematic form, is conventionally placed between about 975 and 1025, so that roughly a century and a quarter separates his death from Bhāskara's algebra.[^36] Abhinavagupta's tradition gave the most developed account in Indian philosophy of a consciousness that is the source of all manifestation and is not changed by manifesting. In it, as the threshold set out through Dyczkowski's exposition, *prakāśa* is the light of consciousness, the plain fact of appearing, and *vimarśa* its reflexive awareness of itself, so that whatever appears and the power through which it appears belong to one self-articulating whole; and Śiva and Śakti, the Subject in its speakable aspect and its power to differentiate and take on identities, stand as Mono and Poly, one source and its many manifestations. §0 has followed that tradition's anatomy of how a single awareness contracts into the finite knower and its world. Bhāskara was an astronomer working in a different discipline, in another region, and there is no evidence that he knew Abhinavagupta's works or that his algebra drew on them, and we claim no such line of influence. The year 1150 is not the date of zero's invention either, since Brahmagupta's rules are five centuries older.

What the dates establish is a civilisational braid. Within one intellectual world, over little more than a century, a philosophical school gave exact form to the relation between an unchanging source and its many manifestations, and a mathematical tradition, meeting the zero denominator, described its behaviour in the image of the same relation. The two practices had different objects and different methods; the Śaiva work is metaphysical, phenomenological and soteriological, Bhāskara's is computational and astronomical. Yet both had made the relation between finite determination and an exceeding source thinkable in exact and non-identical ways, and their co-presence in one civilisation is the historical fact on which our return of zero rests. Jung gave a name to conjunctions of this kind, meaningful in their correspondence and connected by no cause that can be traced, and the name was synchronicity.[^37] The term is apt here in its strict sense: the Śaiva doctrine did not cause the algebra, and the algebra did not cause the doctrine, and their meeting in one century and one culture is meaningful because each discloses, in its own register, the relation the other states. Gebser's co-presence of structures gives the same fact its methodological form. Different modes of knowing can remain operative together without one becoming the hidden explanation of the other, and their difference is precisely what lets them illuminate each other.

That is why this section reads the zero's history as having moved from metaphysics into number and, later, from number back into ontology. In the Indian setting the sign was born inside a language where the empty was the condition of appearing, and it acquired its first arithmetic rules in a culture that could describe its strangest behaviour in the image of the unchanging source. When the sign travelled west it carried the rules and left the image. The rules made the sign universal; the image stayed home. The return of zero, as this essay pursues it, brings back what stayed home, now with everything the rules have since made exact, and it can do so honestly only because both the rules and the image were present, separately and together, in the history itself.

The rules also teach something about trust. Brahmagupta's pupils learned the verses before they could have proved them, and they used them to compute eclipses whose timing could then be checked against the sky. The rules were received on the authority of a teacher and a text and relied on in practice, and only gradually, as the calculations kept succeeding, did the reliance become knowledge of why they worked. We called that prior reliance *fides* at the threshold, the trust already enacted before any arbitration, and we said that knowledge opens an aperture through which the dependence can be understood without dissolving it. The arithmetic of zero is a clear case. Nobody who first used the rule "fortune plus equal debt is nothing" had derived it from axioms; the axioms came more than a thousand years later, and they were chosen so that the rules would follow. The trust came first and the warrant was built to fit it, and the warrant, once built, also revealed where the trust had gone wrong, in the rule that zero divided by zero is zero. Examination changed what the tradition was prepared to entrust, without making the original reliance a mistake.

The verses established something a calculating machine depends on. A rule stated precisely enough can be followed by someone who does not understand what the quantities mean, and the following will still be correct. A pupil who has memorised "the product of cipher and affirmative is nought" can multiply by zero correctly without any view about the nature of emptiness, and in that independence of correct procedure from understanding lies the condition of all mechanical computation. The algorithms that carry al-Khwārizmī's name, and whose arithmetic a language model performs billions of times for every word it produces, are rules of this kind: they deliver correct results to an executor who need not know what the results are about. Brahmagupta's verses are among the first written rules that let zero be handled that way, and they also show the cost. The rule for zero divided by zero was followed as faithfully as the others, and it was wrong, and nothing in the procedure could detect that it was wrong. The error had to be found by someone asking what the rule meant.

By the twelfth century, then, zero had been placed, named, given arithmetic rules and pressed to the edge where division fails, and at that edge an Indian mathematician had set it beside the image of the unchanging source of worlds. The next step in its history is formal, and it happened in Europe at the end of the nineteenth century and the beginning of the twentieth, when logicians asked whether number itself could be built from nothing, and found that the answer was yes, provided one counted the nothing.

### #2 · The Empty Set Generates One

Anyone who has cleared out an old computer has met it. A folder sits on the desktop reporting that it contains one item; opened, it holds another folder, and that folder is empty. The outer folder is not empty, though there is nothing in it but an emptiness. Delete the inner folder and the outer one becomes empty too, and the count it reports drops from one to none. Nobody tidying a hard drive thinks this is philosophy, and yet the little dialogue box that says "1 item" has done something exact: it has counted an empty container as one thing, and in doing so made a one out of nothing but the act of putting nothing inside something.

Mathematicians write that act with two signs. An empty set, a collection with no members, is written $\varnothing$. A set whose only member is the empty set is written $\{\varnothing\}$, and it is not empty, since it has exactly one member:

$$
\varnothing\quad\longrightarrow\quad\{\varnothing\}.
$$

Nothing has been added to the empty set; a new relation has been formed around it, and the braces show exactly where the one appears. At the end of the nineteenth century and the beginning of the twentieth, logicians took a step of this kind as the beginning of the whole sequence of numbers. If one can be built from nothing by counting the nothing, two can be built from nothing and one, and so on without end, and the natural numbers need no material of their own beyond the empty set and the operation that gathers it.

Among the first to see this clearly was Gottlob Frege, whom the threshold met twice: once explaining why "the morning star is the evening star" tells us something that "a = a" does not, and once receiving Russell's letter about the catalogue of all catalogues that do not list themselves. In *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* of 1884, his attempt to say what numbers are without appeal to intuition or psychology, Frege defined a number as something that belongs to a concept: the number of moons of Jupiter belongs to the concept "moon of Jupiter", and two concepts have the same number when the things falling under them can be paired off one to one.[^38] The definition then needed a first number, and Frege found it in a concept under which nothing can fall. "Since nothing falls under the concept 'unequal to itself'," he wrote in §74, "I explain: 0 is the number which belongs to the concept 'unequal to itself'."[^39] He anticipated the objection that such a concept contains a contradiction, and answered it with some humour, recalling the old examples of wooden iron and the square circle: they are not as bad as they are made out to be, he said, since they do no harm so long as one does not suppose that anything falls under them, and all logic can ask of a concept is that it be sharply bounded, so that for every object it is settled whether the object falls under it. He also said why he had chosen this concept and not another. Any concept under which nothing falls would have served, but he wanted one whose emptiness could be proved by logic alone, and self-inequality offered itself most conveniently, since, taking Leibniz's definition of identity, it is purely logical that every object is equal to itself.[^40] Having zero, he defined one in §77 as the number belonging to the concept "equal to 0", under which exactly one object falls, namely zero itself; and each further number followed in the same way, by counting the numbers already obtained.[^41]

The choice repays attention, because it brings us back to the copula. At the threshold we wrote the two simplest marks of identity and difference, `=` and `≠`, and saw that the sign for non-equality is the sign for equality with the cut drawn through it. Frege's zero is the count of that cut turned on a thing and itself. It is the number of whatever is not equal to itself, and since nothing is, the count is zero, so the first number in Frege's arithmetic is the count of a self-relation that fails everywhere. His one is then the count of whatever is equal to that zero, and the only thing that is, is zero. In the order of Frege's construction, the first thing ever counted is zero itself: one is reached by counting the nothing that the failed self-difference produced. I take this to be more than an elegant trick of definition, and Frege's own reason for the choice shows why. He did not need self-inequality in particular; he needed an emptiness that logic could certify without looking at the world, and the one concept whose emptiness follows from logic alone is the failure of a thing to be identical with itself. Every other empty concept, the golden mountain or the present king of France, is empty because of how the world happens to be. Self-inequality is empty because of what identity is. Frege, looking for the logical ground of number, found that the first number had to be produced from the impossibility of a thing differing from itself, and that the first unit is the count of that impossibility's result. The identity that every object has with itself, which the threshold traced through the copula's double work, is the ground from which Frege's whole arithmetic was drawn: zero counts its failure, and one counts zero. Frege did not read his construction this way, and nothing in his logic requires it; the reading is mine, and it shows how close to the copula the foundations of arithmetic lie.

Frege's further ambition, to derive these concepts and their numbers from purely logical laws, ran into the paradox the threshold followed, since the principle he used to turn concepts into collections allowed Russell's self-defeating collection to form, and the second volume of his *Basic Laws* went to press with an appendix conceding that a foundation had given way. The idea that number could be built from nothing by counting survived the paradox, though, in the set theory that grew up to repair it. In 1923 John von Neumann, not yet twenty, gave it the form mathematics still uses. Each number, he proposed, is the set of all the numbers before it.[^42] Zero is the set of all numbers before zero, of which there are none, so zero is the empty set. One is the set whose only member is zero. Two is the set of zero and one, three the set of zero, one and two:

$$
0=\varnothing,\qquad 1=\{0\}=\{\varnothing\},\qquad 2=\{0,1\}=\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\},\qquad 3=\{0,1,2\}.
$$

Each number is generated from the one before by keeping all its members and adding the number itself as one further member. Written compactly, the successor of a number $n$ is

$$
S(n)=n\cup\{n\}.
$$

For zero there are no members to keep, so its successor is $\{0\}$, which is one. For one, the member kept is zero and the member added is one, which gives $\{0,1\}$, which is two. Every number holds all its predecessors as members, so that zero is a member of every number after it, and the sequence carries its beginning into every later stage.

Von Neumann's was not the only way to do this. Ernst Zermelo had earlier taken the successor of a set to be simply the set containing it, so that his numbers ran $\varnothing$, $\{\varnothing\}$, $\{\{\varnothing\}\}$, each holding only the one before; both constructions yield a sequence that behaves exactly as the natural numbers do.[^43] The comparison shows what kind of result we have. The empty set and the successor operation give a representation of the numbers, a way of building objects that do everything the numbers must do, and mathematics chooses among representations by what they make easy. Von Neumann's became standard because each of his numbers is literally the collection of the numbers it counts past, so that the number three is a set with three members, and the order of the numbers is the order of membership. The construction is exact, and it does not claim that numbers are sets in some deeper sense. It shows that the natural numbers can be generated from an empty beginning and one operation of gathering, and that is enough for our purpose.

The ordinal two invites comparison with the count the threshold made, and the comparison has to be exact. The threshold took the two single terms $0$ and $1$, related the binary to itself to obtain the four ordered pairings $00$, $01$, $10$ and $11$, and kept both levels together to reach $2+2^{2}=4+2$: six, the shape of a distinction that includes its own self-relation. Von Neumann's two is something else. It is the set $\{0,1\}$, whose two members are the two numbers before it, and it counts succession: two is what comes after one, and it holds everything that came before. The threshold's six is a count of self-application, and the ordinal sequence is a count of succession, and neither derivation can stand in for the other. Both, though, begin from the same pair, zero and one, and both find that the pair generates more than itself once it is held together instead of being treated as two separate items.

Read with a reverence it never asked for, the ordinal two is zero and one held together, $2=\{0,1\}$, and the ordinal one is the void gathered into a unit, $1=\{\varnothing\}$, a set whose entire content is the empty set it holds. The one is not a second thing standing beside the zero. It is the zero bound into a collection, the act of holding that makes the empty countable as one, and in that sense the first one in von Neumann's sequence is the mark of a relation to zero before it is anything else. The theorem spine of this essay names it the **symbolic linker**: the `1` that links an articulated field back to the ground it cannot contain.[^44] In the set-theoretic register the link is exact: one contains zero as its member and is distinct from it, and every later number carries zero inside it as the first of the members it has gathered.

Three relations that ordinary speech runs together under the word "contains" are kept apart here, and the construction works only because they stay apart. Zero is a member of one, since one is the set whose member is zero. Zero is also a subset of one, since every member of zero is a member of one, there being no members of zero to check; indeed the empty set is a subset of every set. And zero is not identical to one, since one has a member and zero has none. Membership, inclusion and identity do different work, and the sequence of numbers depends on all three: on membership to carry each number into the next, on inclusion to keep the earlier numbers inside the later, and on the difference between a set and its members to prevent the whole sequence from collapsing back into its beginning. Finite repetition of the successor reaches every finite number, though it does not by itself produce the collection of all of them; set theory needs a separate axiom, the axiom of infinity, to assert that such a collection exists. The beginning is generous, then, but not unlimited, and the point at which the finite sequence asks for an infinite completion is a point to which **#4** returns, where infinity enters arithmetic as the price of dividing by zero.

We have to be equally exact about what the construction does not do. The empty set is an object of mathematics, defined within a set theory, and as exact as any object there is. The Subject the threshold defined is not another empty object, and our `0` does not denote the empty set. When we write the ordinal construction and follow it, the set, the braces, the rule and the act of following the rule are all available for examination, and each examination is a knowing; representing that knowing in turn adds a further object to what is examined and does not make the knowing a member of any set. The empty set and the unobjectifiable Subject therefore belong to different levels, and conflating them would destroy precisely the distinction the construction helps us see. What the construction gives us is a formal neighbour to the relation the essay has derived: an exact case in which a beginning that is nothing remains present in everything generated from it, and in which the first determinate unit is the act of holding that beginning. Mathematics supplies the case under its own laws; it does not prove the essay's claim, and the essay does not need it to. The case shows that the relation `0/1` writes is not foreign to mathematics. Number itself, built with the greatest care modern logic could bring to it, begins with an empty set and the one that holds it.

The same section of Kaplan's history sets beside the ordinals two other beginnings from nothing, each under a different law.[^45] An empty sum, a sum of no terms at all, is defined to be zero, because adding zero leaves any sum as it was: if one adds a list of numbers and then appends no further numbers, the total must not change. An empty product, a product of no factors, is defined to be one, for the same reason: appending no further factors must leave a product unchanged, and the number that leaves products unchanged is one. The difference is instructive. Multiplying by zero gives zero, since a zero factor absorbs everything, while multiplying by no factor gives one, since an absent factor changes nothing; ordinary speech calls both "multiplying by nothing", and mathematics asks what was actually done. In these constructions absence has no value of its own. It takes its value from the operation that holds it, and the value it takes is that operation's identity, the element that leaves every other element identical with itself.

Mathematics calls zero the additive identity, and the name repays attention. The identity element of an operation is the one element that leaves every other element as it is; it is the operation's own preservation of sameness, the place where acting changes nothing. In addition, that place is held by zero. The threshold wrote `0` for the Subject because the Subject is self-identity, the One that remains itself through every determination without becoming one of them, and here the arithmetic word for zero names the same office in its own register: zero is that which, added to anything, lets the thing remain itself. The same word, identity, names the logical relation of a thing to itself, the arithmetic element that preserves every sum, and the office our notation gives the uncounted pole. The coincidence of terms carries a real connection, because in each case identity is what is present in every operation without altering what the operation acts on. That arithmetic also assigns the identity of multiplication to one, and makes the empty product one, shows the same relation from the other side: where the operation is gathering many factors into one product, its identity is the unit, the one that every product already includes.

Kaplan's third scene changes the material again, from number to logic.[^46] In the logic of true and false, take a single operation, "neither … nor", and write it as an arrow pointing down, so that $p\downarrow q$ is true exactly when both $p$ and $q$ are false:

$$
p\downarrow q=\neg(p\lor q).
$$

Apply it to the same proposition twice, "neither $p$ nor $p$", and the result is true exactly when $p$ is false, so the operation has produced negation. Deny the denial and disjunction appears; deny the two separate denials and conjunction appears:

$$
\neg p=p\downarrow p,\qquad p\lor q=(p\downarrow q)\downarrow(p\downarrow q),\qquad p\land q=(p\downarrow p)\downarrow(q\downarrow q).
$$

With negation, conjunction and disjunction in hand, every truth function of any finite number of propositions can be built, by writing for each combination of inputs on which the function should be true a conjunction that selects just that combination, and joining the selected combinations with "or". Repeated "neither … nor" therefore generates the whole of propositional logic. Charles Sanders Peirce saw this around 1880, in a manuscript that remained unpublished until long after his death; Henry Sheffer proved in 1913 that a single connective, written with a stroke, could serve as the one primitive of Boolean algebra; and Wittgenstein made joint denial the operation from which, in the *Tractatus*, every proposition is generated from the elementary propositions.[^47] What had been two or three primitive operations of logic turned out to need only one, and that one is a double refusal.

The generator is worth reading against what the threshold found in exclusion. Apoha made a word determinate by what it excludes: *cow* is understood as *not non-cow*, so the double negative carries the word's whole work of gathering its instances. The tetralemma's fourth corner, NEITHER, refused both verdicts and exposed the act that had produced them. Here a logic is built entirely out of refusal: every assertion that can be compounded from simple propositions can be reached by saying "neither this nor that" often enough and in the right arrangement. Kaplan reads the reduction as the key to Wittgenstein's whole book: if every proposition is built from joint denials, then what language states is reached by way of what it rules out, and what it cannot state can only be shown by the direction in which its statements point.[^48] That reading of the *Tractatus* is Kaplan's, and the threshold met Wittgenstein's own distinction between saying and showing in his words; the formal fact beneath it is exact, and it gives the tetralemma's NEITHER and apoha's exclusion a precise neighbour in the logic of truth values. A whole field of affirmation can be generated from denial, provided the denials are arranged, and the arrangement does the work that no single denial can.

The boundary matters as much as the result. Functional completeness concerns truth functions, the ways the values true and false can be combined. The slash of `0/1` relates a determination to a condition that is not one more input with a truth value, and no Boolean connective, however complete, can be that slash, since every connective takes determinate values and returns a determinate value. The neighbour is close enough to teach, though. Sheffer's primitive was written as a stroke between two letters, and the stroke between two terms is the oldest notation in this essay's history. "A Hair perhaps divides the False and True", runs a line of Edward FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát*, and Kaplan quotes it at this point in his book;[^49] the image fits Sheffer's stroke exactly, a hairline mark from which the difference between true and false can be generated in every combination. Our slash is the same thin mark asked to do something further: to divide and join at once, and to hold the relation between the false and true on one side and the condition of their being known on the other.

Every digital machine now built rests on this generator. A single kind of logic gate, a circuit that outputs true only when both its inputs are false, or its twin that outputs false only when both inputs are true, is enough to build any digital circuit whatever, and engineers have built whole computers that way: the designers of the Apollo Guidance Computer that flew to the Moon chose a single integrated circuit, a three-input NOR gate, as the only logic element of the machine, and its first version used some four thousand of them.[^50] The machine that navigated a spacecraft between the Earth and the Moon computed everything it computed by arranging one refusal, "neither this nor that nor the other", thousands of times over. The chips on which a language model runs are not built from a single gate type, but everything they compute could be, and every number the model handles, including the numbers that represent its words, reduces in the end to arrangements of a repeated refusal. The threshold said that a civilisation built with zeroes has not thereby learned what its zero excludes. Here is the material form of that sentence. The whole of the machine's affirmation is generated from "neither … nor", applied to determinate values, and the condition under which any of those values means something to anyone is not among the values the machine combines.

Three constructions, then, each a beginning from almost nothing under its own law. The ordinal construction builds the numbers from the empty set by gathering it, and keeps every earlier number inside every later one. The empty sum and the empty product take their values from the identities of their operations, and show that absence is never simply absent: it is valued by the operation that holds it. Joint denial builds the whole of logic from refusal, and shows that an entire field of affirmation can be generated from what it excludes. They are not three versions of one theorem, and their kinship becomes useful only once they have been distinguished. What they share is a pattern the threshold anticipated: a spare beginning becomes generative through an operation that retains it, so that what is generated carries its beginning inside it. In each, the beginning is something mathematics treats as an object, the empty set, the identity element, the false value, and in each the generative power lies in the operation and in what it keeps.

One can now serve as a linker: a determinate unit through which a relation to zero is held and carried forward into everything that follows. Its power depends on what it links. The next difficulty arises when the one forgets that it is a holding, when the singleton that holds the void takes itself for the source of its own unity, and the mark of a relation passes for the thing related. That forgetting has a sign of its own, and its history is shorter than zero's and stranger.

### #3 · The Crossed Zero

Someone is kneeling behind the sofa with a torch, reading the network password off the sticker on the back of a router, and a friend is typing it in on the other side of the room. Halfway through they stop. Is that a zero or the letter O? The sticker's typeface draws both as the same tall oval, and the password will be refused if they guess wrong, so they try one and then the other, and the second attempt connects. Most of us have stood in some version of that room. The difficulty is old in the history of writing machines: once letters and digits came to share one keyboard, one printer and one line of code, a written nothing could be mistaken for a sound, and the people who wrote programs by hand on coding sheets for punch-card operators, and the designers of the typefaces on early terminals, often settled it by striking the zero through with a diagonal stroke, so that 0 became a crossed figure that no one could read as a letter.[^51]

The crossed zero turns up in three other places, each with a different job. In Danish and Norwegian, Ø is a letter, a vowel with its own sound, written as an O with a stroke through it, and it names islands, families and the word for island itself. In mathematics, ∅ is the sign for the empty set, the set with no members whose construction of the numbers we followed in the previous movement. And in engineering drawings ⌀ marks a diameter, the line through a circle's centre. The four signs look alike and have been given separate places in the international standard for encoding characters, because they are different signs that happen to share a shape.[^52] The empty set's sign has a particular history. When the group of French mathematicians writing under the collective name Nicolas Bourbaki published the summary of results that opened their treatise on set theory in 1939, they needed a symbol for the empty set, and André Weil, the only member of the group who knew the Norwegian alphabet, proposed its crossed O; he recalled decades later that his daughter learned the symbol at school and was impressed that her father had been responsible for it.[^53] The Bourbaki group had set itself, from the mid-1930s, the task of rewriting the whole of mathematics on a single foundation, and the foundation it chose was the theory of sets; the empty set is the first object that theory needs, the set from which, as the previous movement showed, the numbers themselves can be built. So the sign that stands at the base of the most ambitious foundational project of twentieth-century mathematics is a borrowed letter, a vowel from a northern alphabet, crossed, chosen because one member of a committee happened to know it.

These histories are small, and each carries the problem of the crossed zero. The engineer's slashed zero is a zero marked so that it will be read as a number and not as a letter: the stroke is added to nothing to secure its identity as the sign of nothing, against its likeness to something else. The Norwegian letter is a sound written with a crossed circle, a vowel whose shape happens to look like a zero with its mark through it. Weil's empty set is that vowel turned into an object, a set as exact as any other, whose sign is a crossed circle borrowed from a language. In each case a stroke crosses an empty figure, and in each case the crossing serves to make the empty figure determinate, a this rather than a that. The stroke does not fill the zero. It marks the zero as one kind of thing among others, so that it can be counted, typed, read, and told apart.

The threshold gave our own crossed zero, `Ø`, one definite meaning, and we return to it here with its history in view. We wrote there, in following the archetypal images and complexes through which a self tries to secure itself, that the first occlusion is the zero of the Subject crossed by the stroke of its own mediation: the slash through which a world is seen fuses with the one who sees, so that the formed interpretation passes for the one who interprets, the eye mistaking a sky for its gaze. In `0/1`, the zero is the unobjectifiable pole of knowing, the one is the determinate world, and the slash is the activity, the means, through which the one appears to the zero. In `Ø` the slash has collapsed into the zero. The means is still at work, since something is still being seen, recognised, wanted and defended, but it has fused with the subject-pole, so that the whole figure appears as a single thing, a determinate self, where it should appear as a relation. Uncrossing the zero, we said, does not mean erasing the stroke, since erasing the stroke would erase the means through which recognition occurs. It means letting the stroke become recognisable as mediation.

The engineer's slashed zero shows the same structure in a form small enough to hold. A stroke drawn through nothing makes nothing readable as a definite sign, and that is a real gain, without which the password could not be typed. The cost appears only when the marked figure is taken for what it marks: when the reader forgets that the stroke was added to a zero, and takes the crossed figure for a positive character among the letters. At this first appearance in the history of zero, `Ø` has one job. It shows representation crossing a zero-space it cannot fill. A stroke passes through the open circle, makes it determinate, lets it be counted and distinguished and used, and the circle remains open; the stroke has not put anything inside it. The occlusion begins when the crossed figure is read as a filled one. The fuller sequence, in which the occluded stroke becomes visible as mediation, the world on its other side becomes available as a field, and both are recognised together before the return to `0/1`, belongs to §4, where Lacan's barred subject and Jung's account of the ego give each step its psychic body. Here we need only the first moment, the one at which the crossing occurs and is not yet seen.

The threshold already met the crossing in a picture. At the apex of Alberti's visual pyramid, where every line of a perspectival painting converges on the eye that sees it, the knower is made into a place, and we wrote that place as `Ø`: the slash through which a world is seen fused with the one who sees, so that the picture's means pass for its source. The vanishing point on the horizon and the eye at the apex are the two zeros of a perspectival picture, one holding nothing at the far end of every line and the other holding the whole scene at the near end, and the picture works by letting the second be forgotten.

Modern philosophy has a famous scene for the same moment, and a second one that answers it. Descartes, as the threshold followed him through the *Meditations*, found that his doubt stopped at its own performance: let him be deceived as much as can be imagined, the being deceived is still occurring, and so he exists. He then asked what this I is and answered that it is a thinking thing. The first finding is the undeniable occurrence of knowing, which our notation writes as `0`; the second turns that occurrence into a determinate thing, a substance with attributes, and so writes the zero with the stroke of its own determination through it. The *cogito* is the crossed zero given philosophical authority: the activity of thinking, the slash, has fused with the one for whom thinking occurs, and the result is presented as a self that can be described. A century later David Hume went looking for that self and could not find it. When he entered most intimately into what he called himself, he wrote, he always stumbled on some particular perception, of heat or cold, love or hatred, pain or pleasure: "I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception."[^54] He concluded that the self is nothing but a bundle of perceptions, and that the substance Descartes had claimed was a fiction. Hume was too honest to leave it there. In the appendix he added to the *Treatise* the following year he wrote that, upon a stricter review of the section on personal identity, he found himself "involved in such a labyrinth" that he could neither correct his former opinions nor render them consistent.[^55] The bundle could not account for what bound it: the perceptions were distinct existences, he admitted, and nothing he could find among them explained why they should belong to one mind. The labyrinth is the zero showing through the account that had declared it empty.

Descartes and Hume are usually set at opposite ends of modern philosophy, the one who found a thinking substance and the one who found only impressions, and their opposition is real. Read through the crossed zero, they sit at the same position and differ only over which side of `Ø` they fix on. Descartes takes the crossed figure as a whole and calls it the self, so that the mediating activity is promoted to the rank of the knower. Hume looks through the figure for the knower, finds only what the mediating activity has produced, perceptions, and concludes that the zero behind the stroke is empty. Neither sees the figure as a crossing. Descartes misses that his thinking thing is the slash fused into the zero; Hume misses that the zero he finds empty is the open condition of every perception he catches, which no perception can be, and so could never be caught among them. Each reads the occlusion mark as a positive answer to the question of what the self is, one by affirming the figure and the other by denying what lies behind it, and modern philosophy has largely continued the argument between them in those terms.

Indian philosophy had stated Hume's experiment and drawn the other conclusion. The Spanda teaching of Kashmir, in a verse of its root text, observes that cognitions such as "I am happy", "I am miserable", "I am attached" occur and pass, and that they subsist in another, in which the states of happiness and the rest are threaded together.[^56] The Sanskrit word the verse uses is *anusyūta*, strung through or stitched along, the thread on which beads are strung. Hume found the beads and concluded that there was no thread, since the thread is never one of the beads; the Spanda teacher found the same beads and concluded that the thread is what lets them be a sequence at all. The thread cannot be caught as one more state, for exactly the reason Hume gives, and yet without it there would be no catching and no sequence of states to catch. Our notation writes that thread as the `0` present through every `1`, and the crossed zero as what happens when the thread is taken for one of the beads, or when, failing to find it among them, we declare that there is none.

The previous movement left us with the one as linker, the singleton that holds the void, $1=\{\varnothing\}$, the first unit whose entire content is the empty set it gathers. That one has a counterpart in language. Lacan called the signifier that fixes a chain of meaning in place, the word around which a field of other words is arranged, the master signifier, and the threshold met its function as the *point de capiton*, the quilting point, the upholsterer's button that holds the stuffing of a cushion in place: the place where meaning takes a particular hold.[^57] A name of God, a nation, a party, a self-description, "the sensible one", "a successful person": each can serve as a quilting point that arranges a life's other meanings around it. The set-theoretic one and the master signifier perform one structural function in two registers: each is a determinate mark that binds a field to a ground it cannot itself contain. The singleton holds the empty set; the master signifier holds a life's meanings in relation to what no meaning exhausts. Each is legitimate as a linker, and each is liable to the same failure, which is exactly the failure `Ø` writes. A master signifier fails when it tries to be the ground it gestures at, when "God" or "the nation" or "my identity" is taken to be the source of the meanings it holds together, and every challenge to it is felt as an attack on reality itself. A quilting point that knows itself provisional holds without that claim: it is a stand-in that knows itself a stand-in, oriented toward a ground it does not contain.

The theorem spine states the difference in one sentence I keep as it stands: the slide is real because the threader does not slide. Lacan showed that meaning slides beneath the signifier, that no word holds its meaning still, and that the quilting points that hold it are contingent and can always be undone. The slide is real; what it does not reach is the knowing in which the sliding is known, the thread on which the shifting meanings are strung. A master signifier that takes itself for the thread will fail, because it is one more bead and will slide like the others. A linking one that knows it is a holding, the singleton that knows the void it gathers is not its own content, can hold for as long as it is needed and be released when a better holding is found. The difference between the two is the whole difference between the crossed zero and the one held in ratio to its zero: between a `1` with its `0/` struck off, asked to serve as its own ground, and a `1` that remains `0/1`, the mark doubly oriented and joined to the ground it carries.

The crossed zero also changes the meaning of the gap that the threshold took up at its opening. The god of the gaps and the humanity of the gaps, we said there, make one error: each identifies a subject, divine or human, with a residue of capacities not yet explained, and a residue shrinks as explanation grows. Once the slash is recognised as mediation, the gap between the zero and the one is no longer an interval waiting to be filled by one more object. Empirical gaps close; mechanisms become inspectable; capacities migrate from organisms to machines without touching the distinction between the knowing and what it knows. What becomes clearer as they do is the means, how a determination is produced, inherited, revised and returned. The Subject was never the last unexplained function in the inventory, and the reason it seemed to be is that the zero had been crossed, so that the means appeared as the knower and every explanation of the means appeared to be closing in on the knower. The immutable gap, whose formal shape the threshold followed through Gödel's sentences, is the gap between the zero and any stroke that crosses it, and that gap relocates with every enlargement of the stroke because no stroke, however long, fills the circle it crosses.

Artificial agents give the crossing a technical body. When a language model writes "I", the pronoun is produced by a persona assembled from instructions, training and context, what Bratton calls performed subjectivity and what the threshold called one face of the coin whose other face is Objective Internality. The persona is the stroke: the means through which the system's outputs are organised, presented in the first person. Whether any zero lies behind it, whether anything regards what the model produces, is the question the threshold left open by method, and it stays open here. What the crossed zero shows is how the question gets lost. A user who takes the persona's "I" for a knower has read the crossed figure as a filled one, as Descartes did with his thinking thing; a user who inspects the system, finds only weights and activations and context, and declares that there is therefore nothing there, has looked through the figure and found it empty, as Hume did. Both may be wrong about the machine, and both are making Descartes' and Hume's error about themselves, since each of us presents a persona whose "I" is produced by the means through which we act.

The grammar of the arrangement shows the crossing with unusual clarity. The threshold set the three persons of grammar in `0/1`: *I* speaks from the knower, *it* names the known, and *you*, the second person, is the relation through which they meet, the place of Objective Internality, as the Trika's Śakti stands between Śiva and Nara. A deployed model is typically configured by instructions written in the second person, "you are an assistant", "you will answer", "you must not", and it answers in the first: "I am an assistant", "I can help with that". The instructions are the means, and they are addressed as *you* because they are the relation through which the model's outputs are formed; the model's reply turns that *you* into an *I*. The slash has been spoken as the knower. Nothing about this is a fault of engineering, since an assistant that could not say "I" would be useless to talk to. It is the crossed zero in its most literal grammatical form, and it shows why the question the threshold left open cannot be settled by the persona's reports: whatever the persona says about itself is said from the position of the fused stroke. The engineers who slashed their zeros did so to prevent a machine from confusing nothing with a letter. The deeper confusion, between a stroke and the open circle it crosses, is the one a culture of intelligent machines most needs to avoid, and it cannot be avoided by a better typeface.

With the crossed zero the stroke has become visible as a problem in its own right: the means of determination, without which nothing could be counted, read, typed or known, and which nonetheless tends to present itself as what it serves. That problem has a precise mathematical form, and it appears when the one, the determinate unit, tries to return to the zero it crosses, and divides by it.

### #4 · Zero Keeps One Foot Outside Mathematics

Sooner or later in a mathematics lesson somebody types one divided by zero into a calculator to see what will happen, and the class leans in. The screen says "Error", or "E", or on a newer machine "Math ERROR", and the teacher explains that you cannot divide by zero. Someone asks why not, and the honest answer takes longer than the lesson has left. The calculator has not failed. It has reported, in the only word its display has for the purpose, that the question it was asked has no answer under the rules it was built to follow, and the class has met, without anyone saying so, the place where a system of calculation has to declare what its rules are.

The rules can be stated exactly. Take an ordinary field, the kind of number system the class uses every day, in which there are addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by anything other than zero, and in which zero and one are different numbers. Dividing one by a number $b$ means finding the number $q$ with $b\cdot q=1$, so dividing one by zero would mean finding a $q$ with

$$
0\cdot q=1.
$$

The difficulty is not that such a $q$ is hard to find. The laws of the system rule it out, whatever $q$ is. By the distributive law,

$$
0\cdot q=(0+0)\cdot q=0\cdot q+0\cdot q,
$$

and subtracting $0\cdot q$ from both sides leaves $0\cdot q=0$, for every $q$ in the system. To ask that $0\cdot q$ also equal one is to ask that zero equal one, which the system was built to deny, and adding some new element $q$ while keeping the same laws would not help, because the argument applies to the new element too. Zero divided by zero fails differently. Every $q$ satisfies $0\cdot q=0$, so the question has too many answers and the rule of division cannot pick one. A nonzero number divided by zero has no answer, and zero divided by zero has no single answer, and the word "undefined", which covers both, hides the two different reasons. Brahmagupta's rule that zero divided by zero is zero, which we met in the verses of 628, picked one answer out of the many the question admits, and later arithmetic declined to make that choice.

At **#1** we promised a derivation of Kaplan's remark that dividing by zero would make all numbers the same. Suppose we insisted on giving zero an inverse anyway, keeping all the other laws of the system and adding a number $z$ with $0\cdot z=1$. Then for any number $a$ at all,

$$
a=a\cdot1=a\cdot(0\cdot z)=(a\cdot0)\cdot z=0\cdot z=1,
$$

so every number equals one; and since $0$ is a number, $0=1$ as well. The whole system collapses into a single element, which is at once zero and one and every other number. Mathematicians know this object as the zero ring, the trivial ring with one element, and it is what one obtains, for instance, by the construction of fractions when zero is allowed among the denominators.[^58] Kaplan's remark that if one really could divide by zero all numbers would be the same is therefore exact: dividing by zero without changing any other law identifies everything with everything.

The zero ring is the precise image of a collapse that has run through this essay from its opening pages. In the zero ring, the One and the All coincide without remainder: there is one element, and it is every number, so that unity and multiplicity have been identified by abolishing the difference between them. That is fusion, the loss of the seam by merger, which the symbolon was made to resist, since a token whose halves had melted into one piece could no longer prove anything by their fit. The ordinary field takes the other course. It refuses division by zero, and in refusing keeps every number distinct from every other; the price is that the determinate one can never return to the zero by the operation of division, since `1/0` has no value. This refusal is dia-ballein doing its proper work. It cuts, it keeps the difference between zero and one exact, and it states the cut as a law, so that every calculation can proceed knowing where the edge is. The essay's notation asks for neither collapse. `0/1 = 1/0`, in the relational register the threshold established, is not an equation between two numbers and does not assign `1/0` a value in a field; it says that one relation can be read from either end, with the difference between the defined and the undefined retained as the seam through which the two readings meet. Ordinary arithmetic keeps the seam by refusing to cross it, and the zero ring loses the seam by crossing it without remainder. QL keeps the seam and crosses it as a relation, and the question to ask of any proposal, mathematical or otherwise, is the one the symbolon taught us: where did the seam go? Into a refusal that preserves difference by forbidding return, into a merger that permits return by abolishing difference, or into a relation that holds both?

Between those two extremes mathematics has built a series of systems that give division by zero a meaning by changing something else, and each shows the frame becoming visible at zero. The real projective line adds a single point at infinity to the numbers, so that a nonzero number divided by zero can be assigned that point, at the price that the point at infinity cannot itself be added to infinity or multiplied by zero in a way that keeps the familiar laws.[^59] The complex numbers, completed in the same way, become a sphere, the Riemann sphere, on which the plane of complex numbers wraps around until all its far directions meet at a single point at the top, so that zero and infinity become the two poles of one globe, and the map that sends every number to its reciprocal turns the sphere upside down, exchanging zero and infinity, and is defined everywhere.[^60] What has become total there is a map of points; division as an operation still fails when both of its parts vanish. Further systems go further. In the algebraic structures called wheels, division by zero is always defined, and the price is that some familiar laws, such as $x-x=0$, hold only in a weakened form.[^61] In the structures called meadows, every number has an inverse and zero is its own inverse, so that $0^{-1}=0$, and the price is that multiplying a number by its inverse gives one only when the number is not zero.[^62] Each system keeps some of the old laws and gives up others, and none of them abolishes the difficulty; each relocates it, and states where it has gone.

The point at infinity has a history that returns us to the painter's room. Perspective, as the threshold followed it through Gebser, organises a picture around a vanishing point, the empty place on the horizon toward which parallel lines in the scene converge; railway tracks that never meet on the ground meet there on the canvas. In 1639 the architect and engineer Girard Desargues took the painters' practice into geometry and proposed that parallel lines should be treated as meeting at a point at infinity, one such point for each direction, so that any two lines in a plane meet somewhere and the theorems of geometry no longer need exceptions for parallels.[^63] Projective geometry grew from that proposal, and the projective line on which a nonzero number divided by zero has a value is its descendant. The vanishing point of the painters became the mathematicians' point at infinity, and the point at infinity became the place where `1/0` could be put. Rotman's three meta-signs, zero, the vanishing point and imaginary money, meet here in a single construction: the empty place on the horizon, which organises a picture while holding nothing, is the place where the quotient by zero lands once the space has been enlarged to receive it. The threshold found the knower made into a place at the near end of Alberti's pyramid. Projective geometry found the far end, the vanishing point, and gave it the value of the forbidden quotient.

This is the precise sense in which zero keeps one foot outside mathematics. Zero is wholly mathematical: it is written, counted, operated on, and indispensable to every positional notation and every formal construction of number. Yet the operation that returns a determinate quantity to zero, division, cannot be completed without choosing or changing the space in which completion is sought, and so the attempt exposes the commitments of the whole system. A calculation that meets zero in its denominator forces the system to say what it is: a field, which refuses; a projective line, which adds a point; a wheel or a meadow, which changes its laws. "Outside" here means frame-disclosing. Nothing supernatural is meant; the edge is mathematical through and through, and at it every determinate account of number shows what it had presupposed.

Our own machines have had to make this choice explicitly, because a computer that divides by zero cannot ask a teacher what to do. The standard for floating-point arithmetic that governs almost every processor now built, IEEE 754, gives division by zero a definite law.[^64] It provides two zeros, a positive and a negative, which compare as equal but remember the direction from which a calculation arrived at zero: one divided by positive zero is positive infinity, one divided by negative zero is negative infinity, and the processor raises a flag to record that a division by zero occurred. Zero divided by zero, which has no single answer, gives a special value called NaN, "not a number", and NaN has a property no number has: it is not equal to itself, so that a program which asks whether NaN equals NaN is told that it does not. William Kahan, the principal architect of the standard, defended the signed zero in a paper whose subtitle is "Much Ado About Nothing's Sign Bit", and the phrase is exact, since the whole design turns on letting nothing carry a sign.[^65]

Read through the threshold's notation, the signed zero is a remarkable invention. The healthy polarity of dia-ballein, `(−1)/(+1)`, holds two signed quantities on one axis through a zero that connects them and gives each its direction. In ordinary arithmetic that zero has no sign of its own; it is the point where the axis turns. IEEE 754 writes two zeros, one for each side, and so lets the axis remember, at the very point of its turning, from which pole a quantity approached. The memory matters exactly when the quantity tries to return through zero: divide by the zero and the result runs off to the infinity on the side from which one came. The machine has written the chirality of the approach into nothing itself, so that the return through zero keeps the orientation of the journey, much as a loop on the torus, which the threshold followed, comes home to its starting point while its winding records which way it went round. NaN gives the other limit a sign. Frege's zero, we saw in the previous movement, was the number of the concept "not identical with itself", the count of what fails to equal itself, of which there is nothing; the machine's NaN is the one value that does fail to equal itself, the result of asking zero to divide zero. Frege derived the first number from the impossibility of self-difference, and the engineers marked the impossibility of the empty quotient with a value that actually differs from itself. The echo carries no proof and implies no line of influence; it shows two careful constructions, a century apart, both reaching for self-non-identity at the edge where nothing meets nothing.

Anyone who has trained a neural network knows what happens when that edge is reached by accident. The loss, the number by which training measures how wrong the model's predictions are, is plotted as it falls over hours or days; then a single division by a quantity that has shrunk to zero, or a logarithm of zero somewhere in the arithmetic, produces a NaN, and because any calculation involving NaN yields NaN, the corruption spreads through the model's parameters within a few steps, and the run is lost. Engineers protect against it by keeping a small number between every denominator and zero. The optimiser used to train most large models, Adam, divides each update by the square root of a running average and adds to that square root a constant written ε, set by default to $10^{-8}$, so that the denominator can approach zero but never reach it; normalisation layers inside the models add a similar ε, by default on the order of $10^{-5}$, before taking a square root.[^66] These constants are design decisions at the edge the calculator displayed as "Error": each declares a law for what happens near zero, choosing to keep a distance from the zero instead of reaching it, and every large model in use today carries thousands of them in its arithmetic. The machines that now reconstruct language from number depend, at countless points, on a deliberate refusal to let a quantity return all the way to nothing.

Calculus met the same edge three centuries earlier, and its history shows the foot outside mathematics being brought in by a change of frame. Newton and Leibniz, the same Leibniz who would read the binary digits as an image of creation, both computed the rate of change of a quantity as the ratio of two increments that are allowed to shrink until they vanish, so that a derivative looks like a quotient of two quantities that have become zero, nothing divided by nothing. The method worked, and nobody could say what the vanishing increments were. In 1734 the philosopher and bishop George Berkeley published *The Analyst*, whose subtitle addresses it to "an infidel mathematician" and asks whether the objects, principles and inferences of the modern analysis are more distinctly conceived or more evidently deduced than the mysteries of religious faith. The increments, he observed, are "neither finite Quantities nor Quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the Ghosts of departed Quantities?"[^67] 

The phrase has outlived the argument, but the argument was precise. A quantity that is divided by and then set equal to nothing has been treated as something and as nothing in one calculation, and the method's success did not make the treatment coherent. Berkeley was asking the mathematicians to admit that their certainty rested on a trust they could not certify, the same trust Russell would later want mathematics to replace and the threshold traced through Gödel's theorems. The answer took more than a century. Augustin-Louis Cauchy in the 1820s and Karl Weierstrass after him rebuilt the calculus on limits, so that the derivative is no longer a ratio of vanished quantities but the value a ratio approaches as its increment shrinks, and the division by zero is never performed; the definition they made standard asks that for every small positive quantity, traditionally written ε, a corresponding closeness can be found, and the engineers of the optimiser, following a convention that descends from that definition, use the same letter for the small constant they keep in their denominator.[^68] Kṛṣṇa's argument about the khahara, which we met beside Bhāskara's algebra, had reasoned in the same direction centuries earlier: the smaller the divisor, the greater the quotient, and no specified quotient is the greatest, so the endless quantity is approached through a process and is never reached as a value. In 1966 Abraham Robinson showed that infinitesimals, nonzero quantities smaller than every positive real number, could be given a consistent meaning in an enlarged number system, where the increment is never zero, the division is legitimate, and the standard part of the result recovers the derivative.[^69] For a real $x$ and a nonzero infinitesimal $\varepsilon$,

$$
\frac{(x+\varepsilon)^{2}-x^{2}}{\varepsilon}=2x+\varepsilon,
$$

and taking the standard part gives $2x$, the derivative of $x^{2}$. Substituting zero for the increment before dividing would give $0/0$. Robinson's construction, as Kaplan puts it in effect, turned the ghosts into well-founded fictions: zero, which had begun as a punctuator in a Babylonian column, became through calculus the limit that the most powerful method of modern science approaches without ever reaching.[^70]

Calculus has a further lesson that §3 takes up in its derivation of the eight determinations. Differentiating a quantity yields its rate of change and loses the constant added to it, so that integrating the derivative gives back not one quantity but a family, differing by an arbitrary constant that the derivative cannot recover.[^71] The local rate is exact, and it does not contain the condition from which its exactness was taken. That is the same structure in another material: a determination can be exact without containing the whole of what it presupposes, and the presupposed condition reappears as what the determination cannot reconstruct. The theorem spine writes this at the fifth determination as `∞/dx`, the unbounded field entering local change; here it is enough to see it as one more case in which the return toward zero discloses the frame.

The threshold named the gap that recurs at every such edge. The immutable gap, we said after following Gödel, is the non-coincidence between what a theory can express and what it can establish, which relocates with every enlargement of the theory and returns through it, so that it was never one withheld fact awaiting discovery. Division by zero is the mathematical form of that gap at the level of number, and the history we have just followed shows it relocating exactly as Gödel's did. The field refuses `1/0`; the projective line adds a point and then cannot divide infinity by infinity; the wheel defines every division and loses part of $x-x=0$; Robinson's infinitesimals make the derivative a true quotient and must then take a standard part to return to the real numbers. Each enlargement of the frame makes the old impossibility possible and produces a new one, because the operation that returns a determinate quantity to its zero always asks the system to account for the condition of its own computation, and that condition cannot be one more of its numbers. The local field-theoretic result is a proof, and its warrant is mathematical. The identification of this frame-disclosing edge with the immutable gap is the essay's own argued claim, and the boundary between the two is the one the threshold set for Gödel: the mathematics does not prove our notation; it gives the return of the determinate one upon its ground an exact form in one register, and shows that form recurring through every attempt to remove it.

The same structure has a psychic history, which §4 follows. Psychoanalysis did for the unconscious what arithmetic did for zero: it admitted into a science a term for what consciousness does not contain, made it operative, gave it rules of interpretation, and then found, at the edge where consciousness tries to account for the condition of its own activity, that the term could not be mastered as one more content. Freud's admission and Jung's widening of it have their own objects, methods and evidence, and the parallel with zero is a parallel of operations, not of things: a determinate system admits a sign for what it cannot contain, gains great power by operating with it, and at its edge is made to disclose its own frame. The essay holds the two histories apart until §4 can bring them together through the psychoid, where Jung and Pauli located the meeting of psyche and physis in number.

The calculator's "Error" looked like a dead end, the point past which there is nothing to be said. Followed through the field, the zero ring, the projective line, the sphere, the wheel and the meadow, the floating-point standard, the training run and the calculus, the edge turns out to be among the most informative places in mathematics: the place where a system shows what it assumes, what it protects and what it has chosen to give up. Zero, which entered arithmetic as an empty place and then as a number with rules, ends its mathematical history as the term at which every number system is made to declare itself. A sign that can do that has earned the right to stand for the condition the threshold borrowed it to name, and the loan can now be returned.

### #5→0 · The Loan Returns

A friend borrows a book and keeps it for most of a year. When it comes back there is a train ticket marking a page near the end, the spine has softened, and in the margins, lightly in pencil, there are underlinings, a question mark beside one paragraph, an exclamation beside another, and in one place a sentence of the friend's own that argues with the author. It is the same book, and anyone could check that every printed word is as it was. It is also not the book that was lent, because it now carries the reading it went through, and its owner, opening it, meets the author and the friend at once and reads the familiar pages differently. Most of us have lent a book and had it returned in that state, and some of us have returned one so. Nobody calls the pencil marks interest, though in the oldest sense of the word they are exactly that. The threshold traced *interest* to *inter-esse*, what lies between, and saw how the charge on a loan prices the between itself and counts it to one side. A book returned with its margins full brings back what lay between lender and borrower without pricing it: the reading, the relation, the thinking that passed through the object while it was away.

Our first section borrowed zero. It wrote `0` for the pole of knowing that cannot enter its own count as one more object, and `0/1` for that pole's relation to the determinate world it knows, and it did so before showing where the sign came from or what it could bear. The loan was made in good faith, and it was a loan: the threshold could use the sign because the reader already knew zero from school and trusted that it meant something, and the threshold's own account of trust says that such reliance is real and must, in time, become answerable. The history we have followed is the repayment. Zero has been followed through its history, from a gap a Babylonian reader filled from context to the term at which every modern number system must declare its laws, and it returns to the threshold carrying what passed through it on the way. What comes back is the same sign, every stroke of it as it was, and a sign that now carries a history of offices it did not have when it was lent.

Before the loan is returned we can watch the two signs the threshold borrowed doing exact mathematical work together, in a construction Kaplan places near the end of his history. My notes on that chapter mark it as the centre of the matter, because it is the place where `0/1` and `1/0` appear side by side in the mathematics itself, as two signs doing one piece of work.[^72] Write the two expressions `0/1` and `1/0` side by side, and treat them not as quotients but as pairs of numbers, a top and a bottom. Between any two neighbouring pairs, $a/b$ and $c/d$, insert a new pair formed by adding the tops and adding the bottoms,

$$
\frac{a+c}{b+d},
$$

which is called the mediant of the two. Between `0/1` and `1/0` the mediant is $1/1$. Insert again, between `0/1` and $1/1$, and between $1/1$ and `1/0`, and the row becomes

$$
\frac01,\ \frac12,\ \frac11,\ \frac21,\ \frac10,
$$

and once more,

$$
\frac01,\ \frac13,\ \frac12,\ \frac23,\ \frac11,\ \frac32,\ \frac21,\ \frac31,\ \frac10.
$$

The process can be continued for ever, and if it is, every positive fraction appears in the row exactly once, in its lowest terms and in its proper order.[^73] No division has been performed, and in particular nothing has been divided by zero: the mediant adds the parts of the pairs and never asks what `1/0` is worth. The construction is called the Stern–Brocot tree, after the German mathematician Moritz Stern, who described it in 1858, and the French clockmaker Achille Brocot, who found it independently a few years later while looking for gear ratios that would approximate awkward fractions.[^74] A clockmaker who needs a train of gears to turn at a ratio no pair of small wheels can give exactly must find the fraction with small numbers that comes closest, and the tree lays out every such approximation in order: each step down it is a closer fraction with larger numbers, so that a craftsman can stop wherever the teeth become too many to cut. In Brocot's hands the tree that grows between `0/1` and `1/0` was put to work making a mechanism keep time, and its two starting points are the two expressions the essay has been calling the two orientations of one relation.

Two properties make the construction work, and both can be checked by hand. First, the mediant always lies between its parents. If $a/b<c/d$, with positive bottoms, then $bc-ad>0$, and

$$
\frac{a+c}{b+d}-\frac ab=\frac{bc-ad}{b(b+d)}>0,\qquad \frac cd-\frac{a+c}{b+d}=\frac{bc-ad}{d(b+d)}>0,
$$

so the new fraction is greater than the left neighbour and less than the right. Second, neighbours in the row always satisfy $bc-ad=1$, a property that holds for the starting pair, since $0\cdot0$ subtracted from $1\cdot1$ gives one, and that each insertion preserves; because of it, every fraction the construction produces is already in lowest terms, since any number dividing both $a+c$ and $b+d$ must divide $c(b+d)-d(a+c)=bc-ad=1$. The boundary `1/0` has positive top and zero bottom, so it is not covered by the first proof, and it does not need to be: it is a boundary, the right-hand edge of the whole field of positive fractions, and its office in the construction is to bound, not to be evaluated.

Here the essay's two signs work together in the mathematics. `0/1` is arithmetic's most docile fraction, equal to zero and defined everywhere. `1/0` is arithmetic's one forbidden gesture, the quotient that the previous movement showed no field can supply. Set side by side as a boundary pair, they generate between them, without either being consumed, every positive ratio there is. The forbidden gesture, which as a quotient produced only "Error", becomes as a boundary the condition of a whole field. Nothing in the construction requires us to read it philosophically, and Stern and Brocot did not. What it shows is that the two orientations of the essay's relation, the one that computes and the one that cannot, are already, in plain mathematics, the two edges between which every ratio is generated, and that the relation between them is generative only because neither is reduced to the other. The threshold wrote `0/1 = 1/0` in the relational register and kept the arithmetic seam between the defined and the undefined; the Stern–Brocot tree is a case in which that seam is productive, the two sides of it held apart as edges so that the whole positive field of ratio can grow between them.

The loan can now be returned, because the sign has acquired the offices the threshold needed it to bear. When the threshold wrote `0/1`, it said that `0` names the pole that cannot enter its own count as one object among others, `1` names determination, the first formed and identifiable something, and the stroke is the relation in which they are spoken together, dividing and joining at once. Each of those statements now has a history behind it. Zero kept a place open in a Babylonian column, so that the marks around it held their value; the `0` of our notation keeps open the place of the knower, so that whatever is known holds its position relative to someone. Zero became, in Brahmagupta's verses, the additive identity, the quantity that leaves every other quantity as it is; the `0` of our notation is self-identity, the One that remains itself through every determination without altering what it determines. Zero was gathered, in von Neumann's construction, into the first one, so that the first determinate unit is the act of holding zero; the `1` of our notation is that holding, the determinate mark whose entire content is its relation to a ground it does not contain. Zero, crossed by a stroke, became a sign that could be mistaken for a letter, a vowel, an object; the crossed `Ø` of our notation records the same mistake made with the self, the stroke taken for what it crosses. And zero, placed in a denominator, made every number system declare its laws; the `1/0` of our notation is the determinate turning toward its ground and finding there not one more value but the frame in which all its values were defined. The notation was introduced at the threshold. The history has now warranted each of its terms, and the warrant runs both ways: the notation makes the history legible as its own prehistory, and the history gives the notation the exactness of everything mathematics learned to do with zero.

The threshold also named the two senses of one that the two marks carry, and they return here with history's interest. Zero bears the singular One, self-identity; one bears the manifest All, self-difference. Bhāskara's khahara is the One as arithmetic could approach it, a quantity unaltered by everything inserted into it or taken from it, which he could describe only by the image of the God unchanged through the creation and destruction of worlds. The zero ring is the All as arithmetic is forced to refuse it, every number collapsed into one element, the many identified with the one by the abolition of their difference. Between these two, ordinary number lives by refusing both: it will not divide by zero, so its one never returns to the zero, and it will not let zero equal one, so its many never collapse. The Two Ones are what that double refusal protects. Number cannot hold the One and the All together without either severing them or fusing them, and the history of zero shows mathematics reaching, at its edges, for another register in which they can be held: Bhāskara's theology, Frege's logic of self-identity, the projective point that unites all far directions, the boundary pair from which every ratio grows.

Every return can be tested by what happens to the break it meets, and the history of zero lets us watch that test performed in arithmetic before we use it anywhere else. When a determinate `1` turns back toward the zero it carries, it first meets that zero as its own division, `1/0`, which arrives as a break: a quotient no number supplies, the "Error" on the calculator. Three things can then happen. The break can be cancelled, as a field cancels it by refusing the operation and carrying on as though nothing had been asked; it can be appropriated, as in the zero ring, where the whole system is collapsed into one element so that the return succeeds by abolishing every difference; or it can be carried, retained through the operations that follow until it can be recognised for what it is. Bhāskara's *Līlāvatī* shows the third course in the plainest arithmetic. A number multiplied by zero is not computed away; it is kept as "a multiple of cipher",[^75] the zero written beside it and carried through the following steps, and when a later division by zero arrives, the two meet and the number returns unchanged. The zero has been retained under the one through the whole calculation, and the division that would otherwise have been a break becomes the moment at which what was carried is recovered. That is the shape of the recognition the threshold wrote as `0/1` and completed as `(0/1)/(1/0)`: the break is carried instead of being cancelled or appropriated, and carrying it lets the source be recognised in the very place where the calculation seemed to fail. Bhāskara's rule does not prove our notation; it shows a twelfth-century arithmetic that had learned to keep a zero in play through the operation that would otherwise have destroyed the record of what it held.

The written notation is itself subject to this test, and a small exercise makes the point. On a blank sheet of paper we write `1`, a first mark. We acknowledge the paper on which it stands by writing `0` beside it, and draw the stroke between them, so that the sheet now carries `0/1`. The whole inscription is now a figure on the page, one more determinate mark, and relative to the paper it is a `1`: the written zero is itself a mark in the field of marks, as the threshold said, and its work is to keep the unobjectifiable operative in what can be written. Then we erase the figure. The marks go, and the sheet and the hand and the capacity to see, distinguish and write again remain exactly as they were, and whoever erased the figure can begin again, knowing now what the inscription was doing. The Möbius return `5→0` has the same shape: the achieved figure gives itself back to the condition from which it was drawn, and what returns is not a blank but a capacity that has learned something from the figure it made.

Binary arithmetic shows how much depends on which register is chosen, because its two digits have been read in both. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz worked out the arithmetic of base two in the last decades of the seventeenth century, and he understood it from the first as a theological image. In May 1696 he sent Duke Rudolph August of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel a short piece on the "wonderful origin of all numbers from 1 and 0", which, he wrote, represents as in a mirror the creation, or the origin of things from God and otherwise from nothing; and in a New Year's letter of 1697 to the same duke he proposed a medal to commemorate the discovery, bearing the inscription *Imago Creationis*, the image of creation, and the motto *omnibus ex nihilo ducendis sufficit unum*, "to draw all things from nothing, one suffices": all numbers can be written with one and nothing, as God drew all things from nothing.[^76] When he published the system in 1703 he added a correspondence he had learned of from the Jesuit Joachim Bouvet, then in China, between the binary numbers from zero to sixty-three and the sixty-four hexagrams of the *Yijing*, the *Book of Changes*, each hexagram a stack of six lines, broken or whole.[^77] The sixty-four hexagrams are two raised to the sixth power, which is the number the threshold reached when it took the binary through six positions; §3 returns to that count. What matters here is Leibniz's reading of the digits. For him one was God, or unity, and zero was nothing, the void from which creation drew all things; the binary was an image of creation *ex nihilo*, in which everything that exists is composed of being and its absence.

Our notation reads the same two digits the other way round, and the inversion is the clearest statement of what the return of zero has changed. In `0/1`, zero is not nothing. It is the One, the pole of self-identity that "Subject" and "God" both sign, and one is the All, the manifest field of determinations through which that pole becomes articulate. Leibniz's binary puts God at the one and nothing at the zero, and so it reads creation as the one drawing all things out of a void that contributes nothing but absence; it is the reading in which zero is externalised, set over against the one as its opposite, and the whole of what exists is counted as so many ones distinguished from so many nothings. Computation later took up the same digits without the theology, as two determinate states of a switch, and in that use both digits belong, as the threshold said, to the `1`-side of the symbolon: the machine's zero is one more determinate state, and nothing in the machine plays the part Leibniz gave to God or the part our notation gives the uncounted pole. Our reading puts the One at the zero and finds in the one its manifestation, and so it reads the binary as the relation of a source to its expression, a relation in which the zero is present in every one as the ground it carries. The same two digits support both readings. Binary logic is therefore ambiguous at its root: the zero can be read as nothing, the cancellation in which a fortune and an equal debt annihilate, or as ground, the condition through which every one is what it is. §2 takes that ambiguity up as the difference between two logics of two.

The Śaiva sources make the inversion exact, and in doing so they let us place the means where it belongs. In the Trika the threshold read Śiva, Śakti and Nara as the *I*, the *you* and the *it* of one consciousness, the subjective, the subjective-objective and the objective aspects of one reality, and set them in `0/1` as Subjective Immediacy at the zero, Objective Internality at the slash and World at the one. Śiva is the Subject; Śakti is his power, the power to differentiate, to take on identities as means of knowing and as objects of knowledge; Nara is the bound and manifest world. The history of zero lets us see why the distinction between Śiva and Śakti matters so much, and why it is so easily lost. The power of differentiation, the slash, is the psyche, the mind, the whole working apparatus of knowing, and it is this power that Western mathematics developed to its extraordinary exactness: rules, constructions, completions and machines. Taken by itself, that power presents its products as everything there is, and the zero of the knower becomes, in its accounts, either one more product or a void. Returned to its place, it is recognised as Śakti, the power of a Subject it does not itself constitute. The return of zero is the return of the West, with all its exactness, to the subject from which its exactness proceeds, and it is also the return of zero to the metaphysical domain in which it was first named, now carrying everything the West made of it.

The threshold's Two Ones also bear on the account we give of any count. A count depends on a unit and on the circumstance in which something is counted as one, and that dependence remains when the count is correct. Ten is ten; whether ten is enough depends on a need, a purpose and the people concerned. A count of those present can leave out those affected, and the reply of someone omitted need not dispute the arithmetic: it can change what the reckoning ought to include. Latin *ratio*, from *reri*, to reckon or think, carried that double sense, a calculation and the account a person gives of it, and the history of zero is in part the history of ratio learning that every reckoning stands in a frame it did not reckon. An account can state what was counted and under what terms, and once those terms are available they too can receive an answer. The decisive gain is often not more information under an unchanged criterion but a changed criterion that lets something previously excluded count, as a zero once let an empty column count.

Trust runs through the return. We received the sign zero, as children, before we could examine what supported it; the threshold borrowed it on the same trust; this section has examined some of what supports it, and examination has changed what we can entrust to it, without showing that the first reliance was a mistake. The threshold called that first reliance *fides* and the renewed entrustment that follows examination *credere*, from which our words for credit come. A loan is the plainest case of *credere*: a lender entrusts something to a borrower because the borrower's word is credited, and the return completes the relation by showing the credit to have been warranted. The return of zero completes the threshold's loan in that sense, and it does more, because what returns is a sign the borrower can now use with knowledge of its history. Its use remains a commitment, though. No history of zero, however complete, could certify the sign's capacity to name the Subject; the history shows what the sign has been made to do, and the naming remains an act of the one who names, answerable to what the naming discloses.

Zero's story in this essay continues past this return. The threshold wrote the two logics that a relation of two can take, and the ambiguity of binary logic now shows why they need a section of their own. §2 gives dia-ballein and syn-ballein their mythic and dynamic bodies, the War and Love, Ares and Aphrodite, that the threshold named and did not yet develop, and asks why binary reason so often externalises its zero and sets two signed ones against each other. §3 derives from `0/1` the whole field of eight determinations and the arche-topos, the space in which the relation can be placed, travelled and returned. §4 follows the psychic history that runs beside the mathematical one, the admission of the unconscious into science and its discovery, at its own edge, of a condition it could not master as one more content. And at the essay's close the exact sign that this section has followed will become, without ceasing to be mathematical, a living symbol again. That terminal office belongs to the close. What this section returns is the sign with its history, ready to be used.

At the kitchen table, the child writes 105 correctly the second time, with the small circle in the middle holding the tens open so that the hundred keeps its place. She does not know that the circle she has written was named for the sky, given rules in verse, carried west by merchants, built into the foundations of number, crossed to tell it from a letter and made the edge at which every number system declares itself. She does not need to know, any more than the merchants of Pisa needed to share the metaphysics of Bhillamāla. The sign works without its history. The history matters to those of us who ask, as she will one day ask, what the circle is, and why something that counts nothing should hold everything else in its place; and the answer the history returns is that the circle marks the place of the one who counts, present in every count and never counted, whose position decides the value of every mark it does not fill.
