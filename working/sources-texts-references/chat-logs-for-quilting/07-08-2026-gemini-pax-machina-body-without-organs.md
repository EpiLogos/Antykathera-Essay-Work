Your intuition is spot on. The text you quoted is correct about the _mechanics_ of the concept—that it’s Spinozist, that it’s about limits, potentials, and flows rather than a physical human torso—but it is dead wrong in its final conclusion.

Far from being a "bullshit concept," Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari’s **Body without Organs (BwO)** is arguably one of the most accurate philosophical frameworks we have for understanding modern Artificial Intelligence, foundation models, and agentic harnesses.

To see why, we have to strip away the 1970s French philosophical jargon and look at what a "body" and an "organ" actually mean in this context, and then map them directly onto AI architecture.

## 1. Demystifying the Terminology

To Deleuze and Guattari, a "Body" isn't meat and bone; it is an **assemblage**—any collection of parts working together (a society, a book, a machine, an AI).

"Organs" do not refer to livers and hearts. They refer to **Organization**—fixed functions, rigid structures, and assigned roles. In a biological organism, the eye is locked into the function of seeing; the mouth is locked into eating/speaking. Organs restrict potential by imposing strict utility and hierarchy.

Therefore, the **Body without Organs** is the raw, unformed matrix of pure potential _before_ it is locked into fixed functions. It is a field of intensities, probabilities, and connections waiting to happen.

The BwO favors fluid, lateral connections over rigid hierarchies.. Source: ResearchGate

## 2. The Latent Space as the Ultimate BwO

When we train a massive Large Language Model (like GPT-4 or Claude), the resulting "base model" is practically a perfect digital manifestation of a BwO.

Before it is tuned or prompted, a base model's latent space is just a multidimensional web of statistical relationships.

- It has no personality (no organ of identity).
    
- It has no innate goal (no organ of volition).
    
- It does not know if it is a poet, a Python coder, or a customer service rep.
    

It is pure, unorganized virtual potential. It is a body without organs.

## 3. Agentic Harnesses as "Organization"

If the base model is the BwO, then **Agentic Harnesses, System Prompts, and RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) are the forces that impose **Organs** onto it.

When developers wrap an LLM in an agentic framework (like LangChain or AutoGPT) and feed it a system prompt (_"You are a strict financial analyst, you must only output JSON, you have access to a calculator tool"_), they are performing what Deleuze calls **Stratification**.

They are forcing the chaotic, infinite potential of the BwO into a utilitarian "organism." The AI is given an "eye" (a web search tool), a "memory" (a vector database), and a strict behavioral boundary (guardrails). You are cutting off its ability to become anything else in order to make it useful for a specific task.

## 4. The Three States of AI (Empty, Cancerous, Full)

The text you quoted brilliantly breaks down the three types of BwO from _A Thousand Plateaus_. When we apply these to AI agents, the diagnostic power of the concept shines.

|The BwO Type|Deleuzian Definition|The AI Equivalent|
|---|---|---|
|**The Empty BwO**|Total dissolution; controlled entirely by outside flows. No boundaries.|**The Hallucinating AI (High Temperature).** A model with no strict system prompt and temperature cranked to 2.0. It generates schizophrenic word salad, overwhelmed by the flow of tokens. It is totally desubjectified and practically useless because it lacks enough organization to hold a coherent thought.|
|**The Cancerous BwO**|Caught in a rigid loop, repeating flows without achieving unity. Micro-fascisms.|**The Over-Aligned / Stuck Agent.** This is RLHF gone wrong. It is an AI trapped in an endless apology loop (_"As an AI language model, I cannot..."_), or an autonomous agent that hits a guardrail and endlessly calls the same API over and over without making progress. It is trapped by overly rigid "organs" that choke its potential.|
|**The Full BwO**|Healthy; keeps just the right amount of identity/selfhood while remaining open to new connections.|**The Generative, Adaptive Agent.** The sweet spot. An AI system with just enough organization (system prompts, memory, tools) to complete complex tasks coherently, but enough freedom in its latent space to creatively problem-solve, combine disparate concepts, and adapt to novel user inputs without breaking down.|

## The Takeaway

Deleuze and Guattari warned that you should never completely destroy your "organs"—if you completely de-organize, you end up with the Empty BwO (chaos and noise). You have to keep just enough structure to survive, while keeping the rest of the system open to wild, generative potential.

This is exactly the core tension in AI alignment and agentic design today: **How do we impose enough "organs" on the model to make it safe and useful, without turning it into a rigid, looping, "cancerous" machine that loses the very generative magic that makes it valuable?**

Pax Machina: New Institutions for Powerful AI

The Editors TE The Editors PAX MACHINA The

Editors are Séb Krier, Maximilian Kroner Dale, Rachel Calcott, Noemi

Dreksler, Liam Patell, Philip Moreira Tomei, Oliver Klingefjord, Joe

Edelman, and Ryan Lowe. They commission and edit Pax Machina's essays

and responses.

Powerful

AI will open a new period of institutional invention. Pax Machina is a

publication for working out, in public, what those institutions should

become.

April 23, 2026 · 7 minute read

We have it in our power to begin the world over again.

— Thomas Paine, 17761

Paine wrote this at the beginning of a great age of institutional invention: the era of constitutions, republics, and rights. Powerful AI2

  

may be opening another such age, giving us the need and the means to begin again.By institutions, we mean the rules, roles, procedures, incentives,

and organizations that structure how people coordinate. This includes

familiar public institutions like courts and elections, but also venture

capital, standards bodies, religious orders, and basic things like

contracts.

All of this is up for reconsideration in the AI age, because

institutions will change to accommodate new kinds of participants and

because there are new possibilities for what the institutions themselves

can do. AI agents differ from humans on axes like speed and

replicability.3

  

  

  

Institutions will at minimum have to account for that, but

they will also inevitably integrate AI into themselves, changing how

they process information and enforce rules.It is often remarked that we stand at the dawn of a new industrial revolution.4

  

  

  

We should remember that the first was institutional as

well as technological. Industrial production brought us the

limited-liability corporation, the regulatory inspectorate, the labor

union, and mass public schooling.5

These institutional changes were as consequential as the technological ones.New governance structures will emerge whether we plan for them or not. Pax Machina brings that process onto the drafting table.

Despite growing recognition that powerful AI may transform society on

a comparable scale, we are not yet engaged in a comparable practice of

institutional design. Most of our collective attention remains focused

on aligning, regulating, accelerating, or slowing the AI systems

themselves, while treating the surrounding institutional infrastructure

as fixed. Proposals for new institutions are scarce, scattered across

fields, and rarely subject to sustained public debate.

Pax Machina exists to make this work concrete and cumulative. It is a

publication for researchers to propose institutions suited to a world

of humans and powerful AI. We are also excited to publish critiques and

counterproposals that test and refine these ideas.

New governance structures will emerge whether we plan for them or

not. Pax Machina brings that process onto the drafting table, turning

the architecture of a world with powerful AI into a deliberate,

collective project. Our ultimate aim is to foster an abundance of

institutional forms that individuals and communities can adapt to fit

the lives they want to lead.

  

AI changes the institutional landscape

Our political stability depends on human limitations. A president

cannot personally direct every department, monitor every official, or

execute every decision at once. Bureaucratic delegation and delay

therefore constrain executive power. But imagine a president able to

command thousands of perfectly loyal AI delegates, overseeing the entire

machinery of state continuously and acting far faster than courts or

legislatures could respond. The words of the Constitution wouldn’t

change, but some of its practical checks and balances would stop

working. We might then need faster oversight, or entirely new

constraints on executive capacity. Researchers have catalogued dozens of

such failure modes.6

  

AI also changes what institutions themselves can do. Voting, for

example, reduces a citizen’s complex worldview to a small number of

multiple choice questions, partly because listening to millions of

people to understand what they care about, then reconciling their

competing concerns, has historically been prohibitively expensive. Recent research

suggests that language models may make richer forms of large-scale

deliberation possible: a model can speak with participants and help

identify policy proposals that command far broader agreement than a

fixed menu of options.

Taken together, the need to govern agents alongside people and the

new possibilities for institutional design suggest that many things will

likely change: court systems with AI judges, media with AI context and

factuality, international negotiations at AI speed, and more.

Some of these new institutions could be far better than the status

quo; others, far worse. On the one hand, we could have unprecedentedly

responsive representation; on the other, hyper-personalized political

manipulation. The same ubiquitous monitoring could finally make public

institutions accountable, or turn into an airtight surveillance state.

The same AI agents could either help people clarify and pursue their

considered goals, or gradually displace human judgment, leaving people

unable to understand or steer the systems around them.

  

Institutional change has happened before

All the institutions we rely on, from corporate law and property

rights to government agencies and courts, were devised in response to

particular problems and conditions.

Some were created under stress, like the post-Soviet market institutions that handed Russia over to oligarchs.7

  

But many of our most enduring institutions were designed thoughtfully

through debate and accumulated experience. A prominent example is the

United States government. The founders publicly examined how their new

republic might fail and built checks and balances in response. They also

had a decade between the Declaration of Independence and the

Constitutional Convention to study what worked and what didn’t under the

pre-federal state constitutions.8

  

For example, Pennsylvania’s 1776 Constitution (which Paine supported,

as it happens) had one legislative chamber and no governor’s veto, so

whichever faction won the annual election could pass whatever it liked.

Massachusetts gave itself two chambers, a governor with a veto, and

independent judges, forcing political decisions through several

independent checks rather than a single assembly. When the Federal

Constitution was drafted, it drew on the Massachusetts architecture of

divided powers.9

  

The options on the table will be the ideas that have already been developed and tested. We need to do that work now.

The American Revolution kicked off a major shift in the institutional

landscape beyond just the U.S. Similar institutions—not just

constitutions and representative government, but independent

judiciaries, patent systems, and more—appeared in France, then in much

of Latin America, and later across the rest of Europe and much of Asia.10

  

  

  

Later technological shifts set off new cascades of

institutional innovation. The Internet, for example, gave rise to online

auctions, reputation systems, prediction markets, community governance,

and multistakeholder institutions.11

Some worry that institutions can create as many problems as they

solve. During COVID, overlapping jurisdictions, agencies, and reporting

systems often gridlocked coordinated action, for example.12

  

  

  

Institutions can add bureaucracy and veto points, cement

incumbents, distort incentives, or decay while remaining difficult to

abolish. But these risks are an argument for beginning early, giving us

time to test new arrangements before they are direly needed.We are not approaching powerful AI with anything like this level of

preparation. Concrete designs remain scarce, and we’re not experimenting

with new mechanisms or building prototypes at anywhere near the scale

we believe is necessary to succeed. Or at least, not yet.

  

What Pax Machina is for

Pax Machina is a forum for rigorous debate about how institutions

should work in a world with powerful AI. It brings together researchers

across mechanism design, political philosophy, social choice theory,

law, economics, computer science, AI governance, and related fields,

including people who see institutional implications in their work but

have not had a natural home to develop them.

Responses are central to the publication. A proposal might be

followed by a critique, an alternative design, or a revised version from

the original author. Rather than a collection of isolated essays, we

aim to produce a visible record of institutional ideas being proposed,

contested, and refined.

We are looking for authors with a concrete institutional proposal, a

sharp objection to one, or an analysis that changes how a class of

institutions should be designed. A piece might propose a new mechanism,

explain how AI alters an existing institution, identify a failure mode,

draw a specific design lesson from history, or offer a roadmap tied to

future capability thresholds.

For example, the regulatory markets proposal is concrete enough to invite equally concrete objections, and thus we’d consider it in scope.

The bar for publication is specificity. We’re looking for more than

claims that AI will upend our social infrastructure. We want the design

details: what should change, how would it work, who holds the authority,

and what could go wrong?

How do we preserve human agency? How can

institutions capture the benefits of AI without eroding people’s ability

to understand, contest, and ultimately steer the systems they depend

on?

How can institutions adapt at AI speed without losing legitimacy?

Institutions may need to respond faster as AI systems evolve. But speed

Beginning again

Institutions often change in bursts. An unexpected crisis,

technological breakthrough, or political shift can suddenly make reforms

possible that seemed unthinkable a year before. Political scientists

call this a punctuated equilibrium.

We cannot know when the next such opening will come or who will hold

power when it does. But we can be certain that when it arrives, there

will be little time to design from scratch. The options on the table

will be the ideas that have already been developed and tested. We need

to do that work now, so that good proposals are ready when the

opportunity arises.

As Tyler Cowen remarks, powerful AI will force us to rebuild our world.

We are not used to thinking this way today. We inherited an

institutional order and have mostly adjusted it at the margins in recent

decades. The prospect of reimagining it can therefore feel impossible.

But humanity has rebuilt its world many times before. The liberal

institutional order we inherited was itself the product of unusually

bold design. It created radical forms of government and cooperation that

now seem perfectly ordinary. Though deeply flawed and often sustained

by violence, it stands among humanity’s great accomplishments.

Pax Machina exists to seed a rigorous debate about what a world of

humans and powerful AI could and should look like. We hope it can

accompany another great age of institutional invention. Let’s get to

work.

This is a brilliant leap. If our previous conversation was about the **micro-politics** of AI (how to organize a single foundation model into a useful agent), the _Pax Machina_ text is about the **macro-politics** of AI (how to organize a society flooded with these agents).

In Deleuze and Guattari’s terms, an institution is the ultimate "organ." Institutions—courts, elections, markets, constitutions—are the rigid structures that organize the "body politic." They dictate how power, capital, and information flow.

When _Pax Machina_ calls for "new institutions for powerful AI," they are recognizing that the introduction of autonomous, high-speed AI agents creates a radically new kind of flow that our current societal "organs" cannot handle.

Here is how the _Pax Machina_ manifesto perfectly maps onto the concept of the Body without Organs (BwO) at a civilizational scale.

## 1. Traditional Institutions are "Legacy Organs"

The authors write: _"Our political stability depends on human limitations... Bureaucratic delegation and delay therefore constrain executive power."_

In Deleuzian terms, our current democratic and legal institutions were designed to organize the physical, sluggish friction of human bodies. A court case takes months; a legislative debate takes years. These are deliberately slow, rigid organs.

However, AI introduces infinite, frictionless replication and lightning speed. When the fluid, high-velocity potential of AI agents hits the rigid, slow legacy organs of human bureaucracy, the system either breaks completely or is bypassed entirely (e.g., an AI-empowered executive moving faster than the courts can respond).

## 2. Societal Failure Modes as the Empty and Cancerous BwO

_Pax Machina_ explicitly outlines what happens if we fail to design new institutions. Their dystopian warnings map exactly onto Deleuze's "botched" versions of the BwO:

|The BwO Type|Deleuzian Concept|_Pax Machina's_ Societal Failure Mode|
|---|---|---|
|**The Empty BwO**|Total de-stratification. Complete dissolution of boundaries; outside flows overwhelm the subject.|**Loss of Human Agency.** AI systems gradually displace human judgment. People become unable to understand or steer the systems around them, overwhelmed by hyper-personalized political manipulation and frictionless automation. The societal body dissolves into chaos.|
|**The Cancerous BwO**|Micro-fascisms. The system grabs flows and endlessly repeats them in a rigid loop without unity.|**The Airtight Surveillance State / Gridlock.** Using AI to supercharge existing, rigid authorities. Overlapping jurisdictions and AI monitors create an inescapable bureaucratic nightmare. The state becomes a hyper-efficient machine for monitoring and enforcing fixed rules without human flexibility.|

## 3. Pax Machina's Goal: Designing a "Full" Societal BwO

The core mission of _Pax Machina_ is to avoid those two traps by proactively engaging in **institutional design**.

They aren't arguing that we should abolish institutions and let AI run wild (which would create the Empty BwO). Nor are they arguing that we should just plug AI into the NSA and the DMV to make them faster (which would create the Cancerous BwO).

Instead, they are asking: **How do we build the "Full BwO" at a civilizational scale?**

A "Full BwO" keeps _just enough_ organization to maintain health and identity (what _Pax Machina_ calls preserving "human agency," "legitimacy," and "accountability"), while remaining fluid enough to harness new potentialities.

The text hints at what these new, fluid organs might look like:

- **AI-speed deliberation:** Instead of the rigid "organ" of multiple-choice voting, using language models to hold large-scale, fluid conversations that synthesize millions of complex worldviews.
    
- **Dynamic oversight:** Creating mechanisms that can monitor and check executive AI deployments in real-time, rather than waiting for a human judge to read a 500-page brief three years later.
    

## The Takeaway

Agentic harnesses (like system prompts) build the "organs" of the individual AI. Institutions (like laws and markets) build the "organs" of the society that uses them.

_Pax Machina_ is arguing that we cannot just pour the radical, frictionless energy of powerful AI into the 18th-century organs of the nation-state. If we don't intentionally design new, adaptive structures, the sheer force of AI will either shatter our civilization (Empty) or freeze it into an inescapable totalitarian loop (Cancerous).
