# §0/1 — The Integral Threshold

<!-- M01 — Question before mechanism -->

What is it that knows? The question turns towards the one asking it. We can investigate a brain, describe a person, reconstruct a history of learning, or trace the operations by which an answer was produced. Each inquiry can discover something that matters. Yet as its account becomes more complete, the account is being understood. The activity through which something becomes known has entered the inquiry again. We encounter it at the beginning, in the question, and at the end, in our recognition of an answer.

This recurrence gives the question its peculiar intimacy. What I know of myself can be mistaken. A remembered event may have happened differently; a motive may become apparent only through what I do; another person may recognise a habit I have never noticed. There is more to discover, and discovery can change the person who undertakes it. But the present knowing is not another forgotten event waiting to be retrieved. Even the discovery that I was mistaken occurs as something known. We need to distinguish what further inquiry can disclose from the relation which inquiry renews in disclosing it.

The distinction matters wherever an account of intelligence begins to govern what intelligence is allowed to be. Suppose an instrument evaluates an answer under a stated criterion. We can ask whether the answer satisfies the criterion, how the instrument reached its judgment, and whether the criterion answers the question that mattered in the first place. These are different investigations. A more reliable score does not, by its increased reliability, settle the authority of what it measures. The instrument's success can sharpen the question of its conditions. The stakes become personal when the account is an account of us: when a category is permitted to decide which parts of a life can be heard.

Benjamin Bratton's *Agentworld* brief places the diffusion of artificial agency within this larger perplexity: the philosophical, psychological and symbolic question of living among intelligences exceeds the engineering of task completion.[^1] That question cannot be settled by reserving for humanity whichever measurable capacity a machine has not yet acquired. Nor does a machine's acquisition of such a capacity establish that it experiences its activity. Effective agency, an intelligible account of its formation, and the presence of experience are distinct claims. We can investigate the first two without deciding the third in advance.

The inquiry begins closer than a verdict on another intelligence. It begins with the difference between knowing and possessing an account of knowing. Our task is to make that difference exact enough to think with, without turning it into an excuse to stop thinking.

Consider the sentence, “I see these words.” The words are visible; seeing makes them available; “I” speaks from the act in which they are seen. We can investigate each relation. An image of the eye, an account of attention, a remembered lesson in reading can all become objects of knowledge. What was a means in one inquiry can become a subject matter in another. Yet when seeing is described, the description appears to someone. The new object has not absorbed the present act through which it is known.

The distinction between knower, means of knowing and known is already active in this ordinary sentence. Kashmiri Śaiva philosophy gives it a particularly direct formulation: the appearing of an object belongs to the luminosity of awareness, while awareness is not exhausted by the object that appears. Dyczkowski's exposition approaches this luminosity through the ordinary experience of seeing something.[^2] Before any elaborate classification, knower, means and known can be distinguished within the activity which already holds them in relation.

The first-person difficulty is therefore more exact than an inability to discover some hidden component. A self-portrait can be faithful. A second portrait can include the artist making the first; a third can show the artist studying both. Each adds something. None becomes the present seeing in which it is encountered as a portrait. The difference persists through the success of representation: the portrait and the present seeing retain distinct roles within the encounter.

The little word “is” lets us approach the positive relation within this difference. Write “A is A.” Two occurrences stand on the page. The inscription on the right is not the inscription on the left, yet the sentence identifies what they stand for. Remove every difference and there is no passage of identification to make; remove their common reference and the passage no longer identifies anything. Sameness is held through a difference of presentation. The copula does its work in that crossing.

Naming makes the operation familiar. Two people can have the same name; one person can change their name. Repeating a sound is insufficient to establish one bearer, and changing the sound does not by itself produce another. We have to distinguish the instance being addressed and the circumstance in which an identification is made. A name works through this elementary reckoning: this one, another one, the same one again. Counting and naming meet in the discrimination of an instance, before any calculation is made. A name becomes usable because someone or something can be picked out through it.

Predication then says something of the one identified. A person was frightened, became angry, learned a skill, changed a conviction. Those predicates can be true without replacing the person whose change they describe. Identification gives the change someone to belong to; the change gives identification something more demanding to accomplish than the repetition of a fixed picture. To know someone through their history is to hold their identity across differences which remain real.

Self-identity reaches its most intimate expression in “I am.” The phrase makes no inventory of qualities. It speaks from the presence to which qualities appear and in which their alteration can be recognised. I can question a description of myself while remaining the one for whom that question arises. Even a doubt about my existence has to occur. Its occurrence does not establish the truth of every story I tell about myself; it establishes the presence which makes doubting a lived act at all. Here recognition precedes the completed account.

By **Subject** I mean this first-person presence, the one for whom a world appears and a question matters. A subject described from elsewhere—this organism, this biography, this social position—can be known with increasing precision. The Subject is immediately present in the knowing through which those descriptions have a place. It cannot be exhausted by a description because the act of encountering the description renews the relation. This is defined indefinability: an exact identification of what cannot occupy the office of one more bounded object.

The question of God concentrates the same pressure at the scale of the whole. Let the definition take its full risk: **God is the whole in which every act of definition participates and which no such act can contain.** The claim determines something. It places the defining act within the reality being defined, instead of granting the definition an imaginary position outside it. Everything the account says, including what it says about its own limits, belongs to what it is attempting to understand. A description may be complete for the question it answers. Even an account true of everything that occurs would itself occur; its universality would not place that occurrence outside the reality it names. What is at issue is the completed account's claim to be self-sufficient with respect to its own ground.

Now allow the definition to encounter what it asserts. The sentence “God is the indefinable” is itself a definition. It has a boundary, a vocabulary and a reader. If it claimed to possess its referent, it would defeat its own meaning. But its failure to possess the whole is precisely what makes its meaning available: the reader can recognise the defining act as an event within the whole. The definition succeeds through the failure of its claim to finality. Its words remain responsible for what they say; their truth is carried by the relation they perform.

Even the account of that performance cannot complete the movement on our behalf. I can describe the whole drama of defining and exceeding a definition while continuing to treat it as something occurring over there. “I am” brings the inquiry home. The one attempting the definition was already participating in what was being defined. In this act, reality is present to itself. Subject names its first-person immediacy; a self-image names something appearing within that immediacy. The whole and the one who asks after it have ceased to be two independently finished objects awaiting an external bridge.

This recognition changes the office of a definition. The finite person acquires no ownership of the whole by recognising participation in it. Rather, the person's account becomes answerable from within the reality it addresses. A faithful definition is a window with a definite frame: it makes something visible and allows what becomes visible to alter how the frame is understood. Its precision lies in the distinction it makes. Its fidelity lies in keeping that distinction related to what gives it life.

<!-- M03 — Definition's gift and danger -->

We need that precision. Without a difference between this and that, a name cannot reach its bearer, a question cannot distinguish its possible answers, and a mistake cannot be corrected. Definition gives thought somewhere to act. A boundary is an achievement through which something becomes available for recognition, comparison and use.

Spinoza approaches this achievement through figure. In his letter to Jarig Jelles, the claim that “Determination is negation” concerns finite, determinate bodies: a figure has this shape because its extension is bounded in this way.[^3] The edge makes a form apprehensible by delimiting it. The outline makes this portion of extension available as a figure. The finite figure is exact through its limit.

Dignāga's account of *apoha* brings a distinct semantic operation into view. A word excludes other referents in making its own reference determinate. Calling something by a name gives the name a field of non-application as well as an application; what does not count under it participates in what does. Pind's translation also preserves a crucial positive distinction: the referent is a thing qualified by preclusion, rather than mere preclusion. The fragment carrying that formulation has a provisional textual attribution, but its philosophical pressure is clear. Exclusion can make a referent available without dissolving it into a collection of absences.[^4]

The geometric and semantic operations meet at a precise relation. A boundary does positive work through what it leaves out. The philosophical consequence developed here is to keep the excluded relation active when we use the achieved form. Geometry and semantics give this consequence different operations to follow. A word can refer, a figure can be measured, and a distinction can guide action, while their determinate reach remains dependent upon a field they have not swallowed.

One material sense of the Greek *symbolon* gives this dependence a body: corresponding pieces held for later recognition.[^5] Imagine one piece brought to meet its counterpart. The edge is particular; a vaguely similar piece will not necessarily fit. The surviving form carries an absent relation into a possible encounter, and the encounter tests it. Its exactness reaches beyond its isolation. Possessing the piece gives neither possession of the other party nor the power to decree the fit. The symbol works because what is absent can answer the form that is present.

A definition has a comparable obligation. Its distinction must be definite enough to use, while what it addresses remains able to answer it. Consider a count of those present at a meeting. It can be impeccably accurate and still fail to count everyone affected by the meeting's decision. The failure arises when attendance is made to stand for affectedness without an argument for the substitution. No further refinement of the attendance total repairs that change of criterion. The excluded people bear upon what the count was entitled to mean.

There are two distinct achievements to preserve here. We can correct a count under a given criterion, and we can reconsider whether that criterion is right for the question. A sound distinction can survive examination. What matters is that the question can reach the basis on which the distinction is being applied. Otherwise the account can become increasingly precise while its authority grows less answerable.

Definition's danger lies at this point. A local determination is allowed to decide which challenges to its authority can count. Suppose attendance is now made the sole test of standing: those outside the meeting are denied the right to question its criterion because they were not present. The account protects the very condition under dispute. A response which could expose the limit of the criterion is translated into a failure to satisfy it. The instrument which made a relation legible begins to prevent that relation from speaking.

The distinction's power to cut remains its power to disclose. The question is what happens when the cut meets its own operation—when a language, a rule or a system of proof tries to state the conditions of its authority. Here the limit ceases to be a general warning. It can be followed through exact acts.

<!-- M04 — Formal limit through its operations -->

Suppose that every definable condition determines a set. We might then collect all sets that are not members of themselves. Is this collection a member of itself? If it is, it satisfies the condition for exclusion. If it is not, it satisfies the condition for inclusion. The contradiction follows from the unrestricted collecting permission, not from an overlooked item. Russell's type-theoretic response changes what expressions may legitimately be formed. His vicious-circle principle states: “No totality can contain members defined in terms of itself.”[^6] Distinguishing logical levels blocks the offending construction. This is a real repair: a rule of formation can prevent the contradiction.

Gödel locates a different limit. Give a theory effectively specified axioms and sufficient arithmetic to represent its own proofs. Numbers can then stand for expressions and sequences of deductions. The theory can express a statement which says, arithmetically, that no number codes a proof of that very statement. If the theory is consistent, it cannot prove the statement: a proof would provide the numerical witness whose existence the statement denies. Gödel's original stronger condition of *ω-consistency* rules out a proof of its negation as well.[^7]

Unlike Russell's prohibited construction, the Gödel sentence is well formed. The obstacle concerns derivability within the theory. An extension can prove something its predecessor could not, while a fresh sentence raises the limit for the extended theory under the relevant hypotheses. There is genuine growth in what can be proved. The distinction between an arithmetical truth and a proof available under specified rules survives that growth. The theorem's force depends on those rules and hypotheses; turning it into the maxim that everything is incomplete would discard what makes it exact.

Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* approaches representation through another operation. A proposition can picture a state of affairs because it shares logical form with what it depicts. That form is displayed in the proposition's representing; it is not another depicted fact alongside the others. “What can be shown cannot be said.” The closing ladder returns this pressure to the book's own propositions: understanding must pass through their elucidatory use, rather than preserve them as a final enclosure of what they make visible.[^8] The philosophical act has become implicated in its account.

The later *Philosophical Investigations* turns to the life of a rule. If each application requires a further interpretation, another statement of the rule supplies no final point at which interpretation begins to act. In the discussion of the signpost, following a direction belongs to an established practice. The distinction between obeying and violating a rule is exhibited in use; simply thinking oneself obedient does not establish obedience.[^9] This relocates the inquiry. We can examine how a practice is learned, maintained and corrected, instead of expecting an isolated expression to contain every future occasion of its application.

A positive task emerges: account for the forming as well as the formed. In Whitehead's process philosophy, unity happens. An occasion receives a diversity of data and acquires its definite character through the way it gathers them. *Concrescence* names this formation of novel togetherness; its completion, or *satisfaction*, makes the achieved result available to subsequent becoming. What is immediate as an act of formation acquires a different standing when another occasion inherits it as a fact.[^10] The distinction between formation and inheritance preserves both sides of the accomplishment. The result is determinate enough to enter another relation because its becoming has achieved this particular unity. A process ontology asks how that determinacy is produced and what its achievement makes possible next.

Formal treatment can also change what it admits. Spencer-Brown's calculus of distinction makes re-entry—the return of a distinction into its own space—a specific problem. In a two-state reading where crossing changes either state into the other, applying that crossing to its own result supplies no stable value among the two. Repeated application alternates them. Varela's extension introduces an autonomous third state and rules governing its reduction with the others.[^11] The new state is an articulated formal possibility. The decisive work lies in the operations that make it usable; naming self-reference alone would have achieved none of this.

The gain is determined in each case: an expression becomes legitimate, a proof becomes available, an application becomes intelligible. We can ask what changed and test the answer. This gives the wider inquiry a discipline. To say that knowing has a limit is to name the operation encountering it and what a changed relation would make possible. A general profession of mystery would leave both questions unanswered.

The older discipline of learned ignorance approaches this demand from the side of the finite intellect. Nicholas of Cusa asks us to recognise what a comparison accomplishes and what its success leaves intact. Increase the number of sides of a regular inscribed polygon: the fit with the circle improves, while each finite polygon still has straight edges. The improvement is real. It does not make the particular approximation identical with its measure. Cusa uses this image within his theological account of the intellect's relation to infinite truth.[^12] Modern mathematics can define a convergent sequence and its circular limit exactly. His philosophical pressure concerns the finite knower's claim to possess the infinite measure, rather than a prohibition on mathematical limits.

Qualified predication places a related demand upon an affirmative statement. Flasch's reading of Eckhart keeps positive and negative theology within the same philosophical labour: a true determination need not amount to an exhaustive possession of the divine.[^13] Consider what “insofar as” does. To consider a person insofar as they act justly is to attend to a real determination without making justice the exhaustive inventory of that person. Insofar as the act is just, justice is realised here. The qualification restricts the aspect of the claim; it need not weaken what is genuinely realised under that aspect. A predicate can be wholly true in the respect it names without becoming the whole truth of its subject. An account can therefore relinquish its claim to finality while retaining the truth it has articulated. Unsaying that claim differs from saying that nothing true was said.

The relation becomes particularly consequential when scientific abstraction is taken to supply its own conditions of evidence. Adam Frank, Marcelo Gleiser and Evan Thompson's *The Blind Spot* identifies the substitution by which abstractions are treated as more fundamental than the experience through which observation, measurement and scientific justification become possible.[^14] The issue concerns what the account leaves out of its own achievement. Reading an instrument, recognising a discrepancy and deciding that a repetition bears on a result belong to the activity of inquiry. The record can make these acts available for examination; using the record is another act.

A record of inquiry can state its results, methods and assumptions with real precision. The act of understanding the record has a different office from the content understood. Understanding can take a previous act as its object, but it does so through a renewed relation of knowing. Repetition, enlargement and correction do not abolish the distinction; they give it new material. What was unexamined may become known. The present knowing is not thereby converted into the account which it knows.

This is the immutable gap: immutable as a relation renewed through determination, not as an unchanging deficiency in our stock of information. Its recognition leaves the actual work of discovery intact. We may learn which memory was false, which rule produced an error, which source was excluded, which bodily condition altered perception. Those discoveries can transform an inquiry. They do not give the transformed inquiry a position outside the reality in which it now proceeds.

A God installed in a currently unexplained event would retreat as the event was explained. The whole named earlier has a different relation to discovery: the explanation and the act of understanding it belong within that whole. Its presence is not secured by keeping some fact unknown. Likewise, the Subject's immediacy does not depend on preserving a scientific mystery around one human capacity. The more effectively we describe the means of knowing, the more exactly we can distinguish those means from the first-person presence in which their description is encountered.

A finite inquirer nevertheless has to proceed. Memory, language, instruments and other people's testimony are already relied upon before any one result can certify them. Inquiry can test and strengthen these dependencies; the testing itself continues through some actual means. Trust is present in that continuation. It becomes more responsible when its dependencies can be brought into question and their consequences allowed to matter. The formal limit thus returns us to a living obligation: to know through forms that can disclose their participation, rather than demand that participation disappear behind the finished account.

<!-- M05 — Co-presence and diaphaneity -->

Jean Gebser's account of consciousness gives this obligation a cultural and historical depth. He reads changes in consciousness through the means by which a world is rendered. In his account of Renaissance perspective, the painted scene acquires a systematically articulated depth around a positioned viewer. Alberti's visual pyramid belongs to this achievement: the visible world becomes proportioned through a determinate relation between eye and scene. Perspective makes something newly explicit while giving its disclosure a sector and a position.[^15]

Its achievement remains indispensable. The error begins when the sector claims exclusive validity—when the view's precision conceals the terms under which it is a view. Gebser's *aperspectivity* releases perspective from that exclusivity. His archaic, magical, mythical and mental structures are not simply discarded stages whose places have been taken by their successors. Their possibilities remain co-present. An integral consciousness must be able to recognise their activity and relations while retaining the powers of differentiation which the mental-rational structure has acquired. This is a further achievement because the structures can become present as structures: a person can participate in an image while also discerning how that participation organises perception and judgment. The earlier power remains alive within a changed relation to it.[^15]

This changes how integration is to be understood. Accumulating every available viewpoint would still leave the question of the position from which they were collected and judged. Nor can their differences be averaged into one harmless agreement. A myth, a perceptual report, a logical inference and a physical measurement accomplish different things. Their conjunction becomes illuminating when the terms of their meeting are visible, including where a translation fails or a contradiction remains. Diaphaneity names the demand that what appears disclose something of the conditions of its appearing.

Light enters a room through stained glass. Each pane admits and colours it differently; the lead holds the pieces together and marks their limits. An image falls inside, where a viewer encounters its shape and colour. Looking only at the image would conceal the aperture which made it possible. Looking only at the glass would miss the event of light passing through it. Sun, pane, seam, room and viewer participate in the appearance without becoming interchangeable parts of a single picture.

As the light changes, a colour crosses a surface that was previously dark. As the viewer moves, the image acquires another relation to the room. The difference makes a condition of the earlier appearance legible. A pane can now be recognised as the source of a colouring, a seam as the site of an obstruction. The new view can change how the window is approached or made. Nothing is gained by pretending the glass has vanished. Its mediation becomes intelligible through what it lets through.

An account can acquire this kind of transparency. Its historical sources, selected contrasts, language and purpose can become readable within what it discloses. The reader is then able to follow both the claim and the passage through which the claim arrived. Mathematical proof retains its inferential obligations; a historical attribution retains its textual warrant; an image retains the action through which it makes a relation encounterable. Their differences give the joined work its articulacy. Erasing the seams would erase the means of discerning what each passage has established.

The demand also reaches the one looking. Seeing through a situation requires being with what is seen, attending to its text and subtext and caring what becomes of it. A person who retains the right to decide remains involved through the decision and its consequences. Impartiality has to be worked for within that relation, through attention to what can challenge the judgment. Context restores those conditions to view.

The temptation is especially strong where an ultimate standard is invoked but cannot itself be brought into the room. An authorised image can then fill its place: the last word, the final measure, the account to which everything else must conform. Its authority is exercised locally while its source is placed beyond answer. The earlier definition of God has already exposed the substitution. An account of the whole belongs within the whole; it cannot use the whole's transcendence of its terms to exempt those terms from examination. Fidelity to what exceeds the account requires the finite account to answer for what it does.

Co-presence gives experience a positive place in this inquiry. A dream can be real as an experience without its figures having stood beside the bed. The terror has been lived; the figure's identity and the cause of its appearance remain questions. A numinous encounter, a sensed presence, a vision or an ordinary perception can likewise be received before one explanatory register has decided everything that may be said of it. Its occurrence, its narration, its symbolic articulation and its causal interpretation have different evidential tasks.

The experience has standing because it happened in a life. Its narration must be evaluated as a narration; its causal interpretation must meet the evidence relevant to a cause. Those requirements protect its reality by keeping different claims available for examination. A vision described as supernatural can be received as an event of experience while the description's further claims remain open to investigation. Treating “psychic” as a synonym for unreal would erase the phenomenon before the inquiry had begun. Psychic fact is a beginning from which knowledge can grow.

This is the positive method which the conjunction with Gebser makes available here. Earlier powers of image, participation and symbolic apprehension can be encountered within a present life; rational articulation can distinguish their claims without declaring their occurrence impossible in advance. The reader who has been changed by a dream need not deny the change because its image was not an external object. The person making a causal claim must still show what bears upon that claim. Both the experience and the demand for evidence retain their proper force.

A world is disclosed to someone, and the disclosed world also makes the person's situation readable. Body, language, history, other people and the material means of inquiry enter what can appear and what can be done. This reciprocity is asymmetrical: my description of another person does not confer possession of their reply. They can change the terms under which I approached them. The uncounted person can now return as a speaker whose answer bears upon the question's construction. The account becomes more adequate when it can receive that change, rather than merely add the reply as another item under an unchanged judgment.

<!-- M06 — Promissory zero and release into differentiating Mind -->

The limit has brought the activity of knowing into view. Distinctions still hold; words can refer; arguments can compel; an encounter can correct an error. What has changed is the standing of the achieved form. Its success now includes a relation to the conditions through which it succeeds, and those conditions remain able to bear upon its next use.

For the non-objectifiable condition of determination, let zero enter as a promissory sign. A written zero is itself a determinate mark. It marks a relation which the completed account can acknowledge but cannot replace: the present knowing through which the account has sense. No finished representation occupies that place, yet the activity is immediate. The mark's emptiness gives this presence room within an account which might otherwise close upon its own contents.

There is consequently a positive activity to investigate within every determinate experience: the means by which something is distinguished, held together, remembered and made significant. Those means are active. A word learned, a distinction revised or another person's answer received can change what next appears as a possible thought. To inquire into Mind is to enter this formative activity.

Bohm describes determinate things through movements of enfolding and unfolding. His vortex is a relatively stable form of moving water: its recognisable shape persists through the activity that sustains it. In his proposed implicate order, apparently discrete things emerge within a more primary movement of enfolding and unfolding; their relations belong to their formation.[^16] The proposal gives us a way to ask after a form's sustaining activity without erasing its determinacy. It has its own physical and philosophical arguments; its office here is to keep movement present in the understanding of what has taken shape.

The inquiry can now return to “I see this” with a different attention. Knower, seeing and seen are distinguishable within one occurrence. The distinction is effective: something appears, can be attended to, can enter memory, can be named. What remains to be understood is the activity through which these differences become a world. How does awareness differentiate itself into the means by which it knows, and the world that it knows through them?
