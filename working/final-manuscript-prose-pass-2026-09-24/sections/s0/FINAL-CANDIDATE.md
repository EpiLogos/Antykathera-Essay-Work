# §0 — Differentiating Mind

The defining recognition at the threshold left an intimacy that no improved description of means could manufacture. What remains is to differentiate the intimacy of knowing from the formations, instruments, and positions through which a life knows and acts, keeping the means real without letting them occupy the knower’s office.

## M07 — Awareness becomes articulate

A voice calls my name. Until that moment it belonged to the surrounding sound; now it addresses me. I turn. A familiar face comes forward, a remembered conversation gives the call its urgency, and the place through which I was passing becomes a place in which something is asked of me. Before I can invent an account of what happened, the street has already changed its shape: what I notice, what I am about to do, and who I am in relation to the speaker have shifted under the address. The call reorganises the field in which anything can matter as information; it does not first deliver a finished packet for later interpretation.

An inventory of sounds and shapes leaves the encounter unexplained. I know the voice as mine to answer, the face as someone already known, the memory as belonging to an earlier exchange rather than floating free among other noises. That knowing is already at work in the materials being present as *this* encounter. It does not arrive afterward as a report filed over finished data. The call has bent awareness toward a display that includes the one who turns—and inside that work I can still err. I may mishear the tone, import an old grievance, or prepare a reply before the request is finished. The same activity that makes the street articulate also makes misreading possible. Correcting the response will require more than a sharper ear: the identification that converts every word into the same injury must become distinguishable in the functions that settle, appropriate, and steer what has appeared.

Appearing is luminous: voice, face, and recollection are manifest, and the luminosity reaches sound and memory that no physical light illuminates. Yet appearing would leave experience unaccounted for if it were only an inert display—a crystal receiving a configuration without knowing its own surface. Awareness apprehends its presentations from within their display: it retains them, distinguishes them, composes them, rather than waiting for a second observer to inspect a finished picture. The Kashmiri account names these powers *prakāśa* and *vimarśa*: luminosity and the reflexive self-apprehension through which luminosity is present to itself. Their distinction arises inside one activity; light is not first posited and only later supplied with a reflecting faculty.[^s0-1]

A further mirror announcing that I have heard would only postpone the demand. The announcement itself can appear, yet I would already need the knowing it was supposed to produce. Reflective commentary is one determinate activity within awareness. Self-apprehension is the power through which commentary and its object are present together. When I think explicitly about knowing, I make a further determination; I do not invent the fact that the thought is known. Stacking observers behind observers tries to manufacture from outside what already belongs to the appearing. Inward self-awareness and outward reflection upon an apparently separate object orient the same act; they do not establish two independent consciousnesses. The object-directed act remains known within the subject.

Blue and yellow keep the world’s detail inside one field of seeing. Were unity to erase those colours, nothing determinate could appear; were each sealed from the other, their contrast could never be apprehended. Awareness holds them by differentiating them. Manifestation and its apprehension belong to one active relation. The appearing world is internal to consciousness without becoming the possession of a particular person’s image of it. The ordinary mirror’s office of reproducing an arrangement is therefore exceeded: what is required is a living display that knows its presentations while they remain differentiated.[^s0-2]

The earlier conversation need not stay vividly pictured all day to return when the voice calls. Retention, recollection, and recomposition into an anticipated exchange do different work. Retention makes something available beyond its first occurrence; recollection presents it again as referring to an earlier occasion; imagination changes its arrangement into an anticipated exchange. A recorded sentence can persist on a page without anyone presently understanding it. Remembering the sentence as something this person said to me is another act: the present appearance carries a relation to an earlier occasion, and that relation permits correction. The speaker can remind me of what came before the words I retained, and what I remember can change its significance without changing into an event which never happened. I may misremember the order of what was said; the correction still finds me in the knowing that received both versions. Recollection is known as referring back. It is neither the past returned bodily nor a present picture whose pastness can be taken for granted. When the anticipated conversation begins, remembered materials are composed into an exchange which has not yet occurred. Confusing those operations would give an imagined accusation the authority of a remembered one.[^s0-3]

Concern, sense taking form, inward phrasing, and audible succession do different work within one utterance. An interruption may change the phrasing while the concern persists; an answer may change the understanding from which the next sentence begins. A sound becomes an address through who speaks, who is addressed, and what is at issue. The fourfold account of *Vāk* distinguishes depths in that articulation: *Parā*, undivided expressive power prior to speaker, speaking, and spoken confronting one another as separate terms; *Paśyantī*, formative disclosure before meaning divides into a sequence of words; *madhyamā*, inward articulation; *vaikharī*, expressed succession. The supreme office is more than a sentence waiting silently to be pronounced. Abhinavagupta’s *Parātrīśikā-vivaraṇa* carries this descent as world-articulating power. Jaideva Singh’s English presentation makes that descent available as an encounter in which plural readings remain answerable to a rigorous text, and in which understanding is renewed participation rather than possession of a stored formulation—precisely the pressure under which a technique can forget its end and begin collecting means for their own sake. The grammatical persons of the encounter change with address: I, you, and the person spoken about are relations within the exchange, not three kinds of substance. Something becomes distinguishable, is gathered into meaning, and acquires an expression through which another encounter can change it. Manifestation enters language without being reduced to language.[^s0-4]

The name heard in the call places me in an address: someone is speaking to me, here, about something that may require an answer. The speaker’s I becomes the hearer’s you; either can become the person spoken about. Someone described in their absence as unwilling to help can, when addressed, answer that the earlier request never reached them. The reply changes the basis on which the portrait was made. It restores a capacity lost when the person became only the object of a description. Address keeps that capacity inside articulation itself.

The street is already differentiated, and the vortex met at the threshold—form sustained by movement—remains a neighbouring physical image of that dependence. It does not answer how differentiation is apprehended. What the call has forced open is the need for an articulable apparatus: judgment, appropriation, and coordination that can themselves be mistaken, revised, and released.[^s0-5]

## M08 — The inner instrument

I may know who speaks and still hear accusation where there is concern. The mistake may lie in the judgment that settles what the call means, in the identification through which it becomes a threat to me, or in attention already selecting confirming evidence. Immediate appearing and knowing remain what they were in the call; what must now be distinguished is the constituted apparatus through which that knowing is articulated, appropriated, and steered—an apparatus that can fail and be revised without the knower becoming one more repaired part among its parts.

*Buddhi* discriminates and settles a presentation as this rather than that. *Ahaṃkāra* appropriates: the determination acquires the form of I and mine—my concern, my injury, my achievement, my task. *Manas* coordinates sensory presentations, remembered associations, and possible responses, attention moving among them. Together they are the classical inner instrument (*antaḥkaraṇa*). In the *Sāṃkhya Kārikā* these functions belong to an instrument whose activity is distinct from witnessing *puruṣa*. The Śaiva field receives the instrumental distinctions into its own field of manifestation without adopting Sāṃkhya’s passive witness as its complete account of consciousness. Distinguishing their contributions lets inquiry do more than rename a final result: each function can be conditioned, reflected upon, and functionally decomposed, while the subject remains the pole under which those functions and their products appear.[^s0-6]

These functions are co-active. Hearing an insult may already involve an injured self-position; attention may search for evidence before a judgment is voiced. A correction of hearing must reach what was heard; a correction of judgment, the grounds on which it settled; a loosening of appropriation, the claim through which this event became a verdict on who I am. No little judge sits behind these functions directing them; that would only repeat the instrument inside itself.

Ahaṃkāra’s I-making remains distinct from Subjective Immediacy. The appropriation *this happened to me* can be noticed, doubted, and changed. Its image of who I am can be recalled from yesterday or projected into tomorrow. Those determinations are known; their possessive grammar does not make them the knower. I can relinquish an identification without the relinquishment becoming nobody’s experience. What changes is the constituted standpoint through which the encounter has been received.

Yoga makes the discrimination a disciplined inquiry. The *Yogasūtra* distinguishes the seer from identification with mental modifications and appoints practice and dispassion to their transformation. The instrument’s operation matters because it bears upon bondage and release.[^s0-7]

When the misheard call loosens, three changes can occur that must not be confused. The agitation may subside: I hear the rest of the sentence, tolerate a pause, and answer without the earlier haste, while the conviction that I have been wronged remains untouched—calm making the grievance more articulate and its presentation more persuasive. Or I may understand how the grievance formed—the remembered exchange, the expectation of another demand, the impulse to defend myself—and that understanding can alter the next response, or harden into a new possession: I become the one who understands the entire relation, including why the other person fails to understand it. A better account has become a more resilient self-image; its practical value shows only if an encounter can still correct it. Attention can include more than the object under judgment: a bodily tension may disclose how urgently a reply was being prepared; an earlier memory may disclose why these particular words sounded like a repetition. The judgment remains present while its circumstances become available with it. The inquiry has gained objects and relations within the means. It has not obtained a picture of Subjective Immediacy standing behind them.

Recognition turns on a different dependence. The threatened self and the perceptive self are both positions that can appear and be known. Perfecting one of them does not produce the knower for whom they differ. That fact is recognised in the act; it is not produced by adding a third, supposedly invulnerable portrait whose success must now be defended. A criticism can concern what I have said or done without deciding the reality of the knowing in which I receive it. Whether the criticism is true remains open—there may still be an injustice to resist, a promise to keep, or a mistake to repair—while the demand that a portrait secure being loses its necessity. An apology need not be an admission of non-being; a refusal need not establish a sovereign self beyond relation. The difference from improved self-description is practical as well as conceptual: the account of myself can serve the encounter instead of making the encounter serve its preservation.[^s0-26]

When the end of a practice is obscured, acquiring means can become the substitute end. The practitioner waits for the next technique to deliver the one who will finally be complete. Preparation can make recognition more available, as steadier attention can hear what agitation misses. What is recognised cannot be an object manufactured by the preparation, and recognising it does not finish the work of living. The instrument remains capable of cultivation, error, and repair.

Buddhist training presses a related limit from another side: appropriation around what appears is loosened so that seeing, hearing, sensing, and cognising are not seized as the possessions of a fabricated self-thing. That release does not require the Śaiva account of universal self-awareness, yet it bears directly on any attempt to turn the positive experient into a thing possessed by itself. (A verified house for the classical Bāhiya instruction remains an acquisition debt before any public quotation of its wording.)[^s0-8]

Language, bodily discipline, instruction, and memory participate in these changes. The word *inner* cannot mean sealed off from bodies, teachers, and a shared world. They help constitute the situation from which judgment proceeds. The instrument is internal as the means through which this life encounters its world, and objective because its operations can themselves become available for discrimination and change. Distinguishing those operations recovers a further pulse: alternatives must be formed and gathered into a course definite enough to act upon.

## M09 — An alternative becomes a course

A pause becomes possible where a defensive reply had seemed immediate. An apology, a question, and a refusal can be distinguished as courses with different consequences. An alternative alone does not carry an action through. Something must gather the relevant differences into an intention definite enough to follow.

*Vikalpa* articulates qualified alternatives: a possibility becomes thinkable as distinct from another. *Saṃkalpa* gathers selected relations into a formed resolve or course. An intention already discriminates the situation, the intended act, and what would count as carrying it out. Alternative formation already proceeds through concerns that make some differences consequential. Differentiation gives commitment its possibilities; commitment gives differentiation a direction and a cost.[^s0-9]

Before a promise is given, I consider what is being asked, the time available, other obligations, and what I can actually do. Once given, it changes these circumstances. Tomorrow’s hour is committed; a later request must be received in relation to it; another person can organise their activity in reliance upon my undertaking. An intention has entered the world as a condition of further intention. Its reality lies partly in this change of possibilities while its fulfilment is still to come. The promise already alters what can next be judged and done; fulfilment will answer that altered field, or fail it.

The course also inherits what it did not choose. To promise one kind of help may withhold another; accepting a time may displace a competing obligation. A responsible account need not preserve every thought that passed through the decision. It must retain the differences which make the commitment intelligible and the circumstances under which it would need to change. If the promised visit was meant to provide help, and the person now needs that help elsewhere, arriving at the original door may obey the retained instruction while failing the undertaking. Fidelity answers the purpose. A sequence of completed steps alone does not decide whether the help was given; intention must remain effective through changing circumstances, performance must alter what can next occur, and a result must answer to what was undertaken.

Habit is one achievement of this passage. A learned skill carries earlier discriminations forward so that each act need not begin again in uncertainty. The danger begins when the achieved course is insulated from its formation. A rule once fitted to a circumstance becomes the rule by which every circumstance is admitted or dismissed. Attention finds what it expects; memory retrieves confirmation; identification makes revision feel like an assault on the one who acts. This is script in its strict sense: conditioned will whose conditions have become unavailable to the will it directs. Useful acquired habit remains distinct from that arrest: the former carries formation forward so inquiry can continue; the latter conceals the alternatives from which the course was selected.[^s0-10]

Replacing a script’s final sentence can leave that relation intact. *This person is accusing me* might be replaced by *this person is not accusing me*, and the new formula may be equally deaf. If a real criticism is being made, compulsory reassurance prevents it from being heard. The useful correction follows the supposed accusation through the tone, the retained memory, the expectation, and the words actually spoken, until anticipation ceases to stand as sufficient evidence and a question becomes possible whose answer can decide what the formula could not.

Thought here works as medicine for a determination: it uses a distinction to loosen the necessity claimed by another distinction. Its success is a reopened encounter. *I must always remain open* could itself become a command which forbids a warranted refusal. When the corrective thought has restored the capacity to hear and judge, its office can end. The ability to relinquish it is part of what it has made possible.[^s0-27]

Patañjali gives *vikalpa* a narrower sense in *Yogasūtra* I.9: verbal cognition without a corresponding object. Elsewhere the question is whether relations are confined to conceptual construction or can express a real power of connection. The pairing developed here concerns the formation of an actionable field and asks how a formed cognition acquires authority over a course and how that authority can change—without attributing the entire pairing to a shared doctrine across schools.[^s0-11]

A reply can reveal that a remembered accusation was a misunderstanding, and the new event changes the living inheritance from which another judgment can arise. A standpoint may profess impartiality while leaving its investment in the outcome unexamined, already deciding whose answer will count. Context becomes effective when that participation, including its power over the other, enters what can be questioned.[^s0-14]

Openness acquires a discipline. An unfamiliar experience can be received as a report of something lived even where an established account has no ready category for it; what appeared, how it was understood, and what further claims follow remain distinguishable questions. Another person’s refusal must be capable of changing what I may do. Freedom enters the constitution of the field when an encounter can revise the terms through which the next encounter is judged. The chosen course must then disclose what its positive shape owes to what it has excluded.

## M10 — Exclusion within the positive

Calling an utterance a promise distinguishes it from a prediction, a hope, and an idle remark. The words alone may not settle the difference. The speaker’s undertaking, the circumstances of address, and the expectations reasonably formed by the hearer bear upon what occurred. The determination is positive: someone has undertaken something. Its content nevertheless depends on the alternatives from which it is distinguished. Remove those contrasts, and the same sounds no longer carry a determinate commitment.

The Buddhist theory of *apoha* gives this dependence a rigorous semantic form. A general term need not grasp a separately existing universal in order to refer to particulars. Its application is determined through exclusion of what does not answer to it. Ole Holten Pind’s critical translation of Dignāga locates the word’s work in this exclusion of other referents. The term’s positive reach is an achievement of distinction.[^s0-15]

A thing determined through preclusion differs from mere preclusion. A retained annotation in Pind’s work—its attribution to a further fragment provisional—speaks of a positive referent qualified by exclusion, distinguished from an absence with nothing answering to it. The cup from which someone drinks remains a positive referent. Exclusion gives the term a reach by determining where it applies. Positive particulars are already within the Buddhist account. Exclusion concerns their determination, not their replacement with mere absence.[^s0-16]

Semantic term-formation and the denial of a proposition are different operations. Cancelling a denial in propositional logic restores a truth value; that restoration does not make the contrasts through which a term acquired its application dispensable. The positive result can stand while the criterion of its distinction remains consequential.[^s0-17]

If an undertaking was received as freely given when refusal was never permitted, restoring the excluded possibility changes the relation between the parties. A command and a promise can result in the same outward conduct while differing in the conditions under which the conduct was formed. The completed action cannot, by itself, disclose those conditions. Exclusion need not persist as a conscious recital of every rejected possibility. It remains effective as a boundary of application, an expectation, a constraint on substitution, or a reason why this act rather than another occurred.

Return to the call and the earlier conversation. A causal imprint can explain why a present image occurs as a consequence of an earlier event; Buddhist accounts of continuity are substantive here and do not simply leave each instant disconnected. The further question concerns the image’s being known as a recollection of that event. What is present now is apprehended as referring to what is no longer present. Adding another image labelled *past* would leave the same demand: how are the label, image, and occasion apprehended together? The positive experient names the knowing in which that relation is enacted. It is not another retained item alongside the memory.

Comparison has a related demand. The present tone can differ from the remembered tone, just as the coat’s blue differs from the wall’s yellow. The distinction must remain, yet its terms must be held together for their difference to be known. An account which reduces unity to one content loses the comparison; one which confines each appearance to a self-sufficient instant has yet to account for their being compared. Reflexive awareness gives the differentiating unity a positive office. Awareness can know a change without making its changing contents identical.

Absence makes the same power apparent where no present object can do the required work alone. An unanswered call differs from a silence in which no answer was expected. The sounds may be alike; the situations are not. Memory of the address, an expectation, the passing opportunity, and the possibility of response make the missing answer consequential. Absence belongs to a positive field of relations. Exclusion can therefore be treated as an activity of knowing rather than requiring either a free-standing negative thing or a world of positive items to which the absence makes no difference. This receives the force of apoha without attributing to Buddhism the denial of positive particulars.

In keeping the promise, an intention must survive changes of place, attention, and circumstance; execution must alter what is available; a result must answer to what was undertaken. One step does not contain the whole walk, and a list of steps does not by itself say whether help was given. The unity is in the course through which intention, performance, and result are related. This gives agency more than the office of witnessing products already complete. Knowing and acting are powers of the experient. Their relation allows a consequence to disclose that the intended help has failed, and lets that disclosure change what is done next.

The call ends. Its ending can itself be known, and its having ended changes what may follow. Arising and perishing are appearances of change, not an encounter with a sheer nothing standing outside manifestation. The sound need not endure for its disappearance to be apprehended or its request to remain effective. The account must distinguish the changing content from the manifesting relation through which beginning, continuation, and ending are known. Consciousness here is not another moment of the sound, nor a fixed image preserved after it. It is the active ground of their appearing and apprehension.

Memory’s known reference, comparison’s held differences, absence’s consequential field, intention’s course through execution and result, and the apprehension of arising and perishing each demand positive places: an experient and its powers, recognition of manifestation as self-disclosing, and the capacity to exclude without turning exclusion into a free-standing negative substance. A passive witness separated from the instrument’s powers would leave their unity external to the witness; an inventory of passing contents would leave the knowing of their relations unexplained. The positive Śaiva relation serves this inquiry because it gives those powers their source without erecting a second observer behind them. For the field examined here, this is the more adequate account: its unity acts through the differences which remembering, comparing, and choosing continue to require. Adequacy names a criterion for this inquiry’s required operations, not a ranking of traditions in general.[^s0-28]

The Buddhist criticism of the fabricated proprietor remains binding within that adoption. Where analysis exposes the personal self as a construction that appropriates memory, intention, and experience, the criticism holds. I can mistake the self-image that appropriates the call; I can revise the history it claims; I can relinquish the position it defends. The encounter between Buddhist psychotherapeutic inquiry into selfing and the demand to keep anatta without erasing the one to whom experience is manifest makes the distinction decisive: criticism of a self-thing need not be treated as erasure of the experient. The Śaiva conclusion does not rescue the proprietor under a metaphysical title. It identifies the sourceward relation which the proprietor had claimed to possess. The strength of the claim lies in keeping its positive office while allowing the appropriation to fail.[^s0-29]

Huayan’s jewel net offers a neighbouring image of dependence without merging the traditions’ answers. A jewel in the southwest is selected; the others appear in it, and among their reflections the selected jewel appears again. Particularity makes the traversal possible. What seems locally contained discloses relations through which the containing jewel itself appears elsewhere. An ink mark on this jewel returns through the reflections without physically marking every jewel, and the teaching itself limits the optical simile: images enter one another while material substances remain distinct, whereas the interpenetration taught concerns phenomena more fully. Mutual inclusion is shown through a local entrance that still needs the many; neither the local jewel nor an undifferentiated totality can replace the action of the others.[^s0-18]

A course can have local unity while retaining the differences through which it formed and the relations through which it takes effect. Such relations must have an effective reach. This person can hear the call; this hour can be promised; this action can provide help which another cannot. Limits become the conditions of an inhabitable world.

## M11 — The world takes measure

A sound may be audible here and inaudible elsewhere; a remembered skill may make one task straightforward and leave another beyond reach. An intention can be sincere while the power to fulfil it is absent. Without these differences there would be no particular field in which something could be understood, wanted, attempted, or resisted. Differentiation needs an ordered anatomy: principles through which consciousness becomes manifest as an experienced world.[^s0-19]

The thirty-sixfold account is carried here through six turns of contraction and recognition—a compression that preserves the direction of dependencies rather than replacing the tradition’s enumeration, and without treating six turns, thirty-six principles, and the native eight-determination traversal as interchangeable counts. Outward, differentiation produces a determinate conscious position, world, and means: the call can be heard, the promise given, the hour measured, the hand reach. Rootward, recognition follows those determinations to their source without cancelling them: the powers that made hearing, promising, and reaching possible are known as powers. Return restores the means to use without letting a means claim to be the whole knower. The same field read in two directions is the crossed movement this section requires.

The opening remains *Anuttara*, the unsurpassable, or *Paramaśiva*: the condition within which knower and known arise. It cannot be placed above them as a largest object, since such a placement already employs the distinctions whose possibility it names. Its priority is enacted whenever a determination appears without exhausting the capacity through which it is known.

Śiva and Śakti articulate the first difference within that unity: consciousness and its power of manifestation. A luminosity incapable of manifesting or apprehending anything would leave no world to explain; a power wholly outside awareness would leave its effects unrelated to their appearing. The prakāśa–vimarśa relation remains active. Manifestation is consciousness becoming determinate without placing its forms outside the field in which they are known.

The next turn gives manifestation two orientations. In Sadāśiva, the appearing universe is held toward the I-pole; in Īśvara, the disclosed this comes forward within the same unity. Their conventional expressions, I am this and this am I, concern consciousness and manifestation at a universal level. They do not describe an ego claiming the universe as its property. The distinction precedes the finite appropriation examined in the inner instrument. The world can be read from the side of its appearing or from the side of the power by which it appears; the emphasis changes without severing their relation.

With *Sadvidyā*, I and this remain distinctly held together. With *Māyā*, that difference becomes effective as separation: the knower confronts something whose determination is required. Māyā gives measure to manifestation. This can stand forward while that is withheld; an object acquires a boundary and a standpoint acquires a reach. The world becomes tractable through these differences. Finitude has a productive office before it becomes a problem for recognition. Revealing and concealing are powers of the same field; the horizon is neither the unobjectifiable Subject nor a mere hole in a picture.

The *kañcukas* articulate the standpoint’s finite powers. *Kalā* limits efficacy: I can do this much. *Vidyā* limits knowledge: I know something while other things remain unknown. *Rāga* gives desire its directedness from a situation experienced as incomplete. *Kāla* makes temporal limitation effective: an opportunity comes, passes, or must be awaited. *Niyati* gives circumstance its determinate ordering, in which this act has these conditions and consequences. More information does not itself grant greater efficacy, and stronger desire does not remove a temporal limit. These contract different powers; they are not five names for one vague finitude.[^s0-20]

The bounded *puruṣa* inhabits those relations as a particular field of experience. Within the lower articulation, the three functions already distinguished belong alongside capacities of sensing and acting, sensory qualities, and embodied forms. The hand that reaches, the ear that hears, the memory that guides a response, and the object that resists it belong to an achieved situation. Keeping only cognition would omit the means by which an intention meets a world; keeping only the objects would omit their availability to a situated knower. To repair a cup requires this reach, this material, this before and after. Unlimited power is not an extra tool which must be acquired before the finite act becomes real.

This hour can be devoted to something because it has a boundary. A capacity becomes useful through a determinate relation to what it can affect. Each share nevertheless depends on an order it does not contain. The hour belongs to other commitments and other lives; the capacity depends on a body, acquired practice, and enabling circumstances. The error begins when a received share claims authority over the whole which makes it possible.[^s0-21]

An aperture gives a view its composition; a frame makes a particular presentation possible. A horizon concerns the wider field in which something can approach, recede, or become newly relevant. Walking changes what becomes visible. Another person’s reply changes what the situation asks. The frame usurps the horizon when whatever exceeds its present account is declared incapable of mattering.[^s0-22]

Within a dream, a path can lead somewhere, an encounter can matter, and fear can organise an entire situation. The condition of dreaming need not appear among that situation’s objects. Waking changes the relation of the experienced world to its condition; it does not make the experience something that never occurred. The waking person still encounters the demands and resistance of a world.

A passage between states is not already the recognitive return. In non-conceptual absorption—*nirvikalpa* in this comparison—the discriminations sustaining an ordinary problem may fall silent. While freedom is made dependent on keeping them silent, their return threatens the achievement. Speech, desire, or a difficult encounter can then be received as a loss of the state. Non-conceptuality alone does not establish recognition. It describes a condition of experience; it has not yet disclosed why that condition and its alteration are known.[^s0-30]

*Pratyabhijñā*, recognition, concerns the ground present through the difference. Quiet does not manufacture that ground, and a thought’s reappearance does not remove it. I recognise finite capacities as modes of a power they never independently originated. Judgment continues, appropriation can serve a situated life, and attention can coordinate an act. None has to become the whole in order to be real. Dependence is known amidst activity; activity need not disappear before dependence can be admitted. A steadier state may help me hear; a better account may help me understand; neither makes the one who knows arise as its product. Recognition frees these means from being required to secure their own ground. It does not free me from the promise or give my interpretation immunity. The other person remains able to correct me. Skills, habits, and obligations continue to require attention because a finite life still proceeds through them.

The scope of consciousness changes with the direction of this consideration. From a bounded standpoint, consciousness names what is present within a psychic field against an unconscious depth. A motive becoming conscious changes that distribution and can transform the field. From the sourceward direction, Consciousness names manifestation and its ground, including the dependence of this bounded division. The whole Self is not simply the present conscious standpoint enlarged until nothing remains hidden. Bringing a motive into view and recognising the condition of the view are different operations. The unconscious is neither abolished by the latter nor placed outside Life by the former.[^s0-31]

Descent orders the dependencies through which a finite world becomes workable; return makes those dependencies recognisable from within. A judgment can be followed toward the powers which formed it, and that recognition can change how the next judgment is made—provided the constitution through which the judgment proceeds can itself come into view without being mistaken for the knower.

## M12 — Objective Internality

Once contraction and recognition have made finite means intelligible, their collective office can be named without swallowing the knower. Language makes some distinctions readily expressible; memory makes earlier encounters operative; concern weights what matters; a body gives an act its reach and resistance. Other people speak, teach, promise, and refuse. Tools and institutions distribute capacities an isolated individual does not possess. Changing these relations changes what can appear, what can count as a reason, and what can actually be done. The mediating constitution through which a Life encounters and acts within a World is what this inquiry calls **Objective Internality**—earned here as the constituted means already under examination, not installed as a prior definition of Mind, and not confused with Subjective Immediacy merely because the means can become objects of reflection.[^s0-23]

Internality: judgment arises from an operative field of meanings, capacities, and concerns. Objective: those conditions have determinate relations and consequences which can themselves become objects of inquiry. Some can be reported, represented, compared, or altered; others remain tacit until an encounter discloses their activity. Objectivity does not require the whole interior to be exhaustively visible. What is disclosed must be distinguishable and answerable according to the relation at issue. Bodies, social arrangements, permissions, affect, and technical means can alter what is available; a representation of relations is not their entire real constitution.

A revoked permission shows the difference between this constitution and its description. A person may retain the same skill, goal, and memory, yet an action formerly available is no longer permitted. Conversely, a forgotten obligation may become effective again when the undertaking is recalled. In the first case an instituted relation changes the field of action; in the second, a retained memory returns to operative significance. A diagram might accurately record either change. It remains one means of making the constitution legible, rather than the whole constitution whose consequences it depicts. The field is notational through the marks by which something becomes addressable, nomological through learned regularities and permissions that constrain transitions, and exclusion-constituted because a determinate act selects against alternatives that remain conditions of its sense.

An account of a promise may contain the right words while leaving the promised person’s changed situation unable to affect the course. Adding a record of their reply does not repair this unless the reply can change what is authorised, attempted, or counted as fulfilment. The relation has to return into conduct. A world can be richly represented and still governed by an impoverished means of receiving it.

Subjective Immediacy names the knower, *pramātṛ*: that to which voice, recollection, judgment, and response are manifest. Objective Internality occupies the office of means, *pramāṇa*: the constitution through which something becomes available for knowing and action. World occupies the office of known, *prameya*: what is encountered through those means. Life, or Mind in the strong idealist sense developed here, names the whole relation. The means can themselves enter what is known without becoming the subject for whom the inquiry appears.[^s0-24]

These offices are relational. A remembered judgment can become the present object; the language used to examine it can itself be examined later. An inventory of examinable operations does not acquire Subjective Immediacy’s office by accumulation. Equally, the knower does not stand outside Life as an additional object looking in. Knowing is already this relation, whose differentiations permit both a world to be encountered and an account of its means to be corrected.

Pramāṇa also carries a question of warrant. Recollection, perception, and testimony may offer the same proposition while giving different reasons for accepting it. An occurrence within the means is not automatically valid knowledge. How does this cognition reach its object, what transformation does it perform, and where may it fail? Recollection can be checked against a record, testimony against its source, a perceptual judgment against a renewed encounter. The mode of access determines what correction must reach.

A *paradigm* is the lived organisation of these means. It governs what becomes salient, which differences count, what powers can be brought to bear, and how a consequence returns. An explicit worldview articulates part of that organisation. Someone can profess a new view while acting from the old distribution of attention, permission, and value. The paradigm changes when those constitutive relations change. Its representation can help by exposing the discrepancy between the declared view and its enactment.[^s0-25]

Making the instrument explicit should increase the freedom of its use. Treating the corrections presently representable in a model as the only possible corrections makes that model’s limits sovereign. A useful model makes particular dependencies investigable: which memory is being treated as evidence, which capability exists, whose permission matters, which result can require a change. The model’s completion is the completion of a means, not the completion of the life it serves.

Artificial agency gives this constitution a technical instance. Retained context, selected sources, capabilities, permissions, and ongoing encounters can form an operative world from which a system distinguishes and acts. Altering a source or a permission can change its next possible act; a result can become a condition of a subsequent one. These relations are real and investigable; their field extends beyond a model considered in isolation into the technical and social conditions of its use. Whether phenomenal Subjective Immediacy accompanies a particular artificial process remains a separate question. Functional richness does not settle it, and unfamiliar material does not settle it in the opposite direction. The positive constitution of mediation does not wait for that phenomenality question to be closed.

To organise an agent’s world is to participate in the formation of its possible judgments and acts. A retained record, an available tool, and an authorised course have different offices; their accumulation does not ensure their proper relation. An objection which merely appears in a log may leave the next act unchanged. An objection which revises the relevant permission or judgment has altered the operative world. The inquiry into constituted mediation therefore reaches the technical field through an actual problem: how can a received correction become effective in the means by which action proceeds?

The call that opened this differentiation has become a promise, and the promise a test of the field that sustains it. Voice, memory, alternatives, another person’s reliance, and the possibility of refusal participate in what the undertaking is; none is the sole source of the whole relation. Recognition restores that dependence to the act while preserving its power to determine. What has not become a positive item can still change the operation: an unanswered call, an unavailable hour, a course left open. An absence can have a definite place and consequence. What changes when such an absence acquires a sign that another person can use? In a numeral such as 205, how can the sign of an empty position alter the value of what is present?
