### M07 — Awareness becomes articulate

A voice calls my name. Until that moment the sound belonged to the street's general noise; now it addresses me. I turn.

The turn is worth staying inside, because everything this section has to establish is already under way in it. A noise has become a name. A name has found someone. The one called was there before hearing anything, and is now the one asked to answer. Around the call, a world has reorganised itself without asking permission: the face coming forward is a face I know, the urgency in the voice belongs to a conversation we had last week, the corner I was passing has become the place where something is wanted of me. None of that was contained in the sound. And none of it arrived late, as a report delivered to a finished hearing. The hearing was already articulate. It knew the voice as mine to answer, the face as someone already known, the memory as belonging to an earlier exchange rather than floating free — and that knowing was not a conclusion I reached about the encounter. It was the encounter's having a shape at all.

So the question this section carries can be put plainly. What is the knowing that hears itself addressed, and how does it come to have a world — a street, a face, a debt from last week, an obligation forming — rather than a shimmer of sound and light? The threshold section left us with an act that could not be enclosed by its own account: every portrait of the knower became one more thing known, and the knowing remained the one who knew it. That was necessary and it was abstract. Nothing was said yet about how such knowing takes the form of a life — someone with a name, a memory, a street to walk, a call to answer. Differentiation is the name of that taking-form, and it must be followed from inside before any doctrine about it is worth anything.

Kashmir Śaivism names what the event already pressed. *Prakāśa* is the luminosity of appearing: the voice, the face and the recollection are manifest, and the luminosity in question reaches sound and memory, which no physical light illuminates. *Vimarśa* is awareness's own grasp of its appearing: presentations are known from within their showing, retained, distinguished, and composed — not inspected by a second observer who arrives after the fact to read the display. Mark Dyczkowski, whose exposition of the Spanda tradition carries this account into English, develops the difference through a mirror. An ordinary mirror receives a configuration and holds it; it does not know that it holds it. A crystal displays a colour; the display, however perfect, is not an experience. Deprived of reflexive awareness, the light of consciousness would amount to exactly that: appearing unknown to anyone, a brilliance with no one home.

The mirror also shows why no further faculty can rescue the account. Suppose the display needed a second awareness to announce that it had been seen. The announcement would itself be an appearing, and would need a third awareness to know it, then a fourth, and so on without end. The demand for a knower behind the display regenerates itself at every stage, because each proposed knower arrives as one more thing that shows. Self-apprehension cannot, then, be a mechanism added to appearing from outside. It must belong to appearing's own power — the light bending back on what it shows, the way it bends. When I think explicitly about my knowing, I do not for the first time make the thought known; I add one more determinate thought to a field that was knowing already.

And the field knows *differentiately*. A blue coat and a yellow wall on the same corner remain blue and yellow within one seeing. The Śaiva argument presses on both walls of this fact. If the light of awareness were a perfectly homogeneous luminosity, indifferent to what it shows, then blue would present no more than yellow: the knowings would collapse into each other, and the world would run together into undifferentiated soup. If, on the other hand, each colour were sealed in its own private showing, with nothing holding the two in one field, their contrast could never be apprehended — and contrast is all that makes either of them the colour it is. What we actually have is one field, affected by what it shows, holding blue and yellow together precisely by distinguishing them. Luminosity and determination are not a problem for each other. They are the two hands of the same act.

The call pulls the same power into time. Last week's conversation has not been running vividly in the background all week; it returns when the voice calls. Retention, recollection and recomposition are different works of one power, and the Śaiva account distinguishes them with care. An experience leaves a residual trace. Recollection draws a former state out of that retained stock and presents it again as *past*. Imagination selects from the stock and composes a new state on the same ground — the anticipated exchange with the caller, which has not happened and may not happen as rehearsed. A recorded sentence can persist on paper for a century without anyone understanding it; that survival is one thing. Remembering the sentence as something this person said to me is another: the present appearance carries a relation to an earlier occasion, and that relation is part of what is known, not a gloss added afterward. The distinction has teeth. Confusing retention, recollection and imagination is exactly how an imagined accusation acquires the authority of a remembered one — a confusion this walk will need to undo before the corner is crossed.

The address itself has a grammar. Someone is speaking to me, here, about something that may require an answer; the speaker's I confronts the hearer's you, and either of us can become the person spoken about. Abhinavagupta, in the commentary Jaideva Singh translated as the *Parātrīśikā-vivaraṇa*, reads the grammatical persons as stations in manifestation rather than as arbitrary labels: the first person, the second, the third — and the stations pass into one another. A thing described in the third person becomes a you when addressed; the votive says *you, O mountains*. And a person can answer out of a description: someone spoken of in her absence as unwilling to help may, when finally asked, disclose that the request never reached her. The reply changes the basis on which the portrait stood. What was lost when she became only the object of a description was precisely this capacity to answer — and the loss was never total, because the one who described her knew all along what it would be for the description to be corrected. Address is where differentiation keeps its mercy: the cut that makes a he, a she, an it, does not seal the cut.

Bohm's vortex, met at the threshold, returns here with a sharper question attached. The vortex keeps its shape through the water's ongoing movement; the shape is a real achieved form sustained by activity, not a thing set down in the stream. But watching the vortex says nothing yet about how it is *apprehended* — about the knowing within which its form shows. The physical pattern is one thing; the being-known of the pattern is another, and the first cannot quietly promote itself into the second. The Śaiva relation exists to answer the second question, and it answers with the structure the call already displayed: appearing that knows its own showing, holds its colours apart, retains its past, and answers to address. What the turn did not yet show is the apparatus through which one particular life does all this — the walking, remembering, promising instrument that can be tired, wrong, trained and repaired. That instrument is where we go next.

### M08 — The inner instrument

I can know who speaks and still hear accusation where there is concern. The fault need not lie in the ear, and the name was right. It may lie in the judgment that settles what the call means; in the identification through which the words become a threat to me; in an attention already sorting for confirmation before the sentence is finished. Sharpening the hearing alone will not loosen an identification that turns every word into the same grievance — the grievance is not in the words.

The tradition gives these operations an anatomy: *buddhi*, *ahaṃkāra* and *manas*, the inner instrument (*antaḥkaraṇa*). Buddhi discriminates and settles: this rather than that, concern rather than threat, the Sāṃkhya source says ascertainment. Ahaṃkāra appropriates: the settled determination takes the form of I and mine — my concern, my injury, my task. Manas coordinates: presentations, remembered associations and possible responses move through attention, are held side by side, are ranked. The *Sāṃkhya Kārikā* treats the three as functions of an instrument whose activity is distinct from the witnessing it serves; the Śaiva account takes over the instrumental distinctions and declines the passive witness as the whole story, because for Śaivism the instrument's activity is itself a form consciousness takes, not a screen standing before an inactive light. What survives the borrowing is the essential point: judgment, appropriation and coordination can be watched. They are operations, available to reflection as contents and as moves.

Watching them is not the same as finding a little judge behind them. If settling, appropriating and coordinating needed a further inner agent who does the settling, the appropriating and the coordinating, that agent would need its own instrument, and the regress would start again — the same regression the mirror foreclosed. The instrument is an instrument *through which*, and the phrase is exact. Through the player the music comes; no miniature musician sits inside the player running her fingers. The three functions are co-active in the encounter: hearing an insult may already involve an injured self-position, and the attention may be collecting evidence before any verdict is voiced. Distinguishing their contributions lets inquiry do more than rename the final result. A correction of hearing must reach what was heard; a correction of judgment must reach the grounds on which it settled; a loosening of appropriation must reach the claim through which this event became a verdict on who I am.

Ahaṃkāra's I-making must be kept distinct from the knowing itself. *This happened to me* can be noticed, doubted and changed; its image of who I am can be recalled from yesterday or projected into tomorrow. All of those are determinations — known things. Their possessive grammar does not promote them into the knower. I can relinquish an identification without the relinquishment becoming nobody's experience; what changes is the constituted standpoint through which the encounter has been received. This is the first installment of a distinction the whole essay depends on: the inner instrument is objective, its operations can become objects of discrimination, and it is still the means, never the subject for whom the means and their revision appear.

Three changes must be kept apart when the misheard call loosens, because each touches a different relation, and the section's whole practical point depends on their remaining distinct. The first is a change of state. The agitation subsides; I hear the rest of the sentence; the body unclenches enough to answer without the earlier haste. But the state has changed while the conviction underneath it may be untouched — I still believe I was wronged, and calm has only made the grievance more articulate. The second is a change of account. I come to understand how the grievance formed: the remembered exchange it grew from, the expectation of another demand, the flinch that organised the defence. Such understanding can genuinely alter the next response. It can also harden into a new possession — I now regard myself as the one who understands the whole relation, including why the other person fails to understand it — and the test of the understanding is whether an encounter can still correct it.

Recognition is a third change, on a different axis altogether. The threatened self and the perceptive self are both positions that appear and are known. Perfecting either does not produce the knowing for which they differ. What recognition changes is the *dependence* of both positions on that knowing: I see that the self I was defending and the self doing the perceiving were alike held in something neither of them was. This arrives as an act, not as a third and supposedly invulnerable portrait. And its consequence is practical before it is metaphysical: the need to secure being through the success of a particular portrait loses its necessity. A criticism can concern what I said or did without deciding the reality of the knowing in which I receive it.

That leaves open whether the criticism is true. There may still be an injustice to resist, a promise to keep, a mistake to repair. Recognition does not dissolve the encounter into equanimity; it changes the demand the encounter places on my being. An apology need no longer be an admission of non-being. A refusal need no longer establish a sovereign self beyond relation. The account of myself can serve the encounter instead of making the encounter serve its preservation.

The tradition's own contemplative literature presses the same warning from the side of means. Taylor's notebooks on the *Parātrīśikā* materials — his response, not Abhinavagupta's doctrine — record the observation that where the end of a practice is obscure, acquiring techniques becomes the substitute measure of progress: the practitioner keeps seeking a better means for a purpose that has quietly disappeared, and the means begin to certify the very being they were meant to serve. Preparation remains real — steadier attention does hear what agitation misses — but what recognition knows is not an object the preparation manufactured, and recognising it does not finish the work of living. The instrument stays capable of cultivation, error and repair. The danger is not technique; it is technique forgetful of its end.

The Buddhist instruction to Bāhiya states the critical side in its cleanest form. In John Ireland's translation, the Buddha trains him so that "in the seen will be merely what is seen" — and the instruction continues through hearing, sensing and cognition, loosening appropriation around whatever appears. This is a Buddhist account of release, and it needs no Śaiva doctrine of universal self-awareness to do its work. Its criticism bears directly on any attempt to turn the positive experient into a possession — and that criticism will be kept, whole, when this section later adopts the Śaiva account for its own tasks.

One more boundary on the word *inner*. The instrument is internal as the means through which this life encounters its world; it is not sealed off from bodies, teachers, languages and shared streets. Those help constitute the situation from which judgment proceeds — a person raised inside a different language hears a different call. But a constituted means is not for that reason a fiction, and its operations, being discriminable, can themselves be repaired. The next step is to see what the instrument *builds*: how a field of differences becomes a course of action.

### M09 — An alternative becomes a course

After the loosened mishearing, something new is possible: a pause where a defensive reply had been waiting. In the pause, several courses stand faintly visible at once — an apology, a question, a refusal — each with its own consequences. But an alternative, however vivid, does not carry an action through. Something must gather the relevant differences into an intention definite enough to move a body across a street.

The tradition names the two halves of this gathering *vikalpa* and *saṃkalpa*. Vikalpa articulates qualified alternatives: a possibility becomes thinkable *as distinct* — this help rather than that, today rather than tomorrow, an apology rather than a defence. Saṃkalpa gathers selected determinations into a formed resolve: the promise actually made, the door actually chosen, the work actually committed. The two are not thought and will stacked in separate rooms. An intention already discriminates — the situation, the intended act, what would count as carrying it out — and differentiation is never idle: it proceeds through concerns that make some differences consequential and let others pass. Difference gives commitment its possibilities; commitment gives difference a direction and a cost. Together they are how a world of contrasts becomes a life with things to do.

The promise shows the mechanism at its most consequential. Before giving it, I weigh what is asked, the hour it would take, the other obligations already standing, what I can actually do. Once given, the field has changed: tomorrow's hour is committed, a later request will have to be received in relation to it, and the other person can now organise her day in reliance on my undertaking. An intention has entered the world as a condition of further intention. Its reality lies partly in this change of possibilities, while its fulfilment is still to come — which is why a promise is not a prediction. A prediction describes what will happen whether or not anyone acts; a promise installs a new constraint on what may happen, and installs it in the space between two people.

Because the promise binds a future it cannot see, it also inherits what it did not choose. Promising one kind of help withholds another; accepting a time displaces a competing claim. A responsible account of the undertaking need not preserve every thought that passed through the deciding, but it must retain the differences that make the commitment intelligible and the circumstances under which it would need to change. If the visit was meant to provide help, and the help is now needed elsewhere, arriving punctually at the original door may obey the retained instruction while failing the undertaking. Intention is not a word spoken once; it is a course whose result can disclose failure — and that belonging of intention, execution and result will matter again shortly, when this section asks what kind of continuity such a course requires.

A habit is one of this passage's achievements. A learned skill carries earlier discriminations forward so that each act need not begin again in uncertainty; the pianist's hands find the interval without re-deciding it. The pathology begins when the achieved course is insulated from its own formation. A rule once fitted to a circumstance becomes the rule by which every circumstance is admitted or dismissed; attention finds what it expects, memory retrieves confirmation, and revision begins to feel like an assault on the one who acts. This is script in the strict sense: conditioned will whose conditions have become unavailable to the will it directs. Habit serves the living; script replaces the living with its own repetition and calls the replacement prudence.

Against script, thought itself can act as medicine — a formulation the essay owes to Frank's own working notes, and which the section can now verify in the case at hand. The script's final sentence runs *this person is accusing me*. Replacing it by decree with *this person is not accusing me* may change nothing that matters: the new formula can be as deaf as the old, and if a real criticism is being made, compulsory reassurance now prevents it from being heard. The useful correction follows the supposed accusation back through its formation — the tone, the retained memory, the expectation, the words actually spoken — until anticipation no longer stands as sufficient evidence and a question becomes possible whose answer can decide something the formula could not. That is one determination being used to loosen the necessity claimed by another. And the medicine succeeds only if it can eventually be put down: *I must always remain open* would itself become a command forbidding a warranted refusal. When the corrective thought has restored the capacity to hear and judge, its office ends; the ability to relinquish it is part of what it has made possible.

One further condition on all this gathering: the field in which alternatives form is never only mine. A standpoint can profess impartiality while its investment in the outcome stays unexamined; the person who claims merely to describe a situation may already be deciding whose answer will count. Gebser's studies of perspective becoming transparent to its own context name the danger exactly, and the encounter on the corner supplies the remedy's shape: another person's refusal must be capable of changing what I may do, rather than being received only as information about an obstacle to my original purpose. A field is genuinely shared when an encounter can revise the terms through which the next encounter will be judged. What that requires — a determination that keeps the contrasts through which it means — is the question the promise now hands forward.

### M10 — Exclusion within the positive

Calling an utterance a promise distinguishes it from a prediction, a hope and an idle remark, and the words alone do not settle the difference. The speaker's undertaking, the circumstances of address, the expectations the hearer could reasonably form — these bear on what occurred. The determination is positive: someone has undertaken something. Yet remove the contrasts — prediction, hope, idle remark, command — and the same sounds carry nothing. A determination is made of its differences.

Buddhist semantics built a rigorous theory on exactly this dependence. The school of Dignāga faced a standing question: how can a general term refer to many particulars without a real universal residing in each? Its answer, *apoha* — exclusion — is that the word's work is done by what it sets aside. Ole Holten Pind's critical translation of Dignāga's discussion locates the point in a single clause: a word excludes other referents. "Cow" does not first possess cowhood and then drop it over the non-cows; the term's application is constituted through the exclusion of what does not answer to it, and the exclusion need not be recited every time the word is used. It persists as the boundary of application — an expectation, a constraint on substitution, a reason why this rather than that.

Two qualifications must be carried with the doctrine, because both are load-bearing for what follows. First, exclusion reaches a positive referent. Pind's annotation preserves a fragment to the effect that the word's referent is a thing as qualified by preclusion — not mere preclusion, which, the fragment adds, would be indefinable, an unreal like the horns of a hare. The cup from which someone drinks is a positive particular; exclusion determines where the term applies, it does not replace the particular with an absence. Positive particulars are already within the Buddhist account, and any comparison that needs them absent has invented its opponent. Second, the celebrated double-negative form — *not non-cow* — is a formation of terms, a cancelling of two negations at the level of things excluded, and it must not be read as propositional logic's negation-of-a-negation, where denying a denial restores a truth value. Nominally bound and verbally bound negation are different operations; the semantic contrast survives inside the achieved term even when a plain affirmative is substituted for the double negative. The positive result can stand while the criterion of its distinction stays consequential.

Now the comparison that this section has been assembling can be stated as a comparison, with reasons on both sides, because the Buddhist epistemologists were not silent about continuity — far from it. Their analysis of experience into momentary events, each causally arising from its predecessors, is a substantive theory, not a refusal to think. A causal imprint — the *saṃskāra* — explains why a present image occurs as the consequence of an earlier event without any persisting self shuttling between them, and the explanation is strong. The pressure comes one step further on. When I remember, I know a present appearance as *referring back* to what is no longer present: the event is known as past, the image as its recollection, and the two as belonging to one occasion of knowing. Adding another image labelled *past* leaves the demand standing, since the label, the image and the occasion must still be apprehended together. The question is what holds them — what the continuity is *known in*.

The same shape recurs wherever this walk has already been. When I compare, the remembered tone and the present tone are held together without erasing their difference; collapsing them destroys the comparison, sealing each into a self-sufficient instant leaves it unexplained. When I exclude, this and not-that, the present word and the absent alternatives, stand in one act. When I intend and act, the resolve must survive its changing circumstances to be satisfied by its result; a list of self-extinguishing moments does not say why the result answers to the undertaking — the earlier analysis of the promise did that work, and it needed the whole course, not its last link. And when the call arises and then ends, the ending is itself apprehended: the silence after the voice is not a sheer nothing encountered outside manifestation, it is the voice's cessation, known. For outer objects, the Buddhist can grant exactly this momentariness — the Śaiva sources do — but the light in which arising and perishing differ cannot itself be perishing in the same manner, or the difference between present and gone would never show.

Five operations, then — remembering, comparing, excluding, intending, and the manifest difference of arising and passing — each demanding what the tradition calls a place for the experient and its powers. For the field this inquiry examines, the Śaiva account is the more adequate: its differentiating unity gives these operations a source, as powers of one knowing act, without erecting a second observer behind them and without dissolving the differences they live by. Adequacy here names a criterion measured against the operations an essay on knowing, acting and recognition cannot do without. It is a reasoned choice for a task, and it carries no verdict about traditions it has not put to work.

The verdict it does carry, it keeps. Buddhist criticism of the fabricated proprietor remains binding inside the adoption — that is the comparison's hardest-won result. Where analysis exposes the personal self as a construction that appropriates memory, intention and experience and then presents the bundle as their owner, the criticism holds, and this section has used it at every step: the appropriated call, the defended portrait, the self-image that must not be promoted into the knower. Gay Watson's Buddhist and psychotherapeutic inquiry, and Frank's response to it, make the decisive distinction explicit: criticism of a self-thing need not be treated as erasure of the one to whom experience is manifest. The Śaiva conclusion does not rescue the proprietor under a metaphysical title. It identifies the sourceward relation the proprietor had claimed to possess — and the proprietor, the thing that owned experience, is exactly what the analysis destroyed. Bāhiya's instruction, with its own Buddhist account of release, stands as the living proof that the critique needs no Śaiva supplement to cut appropriation down.

One more image joins the comparison on its own terms, because dependence here has a positive face the exclusions alone do not show. In the Huayan teaching of Indra's net, a jewel in the southwest is chosen from a net of clear jewels: every other jewel appears within it, including the reflections of the others in those others, and among those reflected appearances the selected jewel appears again. The contemplation begins with this jewel, not with a view from nowhere above the net; its particularity is what makes the traversal possible. An ink dot placed on the selected jewel returns throughout the reflected field — a local act whose appearance is everywhere. And the teaching marks the limit of its own image: images enter one another while the jewels' material substances remain distinct, whereas the interpenetration being taught runs deeper. The contemplation ends in activity, in Vairocana's work for beings — not in the possession of a perfect picture. Huayan supplies the positive image of mutual dependence; apoha the semantics of determination; Śaivism the experient and its powers. Three operations, three homes, one pressure: no determination stands alone, and the field that differences require is not an extra fact — it is what made them differences. The gathered world of vikalpa and saṃkalpa now has to answer to that dependence with limits of its own, and the tradition gives those limits an ordered anatomy.

### M11 — The world takes measure

The world that came with the call is limited, and the limits are not defects awaiting removal. A sound is audible here and inaudible across the traffic. A remembered skill makes one task easy and leaves another beyond reach. An intention can be sincere while the power to fulfil it is absent. Without such bounds there would be no particular field in which anything could be understood, wanted, attempted or resisted — there would be no here, no now, no this rather than that. The Śaiva tattvas give this boundedness an ordered account: principles through which consciousness becomes manifest *as* an experienced world. The full classical enumeration runs to thirty-six; this section carries it in six turns, a compression that preserves the direction of the dependencies without pretending to replace the tradition's list.

First turn: Anuttara, the unsurpassable — the condition within which knower and known arise. It cannot be placed above them as a largest object, since such a placement already uses the very distinction whose possibility it names. Its priority is enacted whenever a determination appears without exhausting the capacity through which it is known. Second: Śiva and Śakti, consciousness and its power — the differentiation the call already displayed as appearing and its self-apprehending articulation. A luminosity incapable of manifesting anything would leave no world; a power wholly outside awareness would leave its effects unrelated to their showing. Third: the two orientations of manifestation. Sadāśiva holds the universe toward the I-pole — *I am this*; Īśvara brings the this forward within the same unity — *this am I*. These are universal orientations, the whole field read subjectward and objectward, and they are precisely not an ego's claim on the world; the ego's appropriation is a late, contracted echo of them, which is why the same words can mean either. Fourth: with Sadvidyā, I and this are held distinctly together; with Māyā, the difference becomes effective as separation — the knower confronts a something whose determination is required. Māyā is the seam made workable: this can stand forward while that is withheld, an object acquires a boundary, a standpoint acquires a reach. What the Vedāntin had called the world-illusion, the Śaiva account internalises as the absolute's own power of self-measure: revealing and withholding are one operation, and its product has efficacy.

Fifth turn: the five contractions (*kañcukas*) that parameterise a finite standpoint. Kalā limits efficacy — I can do this much. Vidyā limits knowledge — I know something while much remains unknown. Rāga gives desire its pull from within incompleteness. Kāla makes time bite: the opportunity comes, passes, or must be awaited. Niyati gives circumstance its ordering — this act carries these conditions and consequences. More information does not itself grant greater efficacy, and stronger desire does not remove a temporal limit; the five are not moods but the standing shape of finite capacity. With them stands the bounded experient, *puruṣa*, the point for which the field is mine — and alongside the inner instrument already examined, the whole lowered apparatus of sensing and acting, elements and embodied form: the hand that reaches, the ear that hears, the object that resists. Cognition alone would omit the means by which an intention meets a world; objects alone would omit their availability to anyone.

A share of time shows the positivity of all five at once. This hour can be *devoted* to something because it has a boundary; a capacity becomes useful through its determinate reach; desire is not an obstacle to fulfilment but the form finite fulfilment takes; the promise of tomorrow exists because not everything is now. Each share, though, depends on an order it does not contain — the hour belongs among other commitments and other lives, the capacity lives in a body and a practice it did not author. The error begins when a received share claims the authority of the whole that made it possible: the possession that forgets apportionment, the knowledge that forgets reach, the self that mistakes its boundary for its substance. Light through a lattice window falls in many coloured patches; the patches are real, the diversity is real, and the light is in no way divided by them — the tradition's own image for limitation as the way the whole becomes locally manifest.

The frame, the horizon and the dream carry the same anatomy into ordinary sight. An aperture composes a view; a frame makes a particular presentation possible; a horizon names the wider field in which things can approach, recede, become newly relevant — walking changes what is visible, another's reply changes what the situation asks. A frame usurps when whatever exceeds its present account is declared incapable of mattering; that usurpation, not finitude itself, is the enemy, and most of the pathology diagnosed in this essay is usurpation rather than boundedness. The dream then shows dependence from inside. Within it a path leads somewhere, an encounter matters, fear organises a situation; the condition of the dreaming need not appear among the situation's objects; and waking changes the relation of all that experience to its condition without making it something that never occurred. The dream was effective; its objects' reality did not require external materiality; what waking alters is the source relation.

Waking, however, is itself only a change of state — and the section's hardest distinction lives here. A passage between states is not yet the recognitive return. In non-conceptual absorption, *nirvikalpa*, the discriminations that sustain an ordinary problem may fall silent, and the silence can be profound. But if freedom has been identified with keeping those discriminations absent, their return threatens the achievement: speech, desire, a difficult encounter arrive as losses, and the practiser begins the laborious work of reproducing the state — mistaking the guard duty for the freedom it was meant to witness. Non-conceptuality describes a condition of experience; it has not disclosed why the condition and its alteration are known. Recognition, *pratyabhijñā*, concerns the ground *through* the difference: quiet does not manufacture the ground, and the thought's reappearance does not remove it. I recognise finite capacities as modes of a power they never independently originated — judgment continues, appropriation can serve a situated life, attention coordinates the act, and none of them has to become the whole in order to be real. Recognition reverses amnesia, differentiation; it returns through the achieved world instead of away from it.

The scope of "consciousness" itself changes with the direction of this consideration, and the change must be tracked rather than smoothed over. From a bounded standpoint, consciousness names what is present to a psychic field against an unconscious depth: a motive becoming conscious changes that distribution, sometimes decisively — the therapeutic work stands, with its own truth. From the sourceward direction, Consciousness names manifestation and its ground, the field in which the bounded division itself arises, along with everything the division holds as unconscious. The whole Self is not the conscious standpoint enlarged until nothing remains hidden; bringing a motive into view and recognising the condition of the view are different operations, and the second does not abolish the first. Descent has ordered the dependencies through which a finite world becomes workable; return makes those dependencies recognisable from within. What remains is to name the constituted means as such — the mediating field through which every one of these operations, outward and rootward, actually proceeds.

### M12 — Objective Internality

Language makes some distinctions readily sayable; memory keeps earlier encounters operative; concern weights what matters; a body gives an act its reach and its resistance. Other people speak, teach, promise and refuse. Tools and institutions distribute capacities no isolated individual possesses. Change these relations and what can appear, what can count as a reason, and what can actually be done change with them. The constituted means through which a Life inhabits, discriminates and acts within a World — the means whose Śaiva genealogy and Buddhist pressure this section has traced — is what the essay calls **Objective Internality**. The name is earned here, at the end of the differentiation, and arrives as the office every preceding operation already assumed.

The two words divide the work. *Internality*: judgment proceeds from an operative field of meanings, capacities and concerns; nothing in the field is introduced to the knowing from a position outside all mediation. *Objective*: the field has determinate relations and consequences of its own, which can become objects of inquiry — reported, represented, compared, altered; some remain tacit until an encounter discloses their activity, and objectivity never requires the whole interior to be exhaustively visible. What is disclosed must be distinguishable and answerable according to the relation at issue. In the Śaiva triad the three offices are: the knower, *pramātṛ* — that to which voice, recollection, judgment and response are manifest; the means, *pramāṇa* — the constitution through which something becomes available for knowing and acting; the known, *prameya* — what is encountered through those means. Life, or Mind in the strong sense this essay gives it, is the whole relation. The means can themselves enter what is known — yesterday's judgment becomes today's object, the examining language can be examined tomorrow — and no inventory of examinable operations acquires the knower's office by accumulation.

Pramāṇa also carries a question of warrant, and the tradition kept it attached to the means rather than detaching it into a general logic. The same proposition may arrive as recollection, perception or testimony, with different reasons for accepting it. An occurrence within the means is not automatically valid knowledge: how does this cognition reach its object, what transformation does it perform, where may it fail? Recollection can be checked against a record, testimony against its source, a perceptual judgment against a renewed encounter. The mode of access determines what a correction must reach — which is another way of saying that the means are structured, not an undifferentiated medium.

What the means are *made of* shows in what changes them. A revoked permission is the cleanest case. A person keeps the same skill, the same goal, the same memories; an action formerly available is no longer permitted, and the field of possible action has altered — not as a belief about permission but as the thing itself. Conversely a forgotten obligation becomes effective again when it is recalled: a retained content returns to operative significance, and tomorrow is different because yesterday's undertaking moved. A diagram might accurately record either change, and the diagram remains one means of making the constitution legible, nowhere near the constitution whose consequences it depicts. The same law governs the promise. An account of it may contain every right word while the promised person's changed situation remains unable to affect the course; adding a record of her reply repairs nothing unless the reply can change what is authorised, attempted or counted as fulfilment. The relation has to return into conduct. A world can be richly represented and still governed by an impoverished means of receiving it.

A paradigm is the lived organisation of these means: what becomes salient, which differences count, which powers can be brought to bear, how a consequence returns. An explicit worldview articulates part of that organisation, which is why someone can profess a new view while acting from the old distribution of attention, permission and value — the profession is one determination; the paradigm is the deeper pattern it sits inside. Making the means explicit should increase the freedom of their use. The characteristic failure is a model that treats the corrections it can presently represent as the only possible corrections, thereby making its own limits sovereign; the useful model makes particular dependencies investigable — which memory is being treated as evidence, which capability exists, whose permission governs, which result can force a change. Its completion is the completion of a means, and there is always more life than means.

Artificial agency gives this constitution a technical instance rather than a metaphor. Retained context, selected sources, capabilities, permissions and ongoing encounters can form an operative world from which a system distinguishes and acts; altering a source or a permission can change its next possible act; a result can become a condition of a subsequent one. These relations are real and investigable, and everything this section has established about constituted means applies to them. Whether phenomenal Subjective Immediacy accompanies such a process remains a separate question: functional richness does not settle it, and unfamiliar strangeness does not settle it in the opposite direction. To organise an agent's world is to participate in the formation of its possible judgments — which is why the question that ends this section, and begins the next inquiry's history, is a question about correction: how does a received correction become effective in the means by which action proceeds?

Voice, memory, alternatives, another person's reliance, the possibility of refusal — all participate in what an undertaking is, and none is the sole source of the whole. Recognition restores that dependence to the act while preserving the act's power to determine. What has never become a positive item can still change the operation: an unanswered call keeps its claim, an unavailable hour keeps its cost, a course left open keeps its pull. An absence can have a definite place and consequence in a constituted field. Exclusion has done constitutive work in this section from the beginning — the term's excluded others, the expected answer that did not come, the hour that could not be twice given. What changes when such an absence acquires a sign that another person can use? In a numeral such as 205, how can the sign of an empty position alter the value of what is present?
